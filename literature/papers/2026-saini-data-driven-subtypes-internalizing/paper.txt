A B S T R A C T
Background: The mechanisms underlying the high comorbidity between internalizing disorders (IDs) and func­
tional disorders (FDs) remain unclear. This study aimed to identify data-driven subgroups of major depressive 
disorder (MDD), generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/ 
CFS), fibromyalgia (FM), and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) symptoms in the general population, capturing 
shared symptom patterns while accounting for variation in symptom severity.
Method: We analyzed cross-sectional data from 72,919 adults in the Dutch Lifelines Cohort Study, randomly 
divided into training and validation subsets. Twenty-seven ID and FD criteria symptoms were examined using 
mixed-measurement item response theory models in the training set, with the optimal model validated in the 
validation set. Class characteristics were assessed through associations with risk factors, ID/FD diagnosis and 
comorbidity patterns.
Results: Six classes best described the data: Healthy (57.5%), Pain (13.8%), Tension/Pain (9.1%), Anxiety (9.0%), 
Cognition/Fatigue (6.4%), and Depression (4.3%). All classes showed a combination of ID and FD symptoms, 
with the Cognition/Fatigue and Depression classes being most mixed, and the Pain and Anxiety classes most 
domain-specific. Transdiagnostic symptoms were highly endorsed across all classes. ID–FD comorbidity was 
highest in the Cognition/Fatigue and Depression classes, with the Depression class showing the greatest func­
tional impairment.
Conclusions: The identified classes comprised mixed symptoms from multiple disorders, rather than disorder- 
specific profiles. ID-FD comorbidity may partly result from non-specific defining symptoms lowering the 
threshold for fulfilling criteria for multiple disorders. Symptom-level evaluation can help identify more ho­
mogenous and clinically relevant subgroups. Longitudinal research is needed to clarify underlying mechanisms.
1. Introduction
Internalizing disorders (IDs) are characterized by emotional and 
behavioral responses directed inward rather than toward others [1]. The 
two most common IDs, major depressive disorder (MDD) and general­
ized anxiety disorder (GAD), frequently co-occur, resulting in greater 
social and occupational impairment [2,3]. Functional disorders (FDs) 
involve persistent physical symptoms not attributable to known objec­
tifiable 
pathological 
abnormalities 
[4], 
such 
as 
myalgic 
encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), fibromyalgia 
(FM), and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). IDs and FDs are highly co­
morbid [5], and their co-occurrence is associated with reduced quality 
of life [6]. The mechanisms underlying this comorbidity remain unclear, 
although genetic, biological, and symptom overlap explanations have 
been proposed [7–12].
The heterogenous nature of these disorders may contribute to the 
challenges in uncovering mechanisms underlying ID-FD comorbidity 
[13–15]. Categorical diagnoses may obscure important variation, as

tigation of ID and FD symptoms to identify more homogenous subgroups 
might help reveal shared pathways underlying co-occurrence and 
disorder-specific mechanisms, including genetic or environmental risk 
factors. Previous studies using latent class models (LCMs) within IDs or 
FDs have identified cross-diagnostic subgroups with distinct psycho­
logical and somatic characteristics, risk factors, and treatment responses 
[16–20], and symptom profiles that were mixed rather than “pure”. 
Extending this approach across ID and FD symptoms may help uncover 
cross-disorder symptom patterns that improve understanding of their 
comorbidity.
Traditional LCMs impose categorical distinctions that overlook 
within- and between-class severity variation and the dimensional nature 
of psychopathology. When the underlying structure is dimensional, 
increasing the number of classes may approximate severity differences, 
producing subgroups that do not differ qualitatively and limit inter­
pretability. Symptoms are also often interconnected, potentially 
violating the conditional independence assumption [21]. A previous 
factor analysis study showed that ID and FD symptoms naturally cluster 
along continuous, overlapping dimensions rather than discrete cate­
gories [10]. Hybrid mixture models, such as mixed-measurement item 
response theory (MM-IRT), address these limitations by combining 
categorical classes with dimensional latent traits, identifying subgroups 
that differ in both symptom patterns and underlying severity. Several 
studies show that hybrid mixture models outperform traditional LCMs in 
identifying more meaningful and clinically informative subgroups, 
demonstrating improved model fit with fewer classes and avoiding so­
lutions that merely reflect severity gradients [22–24]. Understanding 
patterns of co-occurring ID and FD symptoms may support more inte­
grated care strategies for patients who do not fit single-diagnosis 
frameworks. Additionally, empirically derived subtypes may identify 
more homogenous groups with specific prognosis and treatment 
response than current classifications.
Existing hybrid model studies have primarily focused on mood and 
anxiety disorders, with none applying these models to understand ID-FD 
comorbidity. In this explorative study, we aimed to identify data-driven 
subgroups based on ID and FD symptom patterns in the general popu­
lation. As a secondary aim, we characterized these subgroups by 
examining associations with disability and external variables to describe 
class differences in clinically relevant characteristics [25–27]. We also 
examined the distribution of ID and FD cases and their comorbidity 
patterns across subgroups to assess how established diagnoses relate to 
empirically derived symptom profiles.
2. Methods
2.1. Participants
Lifelines is a multidisciplinary prospective population-based cohort 
study examining the health and health-related behaviors of 167,729 
persons living in the North of the Netherlands in a unique three- 
generation design [28]. It employs a broad range of investigative pro­
cedures in assessing the biomedical, socio-demographic, behavioral, 
physical and psychological factors that contribute to the health and 
disease of the general population, with a special focus on multi- 
morbidity and complex genetics. For a detailed description of Life­
lines' recruitment procedure, we refer to previous studies [28–30]. The 
Lifelines cohort study has been approved by the Medical Ethical Com­
mittee of the University Medical Center Groningen.
All participants provided written informed consent. We used data 
from 72,919 participants, ≥18 years old, collected during the second 
assessment wave of Lifelines, between 2014 and 2019. All included 
participants had available symptom data for both IDs and FDs.
2.2. Measures
2.2.1. Internalizing symptoms
The Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) was used 
to assess MDD and GAD symptoms as defined by the fourth edition of the 
Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV) 
(Table Supplement 1(S1)) [31]. MDD symptoms, assessed during the 
past two weeks, included depressed mood, anhedonia, appetite change, 
weight gain, weight loss, trouble sleeping, psychomotor retardation, 
psychomotor agitation, fatigue, worthlessness/guilt, concentration dif­
ficulty, and suicidal ideation. GAD symptoms, assessed during the past 
six months, included excessive worry/anxiety, restlessness, muscle 
tension, fatigue, concentration difficulty, irritability, and trouble 
sleeping. All items were self-reported as present (1) or absent (0).
2.2.2. Functional somatic symptoms
ME/CFS symptoms were assessed using the 1994 Centers for Disease 
Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria (Table S2) [32]. Symptoms, 
referring to the past six months, included fatigue, sore throat, tender 
lymph nodes, muscle pain, joint pain, headaches, unrefreshing sleep, 
post-exertional malaise (PEM), and impaired memory or concentration. 
Memory and concentration were combined into a single symptom 
(‘concentration difficulty’), as only one is required by the CDC criteria.
FM symptoms were measured using the 2010 American College of 
Rheumatology (ACR) criteria (Tables S3-S4) [33]. Symptoms included 
≥3 months of musculoskeletal pain and symptoms measured using the 
Widespread Pain Index (WPI) [33] and the Checklist Individual Strength 
(CIS) [34]. The WPI measured 19 body areas with pain during the past 
week and the CIS measured fatigue, difficulty thinking, concentration 
difficulty, and unrefreshing sleep in the past two weeks.
IBS symptoms were examined using the Rome III IBS Diagnostic 
Questionnaire [35], later adjusted to align with the Rome IV criteria 
[36]. Symptoms included recurrent abdominal pain/discomfort (≥1 
day/week for ≥6 months) and three conditional symptoms: related to 
defecation, change in stool frequency, and change in stool form, only 
assessed among participants reporting abdominal pain/discomfort. For 
the analysis, symptoms were combined into a single item, scored 1 if 
participants reported abdominal pain/discomfort and at least two con­
ditional symptoms, and 0 otherwise.
FD symptoms were assessed using self-reported questionnaires and 
dichotomized to reduce estimation complexity and minimize bias that 
may arise from unevenly distributed thresholds in low prevalence 
symptoms [37].
2.3. External variables
We examined the distribution of variables across the identified 
subgroups, selected based on prior evidence demonstrating links with 
both IDs and FDs [10]: (a) Sociodemographics: sex, age [5,38–41], and 
work status (currently employed, unemployed and searching for work, 
unemployed due to disability) [42,43]; (b) Lifestyle: body mass index 
(BMI) [39,40,44] and heavy drinking [27,45,46]; (c) Environmental 
adversity: chronic stress in the past year (e.g., work, home, relationship 
stress) [10,27,47], acute stress in the past year (e.g., severe injury, death 
of a loved one, divorce) [27,48,49], and childhood sexual abuse 
[25,50–52]; (d) Self-rated health [53,54]; and (e) Disability: number of 
days in the past 30 days during which participants were unable to work, 
bed bound, limited in work/activities, or less focused due to their 
physical or mental health [55–58]. The external variables are further 
detailed in the Supplementary Methods.
In addition, we examined the distribution of ID and FD diagnoses and 
their comorbidity across subgroups to explore how different symptom 
profiles may correspond to specific disorders or distinct comorbidity 
patterns.

2.4. Statistical methods
2.4.1. Symptom aggregation
Diagnostic criteria for IDs and FDs include overlapping symptoms, 
which may lead to spurious or difficult to interpret classes due to local 
dependence and indicator redundancy [59]. These transdiagnostic 
symptoms and their associated disorders were trouble sleeping (MDD, 
GAD), unrefreshing sleep (ME/CFS, FM), fatigue (MDD, GAD, ME/CFS, 
FM), and concentration difficulty (MDD, GAD, ME/CFS, FM). Following 
previous studies [10,11], these symptoms were combined to improve 
model stabilization and class separation. If a symptom was reported as 
present in at least one measure, the combined symptom was coded as 1 
(present). The 19 WPI pain location items were summed and dichoto­
mized using the cut-off defined in the FM criteria, as the number, not 
location, of pain sites is diagnostically relevant [33] (Supplementary 
Methods).
2.4.2. Missing data
Missing data on ID and FD symptoms (<0.3%) (Table S5) and 
external variables (<37.5%) were handled with the full information 
maximum likelihood method, using all available item response infor­
mation for each case to provide stable and unbiased model parameter 
estimates [60].
2.4.3. Models
As a preliminary step, we performed exploratory factor analysis 
(EFA) and latent class analysis (LCA) to explore the general structure of 
the 27 ID and FD symptoms and provide a baseline for comparison with 
the hybrid models. EFA was used to check the tenability of a general 
severity dimension across symptoms. LCA identifies mutually exclusive 
subgroups based on response patterns, assuming item responses are 
conditionally independent given class membership [61].
Subsequently, we estimated the MM-IRT models, combining features 
of LCA (i.e., identifying unobserved subpopulations with distinct 
response patterns) and unidimensional IRT (i.e., modeling a continuous 
latent factor to estimate individual differences in severity). Each item 
was parametrized by discrimination (capturing how strongly the item 
relates to the latent trait, i.e., severity), and difficulty (severity threshold 
for symptom endorsement) parameters [62,63]. MM-IRT captures both 
class-specific symptom patterns and individual severity differences and 
relaxes the conditional independence assumption of LCA by allowing 
within-class symptom associations [64].
2.4.4. Estimation procedure
Data were randomly split into a training set (n = 36,459) for fitting 
the EFA, LCA, and MM-IRT models and a validation set (n = 36,460) for 
validating the stability and generalizability of the final MM-IRT model. 
Models were estimated by running 50 random start values, up to 700 
iterations per set, 200 Expectation-Maximization iterations, and 80 
Newton-Raphson iterations to reduce convergence issues and local 
maxima. Model selection was based on interpretability and goodness-of- 
fit statistics [65], including the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) 
[66], sample size adjusted BIC (sBIC) [67], Akaike information criterion 
(AIC) [68], and consistent AIC (CAIC) [69]. Model classification quality 
was also assessed. The final MM-IRT model was estimated in the vali­
dation set without external variables, after which external variables 
were included as distal outcomes to estimate their class-specific distri­
butions. As standard approaches may underestimate associations due to 
classification error, a bias-adjusted three-step method was used [70–72]. 
EFA was performed in Mplus version 8.2 [73] and LCA and MM-IRT 
models were fitted in LatentGOLD 6.0 [74].
3. Results
3.1. Sample description
Of the 72,919 participants included, 59.2% were female and the 
mean age was 49.1 years (SD = 12.4) (Table 1). No significant differ­
ences were observed between the training and validation set (Table S6). 
Overall, 15.4% of participants met diagnostic criteria for at least one 
disorder. Point prevalence was highest for FM and GAD, followed by IBS, 
and lowest for MDD and ME/CFS.
3.2. LCA and MM-IRT models
LCA models improved in fit as the number of classes increased 
(Table 2), primarily reflecting similar symptom patterns differing in 
severity (Fig. S1). A one-factor EFA supported a general severity 
dimension underlying ID and FD symptoms (Fig. S2; Table S7). MM-IRT 
models showed improved fit compared to LCA, and the six-class MM-IRT 
model was selected based on the lowest BIC, sBIC, and CAIC values and 
interpretability. The validation set produced comparable fit indices and 
class proportions and showed adequate classification quality (Table S8- 
S9). Item parameters and between-class external variable comparisons 
are reported in the Supplemental Results.

class, with 57.5% of the participants (Fig. 1; Table 3). Class members 
showed near zero endorsement probability of criteria-specific symptoms 
(<0.06), while transdiagnostic symptoms trouble sleeping, fatigue, 
concentration difficulty, and unrefreshing sleep were endorsed more 
frequently (0.19–0.28). It had the lowest proportion of women and 
overall severity profile, including the lowest levels of risk factors,

Class 3 (‘Tension/Pain’, 9.1%) showed a similar pain symptom

characterized by high endorsement of GAD symptoms muscle tension,

symptoms (0.41–0.56). This class had the highest proportion of women 
(70.8%). FD prevalence was similar to the Pain class, with 5.0% also 
meeting GAD criteria.
Class 4 (‘Anxiety’, 9.0%) was characterized by high endorsement of 
all seven GAD criteria symptoms. The most frequently reported were 
muscle tension (0.90), fatigue (0.82), and restlessness (0.70). Partici­
pants were comparatively younger (mean 42.4 years), more likely 
employed (86.9%), and reported high chronic stress. It had the highest 
prevalence of GAD (39.5%) and greatest proportion of MDD-GAD only 
comorbidity (7.9%). Compared to the Tension/Pain class, it showed 
higher MDD specific symptom endorsement and lower FD specific 
symptom endorsement. Correspondingly, 15.4% met criteria for MDD, 
whereas none met criteria for ME/CFS or FM.
Class 5 (‘Cognition/Fatigue’, 6.4%) was defined by high endorse­
ment of concentration difficulty (1.00), fatigue (0.84), difficulty 
thinking (0.80), and unrefreshing sleep (0.73). Moderate GAD (<0.58) 
and pain (<0.31) symptoms and minimal MDD symptoms (<0.15) were 
endorsed. This class showed the highest prevalence of FDs compared 
with the other classes.
Class 6 (‘Depression’, 4.3%) showed the highest endorsement of 
MDD symptoms across classes, particularly anhedonia (0.51), depressed 
mood (0.39), and appetite change (0.24). Moderate GAD symptoms 
(restlessness, muscle tension, and irritability, 0.30–0.44) and moderate- 
to-high transdiagnostic symptoms (0.45–0.67) were also reported. FD 
symptoms were modestly endorsed (<0.22). MDD prevalence (29.8%), 
disability-related unemployment (11.0%), and disability days were 
highest in this class, with greatest limitations in work/activities or 
productivity. The Cognition/Fatigue and Depression classes had 
approximately 16.0% of participants meeting criteria for at least one ID 
and one FD, the highest across all classes.
Transdiagnostic symptoms were endorsed across all classes, with the 
highest prevalence in the Cognition/Fatigue class. IBS symptom 
endorsement was similar across classes (0.12–0.16), except in the 
Healthy class, where it was lowest. ME/CFS symptoms sore throat and 
tender lymph nodes showed near zero endorsement across all classes 
(<0.03). Item parameters reflected these patterns, with transdiagnostic 
symptoms showing low difficulty and discrimination, and sore throat, 
tender lymph nodes, and IBS symptom showing high difficulty and low 
discrimination (Tables S10-S11).
4. Discussion
Hybrid mixture modeling was used to identify six classes of in­
dividuals based on ID and FD symptoms while capturing individual-level 
variation in symptom severity in a large general population sample. 
Resulting classes reflected distinct but partially overlapping symptom 
profiles, including predominantly FD-related (Pain), ID-related (Anxi­
ety), and mixed presentations (Tension/Pain, Cognition/Fatigue, 
Depression), alongside a large low-symptom (Healthy) group. Trans­
diagnostic symptoms, including fatigue, sleep problems, and concen­
tration difficulty, were consistently endorsed across all classes. ID-FD 
comorbidity was highest in the Cognition/Fatigue and Depression 
classes, with the latter showing greatest functional impairment. Our 
findings support a hybrid conceptualization requiring both categorical 
and dimensional features to capture heterogeneity in ID and FD 
symptoms.

symptoms using a hybrid mixture modeling approach. However, our

cases were present across all classes, indicating that IBS was not strongly 
linked to any specific class. This aligns with prior work showing that IBS 
formed a separate symptom cluster in a network model [11] and a 
weakly correlated latent dimension in a factor model [10]. Other studies 
have also highlighted the relative distinctiveness of IBS through its 
weaker associations with other IDs and FDs, larger sex differences, and 
conceptualization as a disorder of gut–brain interaction [5,77]. These 
findings suggest that IBS shares relatively few symptoms and clinical 
characteristics with the other disorders examined.
4.1. Non-specific symptoms and comorbidity
ID-FD comorbidity may partly reflect transdiagnostic or non-specific 
symptoms lowering the threshold for fulfilling criteria for multiple dis­
orders [78,79]. These symptoms occur across multiple diagnostic cate­
gories rather than being disorder-specific. All classes showed relatively 
high endorsement of the transdiagnostic symptoms. These symptoms are 
common in the general population and even their moderate endorse­
ment can increase the likelihood of crossing diagnostic thresholds. For 
example, most individuals in the Cognition/Fatigue class reported at 
least six out of seven GAD items, three out of nine ME/CFS items, and 
four out of six FM items. Although the Depression class showed higher 
overall MDD symptom prevalence, MDD and GAD co-occurred more 
frequently in the Anxiety class, where GAD and transdiagnostic symp­
toms were more and MDD symptoms less strongly endorsed. A previous 
study similarly reported significantly higher transdiagnostic symptom 
endorsement in participants with MDD-GAD comorbidity than in those 
with MDD alone [80]. The high endorsement of transdiagnostic

demonstrated previously, where MDD-ME/CFS comorbidity decreased 
from 10.7% to 2.1% when MDD was defined only by its two core 
symptoms, depressed mood and anhedonia [78].
Etiologically, non-specific symptoms may arise from multiple 
mechanisms rather than a single pathway, resulting in their high 
endorsement across disorders. Moreover, their endorsement may reflect 
different underlying processes depending on the clinical context and can 
complicate diagnosis. For example, somatic MDD symptoms, such as 
fatigue and sleep disturbances, may reflect pain rather than emotional 
distress in individuals with FM, allowing them to meet MDD thresholds 
despite endorsing few core depressive features [81]. Similarly, ME/CFS 
pain symptoms frequently co-occur in FM, potentially contributing to 
higher FM prevalence across classes, whereas sore throat and tender 
lymph nodes are rarely endorsed and are weak indicators of ME/CFS 
[10,76]. These findings indicate that some disorder-defining symptoms 
(e.g., fatigue and headaches) capture general malaise rather than 
disorder-specific pathology [10].
4.2. Strengths and limitations
A major strength of this study is the large general population sample 
size, allowing estimation and validation of the MM-IRT model in inde­
pendent subsamples. Additionally, ID and FD symptoms were assessed 
according to diagnostic criteria within the same wave rather than across 
the lifetime, enabling identification of transdiagnostic subtypes while 
minimizing recall bias. This study also has limitations. First, symptoms 
were dichotomized, potentially resulting in some loss of information 
from the ordinal FD variables. However, the model including a contin­
uous within-class severity factor likely mitigated this limitation by 
allowing gradation at the person-level based on overall symptom pat­
terns. Second, class assignment was based on highest posterior proba­
bility. This can introduce some uncertainty in class membership, as some 
participants may show clear distinctions while others may show similar 
probabilities of belonging to multiple classes. Third, IBS was represented 
by a single binary item, whereas other disorders were modeled using 
multiple symptoms. This measurement constraint may have contributed 
to the non-specificity of the IBS symptom. Fourth, we examined symp­
toms in the context of selected IDs and FDs. In a general population 
sample, similar symptom profiles may also arise from unmeasured 
conditions (e.g., trauma-related disorders or medical conditions). 
However, a previous general population study found that FD symptom 
classes remained largely unchanged after excluding individuals with 
chronic diseases [17], suggesting that such influences are unlikely to 
substantially alter overall class patterns. Lastly, participants in Lifelines 
are predominantly native Dutch and live in the north of the Netherlands, 
potentially limiting generalizability of our findings to populations with 
different cultural and ethnic backgrounds, where ID and FD symptoms 
patterns may differ [82,83].

patterns can yield substantial differences in diagnostic prevalence. 
Although the Tension/Pain and Anxiety classes showed similar GAD 
symptom endorsement, GAD prevalence was much higher in the Anxiety 
class (5.0% vs 39.5%). This likely reflects low endorsement of excessive 
worry in the Tension/Pain class, which is required for diagnosis. It is 
possible that some individuals in this class may have subthreshold GAD 
despite high average endorsement. In the Anxiety class, GAD symptoms 
co-occurred with additional ID and transdiagnostic symptoms, 
increasing the likelihood of meeting full criteria, whereas in the Ten­
sion/Pain class they appeared alongside FD symptoms and lower 
transdiagnostic symptom endorsement, reducing the probability of 
crossing the diagnostic threshold. This categorical approach overlooks 
the continuous nature of symptoms and clinically meaningful sub­
threshold features that may still be associated with substantial disability 
[81,84,85].
MM-IRT results showed that symptoms vary in their contribution to 
subgroup differentiation, with differences in severity thresholds and 
discrimination. Transdiagnostic symptoms and ME/CFS symptoms sore 
throat and tender lymph nodes provided limited information for iden­
tifying meaningful profiles; transdiagnostic symptoms were common 
across severity levels and sore throat and tender lymph nodes rare and 
only emerging at higher severity. These findings have implications for 
diagnostic frameworks, particularly ME/CFS, for which multiple case 
definitions use different symptom sets. Using different case definitions 
may influence the proportion of individuals meeting ME/CFS criteria 
across classes [86]. Therefore, evaluating individual symptom contri­
butions is important for refining classifications and identifying clinically 
meaningful subgroups.
With these six data-driven symptom-based classes, we do not yet 
propose replacing current diagnostic criteria for IDs and FDs. For ac­
curate classification of these disorders, it is necessary to consider vali­
dators alongside symptom-based criteria, such as familial incidence, 
biological markers, and treatment response [87]. Future studies incor­
porating additional validators are needed to clarify mechanisms un­
derlying comorbidity and to evaluate the clinical utility of these classes, 
including their ability to predict prognosis and functional outcomes.
Further research is necessary to understand ID-FD comorbidity. 
Transdiagnostic symptoms were consistently endorsed across classes 
and may act as bridge symptoms connecting IDs and FDs, potentially 
triggering or exacerbating other symptoms [11,88]. Longitudinal studies 
are needed to examine how shared vulnerabilities develop and 
contribute to comorbidity. Additionally, the Cognition/Fatigue and 
Depression classes showed high FD comorbidity with GAD and MDD, 
respectively. This could suggest potential bidirectional relationships 
between IDs and FDs [89,90], although this cannot be determined due to 
the cross-sectional design. Future longitudinal studies should assess 
class stability and identify predictors of ID-FD comorbidity. Methodo­
logically, we estimated unidimensional MM-IRT models and, based on 
prior evidence for the multidimensional structure of ID and FD symp­
toms [10], future work could extend this framework by including mul­
tiple within-class latent traits to examine whether they capture 
meaningful constructs.
5. Conclusion
Using MM-IRT, we identified six ID and FD symptom classes in a 
large general population sample: Healthy, Pain, Tension/Pain, Anxiety, 
Cognition/Fatigue, and Depression classes. Non-specific symptoms may 
contribute to ID-FD comorbidity by lowering the threshold for meeting 
criteria of multiple disorders. Empirically derived symptom profiles can 
inform diagnostic refinement by revealing differences in the clinical 
utility of individual symptoms, while longitudinal research is needed to 
clarify the mechanisms underlying transdiagnostic symptoms.
