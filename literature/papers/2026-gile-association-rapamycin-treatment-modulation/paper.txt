Abstract
Background and rationale  In our ongoing phase II observational pilot trial, the compounded formulation of low-
dose rapamycin significantly reduced fatigue-related clinical symptoms in ME/CFS subjects. Although the underlying 
molecular mechanism remains unclear, exploring metabolic pathways involving circulating blood-borne factors is 
warranted. Recent studies suggest that increased levels of purines may exacerbate oxidative stress in ME/CFS patients. 
It is not known if rapamycin modulates purine biosynthesis and improves disease symptoms. 
Methods and results  To address, we performed a comprehensive LCMS-based quantification of purine biosynthetic 
intermediates in plasma from responder cohort of ME/CFS participants, both at baseline (BSL) and after 90 days 
of rapamycin therapy (T3). Notably, differential regulation was observed in the enzymatic conversion of inosine 
monophosphate (IMP) to xanthosine-5-monophosphate (XMP) and hypoxanthine (HPX) between BSL and T3 
samples. Flow cytometry assays on PBMCs confirmed that rapamycin reduces IMP dehydrogenase activity, thereby 
limiting the conversion of IMP to XMP. Further analyses, including mitochondrial oxidative stress assessments, 
Seahorse OCR following purine supplementation, and flow cytometry indicate that altered purine levels can impair 
mitochondrial energy metabolism, and may contribute to inflammatory processes in microglia.

Post-exertional malaise (PEM) [13] is widely considered 
the hallmark symptom of ME/CFS and remains among the 
most poorly understood and difficult-to-treat aspects of the 
disease. PEM is characterized by a marked worsening of 
symptoms, including fatigue, pain, autonomic dysfunction, 
and cognitive impairment, following even minor physical 
or mental exertion. Symptom exacerbation is often delayed 
by 12–24 h after exertion and may persist for days or weeks. 
Pacing, an energy-conserving practice used by many ME/
CFS patients, does not adequately prevent or relieve PEM 
[20]. Although the molecular mechanisms underlying 
PEM remain unclear, several of our recent studies suggest 
that a subset of patients with mTOR activation-mediated 
impaired autophagy may represent a central pathologi­
cal feature of PEM [9, 21]. We further demonstrated that 
deficits in mitochondrial energy metabolism secondary to 
impaired autophagy may contribute to the profound mus­
cle fatigue and neuromuscular dysfunction observed during 
PEM episodes [17]. Supporting this hypothesis, our recent 
phase I clinical observational study [21] demonstrated that 
low-dose generic rapamycin (6 mg/week), a selective mech­
anistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitor, was well-tol­
erated, safe, and significantly ameliorated PEM symptoms 
in ME/CFS subjects as measured by validated clinical end­
points while simultaneously improving biomarkers associ­
ated with autophagy function as measured by peripheral 
blood. These findings support the therapeutic potential of 
rapamycin in ME/CFS and implicate dysregulated mTOR-
autophagy signaling in disease pathogenesis.
In addition to impaired autophagy, emerging evidence 
from our group suggests that abnormalities in purine bio­
synthesis may contribute to the pathophysiology of PEM. 
Purine biosynthesis [22, 23] is an essential metabolic pro­
cess that generates nucleotide bases required for DNA 
and RNA synthesis [24, 25] and supplies critical inter­
mediates involved in cellular bioenergetics, including 
ATP [26] and GTP [27]. Recent studies [28, 29] further 
indicate that excess extracellular purines may promote 
inflammation by activating purinergic P2X [30] and P2Y 
[31] receptors expressed on myeloid [32] and lymphoid 
[33] immune cells. Consistent with this idea, a recent 
nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-based metabolomic 
study [34] identified altered purine metabolites in ME/
CFS patients, suggesting dysregulated purine metabo­
lism. More recently, we demonstrated that plasma from 
ME/CFS subjects increased the activity of the purine 
biosynthetic enzymes inosine monophosphate dehydro­
genase 2 (IMPDH2) [35] and guanosine monophosphate 
synthetase (GMPS) [36] in human microglial cells under 
hypoxic conditions [34]. Furthermore, high-resolution 
metabolomic analyses [37] revealed significantly elevated 
levels of hypoxanthine (HPX), a purine intermediate that 
can promote reactive oxygen species generation by being 
converted to xanthine via xanthine oxidase [38]. Collec­
tively, these findings suggest that dysregulated purine 
metabolism may contribute to inflammatory signaling 
and oxidative stress in ME/CFS and raise the possibility 
that rapamycin may modulate purine bioavailability and 
attenuate inflammation while restoring mitochondrial 
energy metabolism [35–39]. 
The present non-placebo-controlled observational 
study utilized a uniform, compounded formulation of 
rapamycin [40] designed to improve dosing consistency, 
absorption uniformity, and overall tolerability [41]. Par­
ticipant retention in this decentralized “home-based” 
clinical trial was high, with an 83.33% study comple­
tion rate, allowing sustained longitudinal evaluation 
throughout the treatment period. Clinically, the study 
demonstrated consistent improvements across multiple 
validated outcome measures [42], including the Multidi­
mensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI), the SF-36, the Spe­
cific Symptom Severity Scale (SSS), and additional NIH 
Common Data Elements instruments [43]. Importantly, 
the decentralized study infrastructure further enhanced 
protocol standardization by integrating direct-to-patient 
drug deliver, mobile phlebotomy for biospecimen col­
lection, centralized safety monitoring through Quest 
Diagnostics, and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic 
assessments to evaluate therapeutic drug exposure at 
both the individual and cohort levels [44].
While investigating the molecular mechanisms under­
lying these clinical responses, Seahorse extracellular 
flux analysis demonstrated that a 90-day rapamycin 
treatment significantly enhanced mitochondrial oxida­
tive phosphorylation (OXPHOS) while reducing mito­
chondrial stress in peripheral blood mononuclear cells 
(PBMCs) derived from ME/CFS subjects. High-resolu­
tion LC-MS metabolomic analysis further revealed that 
low-dose rapamycin altered the bioavailability of several 
purine metabolites, including increasing inosine mono­
phosphate (IMP) levels alongside reduced xanthosine 
5-monophosphate (XMP) and hypoxanthine (HPX) 
concentrations. Flow cytometry analyses demonstrated

Workflow of clinical studies
For this clinical trial, participants completed a 90-day 
drug study period in which prior to starting the drug, 
participants completed consent documents, baseline 
questionnaires, and a baseline blood draw. After com­
pleting these baseline tasks, the participant was screened 
to ensure that they would be able to participate in the 
trial, as described above. Once the participant started 
the drug, the 90-day initial trial period began, in which 
the participants took their dose of rapamycin weekly 
and completed questionnaires and blood draws every 
30 days for the trial period (Supplementary Fig. 1A). A 
supplementary table (Supplementary Fig.  1B) lists the 
questionnaires and instruments used at each timepoint 
in this study (BSL-T3), all of which are recommended for 
use as objective endpoints in clinical studies by the NIH 
Common Data Elements disease specific questionnaire 
library. The medical history form and the “Question­
naire for ME/CFS Participants (QMEP)” collected at the 
baseline served to provide previous medical history data 
pertinent to determine subsets for post hoc analyses (i.e. 
viral or non-viral onset, date of diagnosis, comorbid con­
ditions etc.). The total and individual scored components 
of the SF-36, the MFI, and the BAS served as the study’s 
primary endpoints. The FUNCAP, FACIT-F, FACT-G, 
and the SSS served as this study’s secondary endpoints. 
See Statistical Methods for detailed methodology. De-
identified, responses and related study data (safety lab 
results etc.) were maintained in the Simmaron IRB 
approved REDCAP server housed at Cornell University 
that was only accessible as read only to study staff execut­
ing the data analysis. 
Recruitment of human subjects and rapamycin treatment
In our 2nd phase of rapamycin trial, all de novo ME/CFS 
subjects were enrolled according to the 2003 Canadian 
Consensus and IOM criteria, as previously discussed. All 
participants had to be between the age of 18–85 years 
old and reside in the United States to be enrolled in the 
trial. This group of 108 consented participants, currently 
involved in the second phase of the rapamycin trial, rep­
resents a new cohort receiving a different formulation 
and dosage schedule of rapamycin treatment.
Written informed consent was obtained as described 
earlier [21]. Criteria to categorize participants into three 
categories – responders, partial responders, and non-
responders- is the same as in Phase I of the clinical trial. 
Responder status was determined using SF-36, MFI, and 
enhanced IMPDH2 activity in ME/CFS subjects at base­
line, facilitating the conversion of IMP to XMP; how­
ever, rapamycin treatment inhibited IMPDH2, resulting 
in IMP accumulation and attenuation of the purine-
associated inflammatory signaling. Finally, purine sup­
plementation experiments, and siRNA-mediated gene 
silencing studies further demonstrated that altered cir­
culating purines may directly promote inflammatory 
responses in myeloid cells, an effect that was substantially 
mitigated by rapamycin treatment.
Materials and methods
Clinical services and partners
All clinical services in this study were performed by Age­
lessRx (Ann Arbor, MI), a licensed pharmacy and clinical 
provider. Simmaron Research compensated AgelessRx 
for these services on behalf of all participants. Patients 
were recruited by their clinical Principal Investigators 
(PIs) and following informed consent and completion 
of baseline study procedures. Eligibility for treatment 
with compounded rapamycin was assessed by Age­
lessRx providers. Participants deemed eligible received 
a monthly supply of the study drug, which was shipped 
directly to their homes. Monthly shipments continued 
thereafter, contingent upon the participant’s ability and 
willingness to complete required safety labs and other 
study milestones. AgelessRx provided prefilled “stand­
ing orders” (individual test request forms) for central­
ized safety laboratory testing (Quest Diagnostics) at 
predetermined study timepoints (baseline through 
T3 and beyond). These orders allowed participants to 
either present directly to a Quest facility or, more com­
monly, have safety labs collected concurrently with study 
samples via mobile phlebotomy services. The safety lab 
panel was taken every 30 days after baseline). Safety lab 
results for each participant at every timepoint were first 
reviewed by the ordering provider at AgelessRx and then 
securely transmitted to Simmaron study staff through a 
HIPAA-compliant, IRB-approved data transfer portal. 
Safety labs were also independently monitored by each 
participant’s clinical PI. Clinical PIs and AgelessRx pro­
viders worked collaboratively to review safety data and 
communicated with the overall study PI when interven­
tion was warranted, including consideration of repeat 
testing or temporary discontinuation of therapy. Notably, 
no participants in this study presented safety laboratory 
findings that precluded initiation of therapy, nor did any 
laboratory abnormalities during the study necessitate a 
hold or discontinuation of treatment. Additionally, Age­
lessRx and study PIs jointly evaluated potential contra­
indications to rapamycin therapy, including concomitant 
medications. One participant was excluded from the trial 
due to daily use of hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone.

Biobanking
From whole blood, plasma, serum, PaxGene® RNA, and 
NaCit-platelet depleted plasma were extracted, aliquoted, 
and immediately stored at -80 °C. To support longitudi­
nal follow-up, a biobank library was established, in which 
each participant was assigned an individual storage box 
containing the cryopreserved samples. This system 
ensured that there was clear traceability of each sample. 
Since each participant functioned as their own control, 
this individualized library system allowed for efficient 
access to participant samples at each timepoint (BSL-T3).
Upon receipt of the participant blood samples, whole 
blood was taken from one K2EDTA tube was used for 
isolating PBMCs using the STEMCELL EasySep™ Direct 
Human PBMC Isolation Kit (catalog #19654; STEM­
CELL) protocol. Cell count and viability was determined 
using a Countess 3 Automated Cell Counter (Thermo­
Fisher) after adding 1:1 (v/v) enriched cell suspension to 
trypan blue to a Countess Cell Counting Chamber Slide 
(catalog #10228; ThermoFisher). Cell count and viability 
were then saved on a hard disk. The enriched cell sus­
pension was then added to a centrifuge tube, and equal 
parts of PBS was added to the tube. The sample tube was 
then spun in a swinging bucket centrifuge at 300 rcf at 
20 °C for 10 min. After completion, the supernatant was 
aspirated and the pellet was re-suspended in CryoStor® 
CS10 (catalog #100–1061; STEMCELL) and aliquots 
were made with approximately 5  million live cells per 
1mL CryoStor®. The aliquots were then placed into 4 °C 
for 10 min, then transferred into a Mr. Frosty and placed 
inside a -80 °C freezer for 24 h. After 24 h the samples 
were then placed into liquid nitrogen.
Seahorse assay
For PBMCs
For preparation of the Seahorse Mito Stress Test, samples 
were pulled out of liquid nitrogen and placed directly 
on ice. Once thawed, cell count and cell viability was 
checked using the same protocol as in PBMC isolation. 
Cells were then plated based on cell density with approxi­
mately 400,000 live cells per well. The 96-well Seahorse 
plate was then spun at 300 rcf for 5 min, afterwards cell 
adhesion was checked. Excess CryoStor® from the cell 
solution was aspirated out of the plate and Seahorse XF 
Base Media was added to each well. Seahorse XF Cell 
Mito Stress assay was then performed using injections 
of 1 µM Oligomycin, 1.5 µM FCCP, and 0.5 µM Rote­
none + Antimycin A.
For C20 Microglia
C20 Microglia were plated into the Seahorse XF-96 plate 
and allowed to grow for 24 h at 36 °C up to approximately 
200,000 cells per well. After 24  h, the microglia were 
treated with serum-free DMEM supplemented with 10% 
serum samples (BSL, T3, and HC). Then the 96 well plate 
was placed into a hypoxia chamber (10% CO2, 5% O2, and 
85% N2) for 24 h. Before the assay, the existing media was 
washed and then replaced with assay-specific XF media. 
The Mito Stress assay media was XF base media with 10 
mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine. The 
Glycolysis Stress assay media was XF base media with 
200mM glutamine. Both the Mito Stress and Glycolysis 
Stress Tests were then run on the Seahorse XF-96 Ana­
lyzer. For Mito Stress, the assay was performed using 
injections of compounds as described under “PBMC 
Seahorse Assay”. The Glycolysis Stress Test assay was 
performed using injections of 10 mM Glucose, 1 µM Oli­
gomycin, and 50 mM 2-DG (Deoxy-D-Glucose).
Si-RNA Transfection Studies of P2X4 and P2 X7
C20 microglial cells were cultured on a 6-well plate. 
Once reached 80% confluency, cells were transfected 
with 5 picomoles P2 X4 and P2 X7 siRNA (Thermofisher 
AM16708) via Lipofectamine TM 3000 transfection 
reagent (Thermofisher L3000-001) as per the manufac­
turer’s protocol. After 4 h of incubation, cells were treated 
with serum-free DMEM supplemented with 10% serum 
samples (HC, BSL and T3) and placed in a hypoxia cham­
ber supplied with 85%N2: 10% CO2: 5% O2 gas blend for 
an additional 24  h and subjected flow cytometry/FACS 
analysis.

Pump configuration and setup
The pump setup for the LC system [45], including flow 
rate parameters and solvent routing. A C18 column (150

Mobile phase composition 
The gradient profile used for LC MS/MS separation. 
Mobile Phase A consists of 3 mM ammonium formate,

reproducibility.

To measure IMPDH activity, an IMPDH enzyme assay kit 
(Cat #: E-119) was obtained from Biomedical Research 
Service at the University of Buffalo. Around 105 PBMCs

the kit. The detection range aligns with physiological lev­

C20 microglial cells were plated in a chamber slide with 
complete DMEM media for 24  h to reach the desired 
(~ 70%) confluency. After 24  h the media was replaced 
with serum free DMEM supplemented with 10% plasma 
samples and incubated in a hypoxia chamber filled with 
a mixture of 85%N2 + 10% CO2 + 5%O2 gases. After 24 h, 
the plate was taken out and cells were assayed IF analy­

Power analysis
Power analysis was conducted to determine the n 
required to achieve sufficient power. The primary dif­
ference from baseline to T3 was derived from pilot 
Dunnett’s data. To achieve 80% power with the most 
conservative measures (accounting for multiple com­
parisons, alpha = 0.0167, half-effect, d = 0.50), the inflated 
sample size at baseline is 78 with 65 completers at T3 
(83.33% retention), exceeding the planned completer 
count and thus providing at least the target power for the 
prespecified effect size.
Statistical analysis
Percentages and p-values from the chi-square test of 
independence were calculated for categorical variables. 
The mean, range, SEM, and p-values from Student’s t-test 
were calculated for continuous variables. Summary data 
analysis was performed using Python version 3.12.8, pan­
das version 3.0.1, and SciPy version 1.17.1.
Changes in patient-reported outcomes measures were 
plotted using column graphs with individual points and 
SEM bars. A mixed effects model was fit using restricted 
maximum likelihood (REML), similar to a repeated-
measures ANOVA, and the p-values were reported. 
Geisser-Greenhouse correction was used to correct for 
the assumption of sphericity in all variance analyses. 
Between-group comparisons were made using Dunnett’s 
test for each time point versus the baseline. The two-
stage step-up method of Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli 
(BKY FDR) was used to correct for the false discovery 
rate (α = 0.05) for all analyses. Statistical significance was 
determined based on the resulting FDR-adjusted p-val­
ues. QQ plots were plotted for each mixed-effect model 
to assess the assumption of normal residuals. Statistical 
significance was set at p less than 0.05. Inferential data 
analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 11 version 
11.0.1.

Effect of rapamycin on the alleviation of clinical symptoms 
of fatigue in ME/CFS subjects: The design of Phase-II 
trial, execution, data acquisition, and analysis of clinical 
symptoms
Sex-specific dosing strategy and dose-ramping approach
The phase II rapamycin trial consisted of four major 
study milestones and observed patients for 90 days on 
compounded low-dose rapamycin: baseline (BSL), Time­
point 1 (T1), Timepoint 2 (T2), and Timepoint 3 (T3), 
with sex-specific dose-escalation strategies implemented 
for both male and female participants. For female par­
ticipants, T1 was reached after 30 days of treatment and 
marked completion of a four-week dose-ramping sched­
ule in which rapamycin dosing increased sequentially 
from 2.5  mg/week to 5  mg/week, then to 10  mg/week, 
ultimately reaching a maintenance dose of 15  mg/week 
through T3 (90 days) (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Male par­
ticipants initiated treatment at 5 mg/week and increased 
dosing by 5 mg/week each week until reaching a mainte­
nance dose of 20 mg/week through T3.
The rapamycin utilized in this study was a compounded 
formulation manufactured by Precision Compound­
ing Pharmacy (Bellmore, NY, USA) and distributed by 
AgelessRx. It is reported that compounded versions of 
rapamycin are three times less bioavailable compared 
to generic formulation, thus a dose of 15-18 mg/week is 
equivalent to 5-6 mg/week of the generic formulation. 
It is also reported that compounded formulations of 
rapamycin have slightly fewer adverse events, particularly 
in the dose escalation phase. Dose selection and escala­
tion schedules were informed by prior safety laboratory 
findings using the same compounded formulation in 
large independent clinical trials in healthy adults [41, 46], 
and in conjunction with recommendations from collabo­
rating clinicians and pharmacologic validation performed 
with AgelessRx.
Recruitment of participants, timeline, and drop-outs
A total of 108 participants were consented and screened 
on a rolling basis between January of 2025 and March of 
2026 (Supplementary Fig.  2). Following completion of 
baseline questionnaires and blood collection, 20 partici­
pants elected not to initiate therapy, while an additional 
4 participants did not meet independent medical screen­
ing criteria for rapamycin treatment. Of the remaining 84 
eligible participants, 78 initiated rapamycin treatment, 
whereas 6 participants had not yet begun treatment at 
the time of manuscript submission.
Among the 78 participants who initiated therapy, 3 
patients discontinued treatment during the dose-ramp­
ing phase prior to T1 due to adverse events that included: 
non-specific muscle twitching, gastrointestinal symptoms 
(diarrhea), and erythematous dermatitis. 75 participants 
completed treatment through T1. Between T1 and T2, 
1 participant discontinued therapy due to tenosynovitis, 
while three additional participants initiated an ongo­
ing drug holiday for personal reasons unrelated to study 
safety but prevented their continued participation in the 
trial. 1 participant remained actively enrolled between T1 
and T2 at the time of analysis. 70 participants completed 
T2, with 1 additional participant beginning an ongoing 
drug holiday thereafter (unrelated to study safety). 4 par­
ticipants remain on the study between T2 and T3 at the 
time of manuscript submission.
Overall, 65 participants completed the full BSL-to-T3 
protocol. Analysis of the primary endpoints identified 
that among these completers, 26 participants were clas­
sified as responders, 31 as partial responders, and 5 as 
non-responders. An additional 11 participants remained 
actively enrolled at the time of manuscript submission. 
Unless otherwise indicated, all analyses were performed 
using the combined cohort of trial completers and active 
participants (N = 76). Demographic and clinical charac­
teristics of this cohort are summarized in Supplementary 
Fig. 3.
Pharmacokinetic analysis of compounded rapamycin
Pharmacokinetic analyses were performed in 25 ME/CFS 
participants to assess rapamycin bioavailability following 
weekly dosing (15  mg/week in females; 20  mg/week in 
males) (Supplementary Fig. 4). Blood sirolimus concen­
trations were measured on the day of dosing (Day 0) and 
on days 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6 following dose administration.
Among participants assessed on the day of dosing or 
peak (N = 21), the mean plasma sirolimus concentration 
was 9.643 ± 2.855 ng/mL. Mean concentrations subse­
quently declined to 2.756 ± 0.627 ng/mL on Day 1 (N = 9), 
1.45 ± 0.233 ng/mL on Day 2 (N = 10), 1.0 ± 0 ng/mL on 
Day 3 (N = 3), 1.0 ± 0 ng/mL on Day 5 (N = 1), and 1.1 ± 0 
ng/mL on Day 6 (N = 1). Overall, these findings recapit­
ulate previously published pharmacokinetic data from 
large populations and FDA-approved studies of rapamy­
cin. Plasma sirolimus concentrations measured at the 
approximate peak and trough were comparable to those 
previously reported for both generic and compounded 
formulations in healthy adult populations.
Safety lab analysis
No statistically significant differences were observed in 
any safety laboratory parameters between baseline and 
T3 (Supplementary Fig. 5). We also did not observe any 
significant increase in reported infections throughout 
the study period. All reported adverse events (Table  4) 
were non-serious and resolved shortly after treatment 
discontinuation.

Assessment of general and physical symptoms of fatigue
All analyses were conducted using the combined cohort 
of trial completers and active participants (N = 76). Treat­
ment with low-dose rapamycin for 90 days resulted in 
statistically significant improvements in physical activity 
as measured by the Bell Activity Scale (BAS) (Fig. 1A). 
Mixed-effects modeling demonstrated a significant effect 
of time on BAS scores (F2.36,138 = 6.57, **p = 0.001). Post-
hoc Dunnett’s multiple comparisons testing revealed a 
significant increase in BAS scores from baseline (32.2) 
to T3 (38.1) (**p = 0.0055). Significant improvement was 
also observed between BSL and T2 (*p = 0.0209). These 
findings indicate progressive improvement in physical 
activity over the 90-day treatment period, with the most 
robust effects observed at T3.
Rapamycin treatment also significantly improved mul­
tiple fatigue-related domains assessed using the Multi­
dimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI), including general 
fatigue (F2.63,153 = 5.51, **p = 0.0021) (Fig.  1B), reduced 
activity (F2.84,164 = 3.26, *p = 0.0253) (Fig. 1C), and aggre­
gate MFI score (F2.43,144 = 5.90, **p = 0.0018) (Fig.  1D). 
Post-hoc analyses demonstrated significant reductions 
in general fatigue from BSL to T1 and T3 (*p = 0.0146, 
**p = 0.0067), as well as significant improvement in 
reduced activity (*p = 0.0389) and aggregate MFI score 
(**p = 0.0009) from BSL to T3.
Physical fatigue showed a strong trend toward improve­
ment but did not reach statistical significance (F2.55,149 
= 2.77, *p = 0.053; BKY FDR q = 0.0264) (Supplementary 
Fig. 6A).
Assessment using the Specific Symptom Severity (SSS) 
scale demonstrated significant reductions in several 
hallmark ME/CFS symptoms, including fatigue (F2.73,160 
= 15.4, ****p < 0.0001) (Fig.  1E), disturbed sleep (F2.70,157 
= 11.6, ****p < 0.0001) (Fig.  1F), post-exertional malaise 
(PEM) (F2.88,169 = 26.0, ****p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1G), and ortho­
static intolerance (OI) (F2.79,163 = 9.49, ****p < 0.0001) 
(Fig. 1H). Post-hoc analyses revealed sustained and sta­
tistically significant reductions across all timepoints 
(T1–T3), indicating durable clinical improvements 
throughout the treatment period.
Low-dose rapamycin treatment further improved mul­
tiple domains of general and physical health assessed by 
the RAND SF-36 questionnaire. Significant improve­
ments were observed in physical fatigue (F2.43,142 = 
12.4, ****p < 0.0001) (Fig.  1I), energy/fatigue (F2.58,151 = 
9.30, ****p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1J), bodily pain (F2.33,137 = 3.36, 
*p = 0.0309) (Fig.  1K), general health (F2.42,142 = 7.12, 
****p < 0.0001) (Fig.  1L), and aggregate physical com­
ponent score (F2.42,142 = 8.01, ***p = 0.0002) (Fig.  1M). 
Improvements in role limitations due to physical health 
approached significance but did not survive statistical 
correction (Supplementary Fig. 6B).
Assessment using the FUNCAP-55 Functional Capacity 
questionnaire demonstrated significant improvements in 
walking/mobility (F2.14,125 = 8.46, ***p = 0.0003) (Fig. 1N), 
activities outside the home (F2.14,126 = 4.27, *p = 0.014) 
(Fig. 1O), and aggregate FUNCAP-55 score (F0.975,57.2 = 
5.88, *p = 0.0191) (Fig.  1P). It was also found that there 
were significant improvements in personal hygiene (F 
0.435, 25.5 = 3.15, p = 0.1019) (BKY FDR *q = 0.0428) (Sup­
plementary Fig. 6C) from BSL to T3 (*p = 0.044) and sig­
nificant improvement in activities outside the home from 
BSL to T1 and T3 (*p = 0.0246, *p = 0.0294). No significant 
changes were observed in upright time, household activi­
ties, or sensory sensitivity to light and sound (Supple­
mentary Fig.  6D–F). Post-hoc analyses demonstrated 
sustained improvements in mobility and aggregate func­
tional capacity measures across all timepoints.
Assessment of emotional symptoms of fatigue
Mental and emotional components reported in the Mul­
tidimensional Fatigue Inventory survey (MFI) showed 
statistically significant improvements in mental fatigue 
(F 2.91, 170 = 4.82, **p = 0.0033) from BSL to T1, T2, and 
T3 (Fig.  2A). Dunnett’s post-hoc test showed signifi­
cant improvements in mental fatigue T1, T2, and T3 
(*p = 0.0234, *p = 0.0445, **p = 0.0022). No statistical signif­
icance was observed when evaluating reduced motivation 
(F 2.60, 149 = 2.07, p = 0.1154[ns]) (BKY FDR *q = 0.0441) 
(Supplementary Fig. 7A).

Rapamycin treatment improves mitochondrial energy 
metabolism in ME/CFS subjects with PEM
Impaired autophagy resulting from mTOR-mediated 
ATG13 inactivation [9], together with inflammatory sig­
naling [17], may contribute to persistent muscle fatigue 
observed in ME/CFS pathogenesis. Our recent clini­
cal study [21] demonstrated that low-dose rapamycin, a 
selective mTOR inhibitor, alleviated fatigue symptoms 
in a small patient cohort of ME/CFS subjects. Although 
restoration of ATG13-dependent autophagy may explain 
the therapeutic mechanism of rapamycin, the down­
stream functional consequences on mitochondrial energy 
metabolism remain incompletely understood. Previous 
work from our group demonstrated that genetic ablation 
of ATG13 impairs autophagy and significantly disrupts 
mitochondrial bioenergetics, drive proinflammatory M1 
microglia, augments oxidative stress, and disrupts myelin 
integrity in muscle serving nerves [17].Therefore, if 
mTOR 1 blockade via rapamycin reestablishes autophagy, 
could these downstream effector pathways also be posi­
tively affected via low dose rapamycin?
Given the potential role of rapamycin in restoring 
autophagic function, we performed Seahorse extracel­
lular flux analyses to evaluate mitochondrial oxidative 
phosphorylation (OXPHOS) in peripheral blood mono­
nuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated from ME/CFS subjects 
prior to rapamycin treatment (BSL; N = 20), after 30 days 
of therapy (T1; N = 10), and following 90 days of treat­
ment (T3; N = 8). To nullify the biasness of the study, 
we included both rapamycin responders (N = 9), partial 
responders (N = 13), and non-responders (N = 1) in BSL, 
T1, and T3 group. There were also two participants with 
unknown responder status due to dropping out due to an 
AE (N = 1) or an ongoing drug holiday (N = 1). To ensure 
assay consistency, all samples included had approxi­
mately 400,000 viable PBMCs per well.

Rapamycin modulates purine biosynthesis through 
regulation of IMPDH2 activity
Our next objective was to investigate the molecular 
mechanisms through which rapamycin restores mito­
chondrial energy metabolism in ME/CFS subjects. 
Recent studies [35, 38] suggest that disturbances in 
purine metabolism may contribute to ME/CFS patho­
genesis by altering circulating purine bioavailability. We 
previously demonstrated [35, 45] that impaired glycolytic 
and mitochondrial energy metabolism may drive com­
pensatory activation of the pentose phosphate pathway, 
thereby enhancing purine biosynthesis.
Measurements of plasma purines
To examine the relationship between purine metabolism 
and rapamycin-mediated metabolic recovery, we per­
formed targeted LC-MS metabolomic analyses of plasma 
purines in ME/CFS subjects before and after rapamy­
cin treatment. Method optimization, chromatographic 
calibration, standard curve generation, and metabolite 
characterization were performed using our in-house 
metabolomics platform as described comprehensively in 
the Methods section (Supplementary Fig. 9).
Specifically, plasma levels of inosine monophosphate 
(IMP), xanthosine monophosphate (XMP), and hypo­
xanthine (HPX) in ME/CFS subjects (N = 9) before treat­
ment (BSL) and after 90 days of rapamycin therapy (T3) 
(Fig. 4A) were analyzed. These individuals demonstrated 
significant clinical improvement based on BAS (Fig. 4B) 
and MFI (Fig. 4C) scores. LC-MS analyses revealed sig­
nificant upregulation of IMP following rapamycin treat­
ment, whereas XMP level was markedly reduced. These 
changes were confirmed by both pairwise (Fig.  4D–E) 
and cumulative statistical analyses (Fig. 4F–G).
Are these detected levels of IMP and XMP physi­
ologically relevant? Using PPB to µM conversion for­
mula (µM = PPB/ molar mass), average levels (± SD) of 
IMP (molar mass = 348.21 µg/mL) is 0.956 ± 0.704 in 
BSL and 1.83 ± 0.442 µM (Fig. 4H). Even though this 
is a very low-abundant purine metabolite, this range 
matches with previously reported data by other groups 
[47, 48]. Using similar conversion formula, XMP levels 
(molar mass = 364.21 g/mol) were found 0.134 ± 0.086 
in BSL and 0.0912 ± 0.070 in T3 plasma. Since that level 
of XMP is very close to not-detected level the quantita­
tive estimation of XMP in plasma may not be reliable 
(Fig. 4H). However, circulating levels of XMP have also 
been reported by other groups [49, 50], and their findings 
fall within a similar concentration range. Furthermore, 
to better understand the metabolic regulation of IMP 
to XMP conversion, PBMC-based cellular assay such as 
IMPDH kinase activity and FACS analysis needs to be 
done.
HPX, a downstream purine intermediate, was also 
found to be significantly decreased after rapamycin 
treatment (Fig. 4I). Reduced IMP levels at baseline may 
promote increased conversion toward HPX [51], which 
is known to contribute to peroxide radical generation 
and oxidative stress through xanthine oxidase-mediated 
metabolism [39, 52] (Fig. 4J). Together, these findings 
suggest that rapamycin treatment may alter purine flux 
toward a less oxidative and less inflammatory metabolic 
profile. 
IMPDH enzymic activity and IMPDH2-Active PBMC 
population analyses by FACS
Both IMP and XMP function as substrate and product, 
respectively, in a purine biosynthetic pathway catalyzed 
by inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH) 
(Fig. 4J). Although PBMCs express both IMPDH1 and 
IMPDH2 isoforms [53], IMPDH2 is preferentially regu­
lated during inflammatory activation [54]. The reciprocal 
regulation between IMP and XMP following rapamycin 
treatment suggests that IMPDH2 activity may be altered 
in treated subjects who reported a positive response to 
rapamycin therapy.
To evaluate IMPDH2 activity, flow cytometric analy­
ses were performed in PBMCs isolated from six ME/
CFS subjects at baseline and T3. Pseudo color density 
plot (Fig. 4K-L) and histogram analyses (Fig. 4M) dem­
onstrated a distinct population of IMPDH2-immunore­
active PBMCs in baseline ME/CFS samples, which was 
markedly attenuated following rapamycin treatment at 
T3. These findings suggest that rapamycin suppresses 
IMPDH2 activity in circulating immune cells. In order to 
apply more direct approach monitoring IMPDH activity 
(Fig.  4N), we performed a kinetic-based enzyme activ­
ity assay in PBMCs of ME/CFS subjects (N = 6) before

Purine supplementations regulated ROS production in 
PBMCs
To further investigate the relationship between purine 
metabolites and oxidative stress, mitochondrial super­
oxide levels were measured using MitoSOX-based 
flow cytometry. Baseline plasma strongly induced 
mitochondrial superoxide accumulation in PBMCs 
(Fig.  5D). Supplementation of baseline plasma with 
XMP sustained mitochondrial ROS production. How­
ever, addition of XMP to T3 plasma markedly elevated 
mitochondrial superoxide generation, whereas IMP 
supplementation had minimal effect (Fig. 5E). HPX sup­
plementation strongly induced mitochondrial superoxide 
in both baseline and T3 PBMCs. These findings suggest 
that XMP and HPX, downstream products of IMPDH2-
mediated purine metabolism, may directly contribute to 
oxidative stress in ME/CFS PBMCs.
Collectively, these analyses demonstrate that rapamy­
cin suppresses mTOR signaling, decreases IMPDH2 
phosphorylation and activity, reduces conversion of 
IMP to XMP, and lowers the bioavailability of oxidative 
purine metabolites such as XMP and HPX. These meta­
bolic alterations may contribute to reduced oxidative 
stress and improved mitochondrial function in ME/CFS 
subjects.

metabolism in myeloid cells

[57, 58], which in turn may promote inflammatory activa­

reduced mitochondrial superoxide loads accumulation

purine metabolism could influence the energy metabo­

microglial cell culture model [35], which induces

respiratory capacity (*p < 0.05), although maximal respi­

Discussion
While rapamycin is by no means a novel therapeutic 
agent, the use of low-dose regimens, such as those uti­
lized in the present study, differs substantially from the 
higher doses historically employed in transplantation 
medicine for immunosuppression. Interest in low-dose 
rapamycin outside of the clinical context of transplant 
emerged largely from in vivo studies demonstrating lifes­
pan extension and improved health span in mammalian 
aging models and now in human studies [67, 68]. Chronic 
activation of mTOR not only inhibits autophagy but also 
promotes geroconversion, a transition from transient 
cell-cycle arrest to a senescent state characterized by 
cellular hypertrophy, altered metabolic programming, 
mitochondrial dysfunction, and the development of the 
senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [69]. 
Notably, many of these same biological pathways, includ­
ing oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired 
energy metabolism, and immune dysregulation, are 
known to be dysfunctional in ME/CFS and dependent 
on chronic mTOR activation which has been described 
in a subset of ME/CFS patients. Therefore, clinical trials 
aimed to negatively modulate mTOR are highly relevant 
to ME/CFS. This phase II observational trial of rapamy­
cin provides additional support for the clinical utility of 
rapamycin in the management of key disease-defining 
symptoms of ME/CFS, particularly post-exertional mal­
aise (PEM), fatigue, and cognitive dysfunction.
Dosing strategy, clinical outcomes, and safety profile in the

Compounded rapamycin formulations have been 
reported to exhibit substantially lower bioavailabil­
ity relative to generics, with some estimates suggesting 
approximately threefold lower systemic exposure [70]. 
Accordingly, a weekly dose of 20 mg of compounded 
rapamycin is estimated to achieve systemic exposure 
comparable to approximately 6 mg/week of generic 
rapamycin [41]. Compounded formulations can be more 
affordable and accessible; however, sourcing derived from 
licensed providers with complete purity data is essential. 
Clinicians should recognize the bioavailability differences 
between the generic and compounded formulations to 
achieve correct dosing, maximize clinical benefit, and 
reduce adverse events [71].
The sex-specific dosing regimens used in this study 
(20 mg/week for males and 15 mg/week for females) 
were informed by published findings from the PEARL 
ageing study (NCT04488601), conducted in healthy 
adult populations; however, the biological basis for sex-

practice, individualized dosing strategies should incor­
porate patient age, cardiac history, standard hematology 
and metabolic laboratory values, longitudinal whole-
blood sirolimus peak/trough concentrations, CYP3A4/
P450 genotype status, dietary influences related to drug 
administration, and all potential drug-drug interac­
tions from concomitant medications [72]. Importantly, 
abrupt discontinuation of low-dose rapamycin is not 
contraindicated, and although dose-escalation strate­
gies are generally recommended to improve tolerability, 
a prolonged ramping period may not be necessary in all 
patients. Comprehensive information regarding known 
drug interactions is available through the U.S. Food and 
Drug Administration prescribing guidance and should be 
considered alongside medical history and routine safety 
laboratory assessments [70, 73, 74].
Longitudinal safety monitoring revealed no clinically 
significant alterations in hemoglobin A1C, insulin, com­
plete blood count, platelet count, lipid profile, or hs-CRP 
throughout the study period. Similarly, comprehensive 
metabolic analyses demonstrated no significant changes 
in lipid, cholesterol, glucose, or inflammatory biomarker 
profiles during the 90-day treatment period. While it has 
been widely reported that chronic rapamycin therapy 
can result in hyperlipidemia, impaired glucose metabo­
lism, and immunosuppression [75], we did not observe 
clinically significant changes in these safety laboratory 
parameters, nor did we observe an increase in common 
or opportunistic infections within our study population. 
Although our sample size remains relatively small, it is 
conceivable that the low-dose, intermittent administra­
tion schedule utilized in this study may mitigate some 
of the adverse metabolic and immunosuppressive effects 
that have been reported in transplant populations and 
animal models exposed to chronic high-dose and/or 
higher frequency.

Whole blood sirolimus concentrations taken at both 
“peak” (2 h after dose administration) and “trough” (taken 
approximately 48 h after dose administration) demon­
strated substantial inter-individual variability across the 
study population as reported by others. Sirolimus results 
are described in the Results section. Notably, neither 
peak nor trough sirolimus levels correlated with clinical 
response categories (responders, partial responders, or 
non-responders) [41]. Due to significant intra- and inter-
individual variability in sirolimus pharmacokinetics, drug 
levels often correlate poorly with clinical response, even 
in transplant patients, and thus require regular drug 
therapeutic monitoring. As such, it is not surprising that 
trough levels did not correlate with symptom improve­
ment in our low-dose, once-weekly dosing paradigm [74, 
76].
Future trials investigating the pharmacokinetics of low-
dose rapamycin should carefully account for CYP3A4/
P450 genetic status, fasting or the type of dietary intake 
at the time of dosing, and the timing of blood collection 
relative to drug administration. Sirolimus has low aver­
age bioavailability ranging between 14 and 20% due to its 
large and highly lipophilic structure. Oral formulations 
face additional challenges due to high CYP3A4/P450 
expression in the small intestine and liver. Studies have 
shown that administration of oral sirolimus with a high 
fat meal can slow absorption and reduce the peak Cmax, 
and the ratio of Cmax to AUC lengthening the time to 
achieve max concentration and can moderately increase 
the bioavailability up to 35% compared to when adminis­
tered under fasting conditions [77]. In addition to better 
drug absorption and increased bioavailability, we found 
anecdotal evidence in our cohort that a high fat meal at 
the time of dose appeared to reduce GI symptoms (gas, 
diarrhea and bloating).
Clinically, treatment with compounded rapamycin 
produced broad improvements across multiple validated 
measures of ME/CFS symptom severity, physical func­
tion, cognition, and quality of life. Significant improve­
ments were observed in physical fatigue-related domains 
measured by BAS, aggregate MFI scores, SSS fatigue, 
PEM, orthostatic intolerance (OI), disturbed sleep, and 
several SF-36 physical health domains, including bodily 
pain and physical functioning. Improvements in emo­
tional and cognitive domains including mental fatigue, 
communication, social functioning, and concentration 
were detectable as early as T1 and continued through 
T3. Notably, additional non-canonical fatigue assessment 
instruments, including FACT-G and FACIT-F, also dem­
onstrated significant improvements in physical, emo­
tional, social, and functional well-being, suggesting that 
rapamycin treatment broadly improved fatigue-associ­
ated symptom burden and overall quality of life in ME/
CFS subjects.
Wearable device data were collected from a subset of 
participants; however, the absence of a standardized 
wearable platform limited meaningful cross-subject com­
parisons. This limitation was driven in part by budgetary 
constraints, but reflects broader challenges associated 
with wearable implementation in ME/CFS research. Cur­
rent commercially available devices vary substantially in 
cost, battery requirements, software architecture, and 
accessibility to raw physiologic data, limiting inter-device 
compatibility for advanced metrics such as heart rate 
variability, sleep architecture, and estimated VO2 max. In 
addition, few platforms have been validated specifically 
for long-term monitoring in ME/CFS or other highly dis­
abled patient populations and are designed to track and 
promote physical exercise which is contraindicated in 
ME/CFS [78]. Future studies will require the implemen­
tation of a single, validated wearable platform suitable for 
decentralized clinical monitoring and compatible with 
the functional limitations of this patient population. Until 
such approaches become standardized, validated self-
reported outcome instruments, including those used in 
the present study, remain the primary measures of clini­
cal efficacy in ME/CFS research.
Taken together, the study enhancements implemented 
in Phase II resulted in a substantially higher study com­
pletion rate (83%) compared to Phase I (47%) and we 
found that compounded rapamycin was safe and gen­
erally well tolerated among a representative cohort of 
ME/CFS patients. While these results show promise, 
the lack of a placebo control in this trial must again be 
acknowledged.
Viral versus non-viral cohorts
Acute viral illnesses, including SARS-CoV-2 infection, 
are a well-recognized precipitating factor for a subset 
of ME/CFS cases. Accordingly, we investigated whether 
participants reporting a viral onset of illness demon­
strated a differential response to rapamycin therapy com­
pared to those reporting a non-viral onset. Among trial 
completers and active participants (N = 76), individuals 
with a reported viral onset history (N = 39) demonstrated 
a higher proportion of clinical responders to rapamycin 
therapy relative to the non-viral onset cohort. Specifi­
cally, 48.72% of participants in the viral-onset group were 
classified as responders, whereas only 18.92% of partici­
pants in the non-viral onset group achieved responder 
status. In contrast, partial responder classifications were 
more frequently observed in the non-viral onset cohort. 
Although these subgroup analyses remain exploratory 
and are limited by sample size and self-reported ill­
ness onset histories, the findings raise the possibility 
that viral-associated ME/CFS endophenotypes (includ­
ing Long-COVID patients who meet ME/CFS criteria)

Evaluation of functional outcome of phase-II rapamycin 
trial
While previous results indicated that rapamycin may 
boost autophagy in ME/CFS patients by increasing 
BCLN-1 and suppressing S258PATG13, molecular 
changes do not always equate to functional cell recov­
ery. Therefore, we conducted functional assays to assess 
rapamycin’s impact on metabolic recovery.
First, we examined mitochondrial energy metabolism in 
PBMCs from ME/CFS subjects
Seahorse OCR analysis showed rapamycin significantly 
enhanced mitochondrial reserve capacity, suggesting 
improved energy metabolism under stress. Additionally, 
higher reserve capacity negatively correlated with PEM, 
OI, and sleep disturbances.
Second, we evaluated rapamycin’s effect on inflammation in 
myeloid cells
ME/CFS is linked to impaired oxygen utilization, con­
tributing to OI and PEM. In vitro, exposing myeloid cells 
to ME/CFS serum under hypoxia increased inflamma­
tory markers (CD40, iNOS). Similarly, baseline plasma 
upregulated M1 antigen expression and iNOS. However, 
plasma from rapamycin-treated patients reduced M1 
antigens and iNOS while elevating anti-inflammatory M2 
marker CD206, highlighting rapamycin’s potential to mit­
igate inflammation in ME/CFS.
Third, we investigated the impact of rapamycin on purine 
metabolism and its relationship with inflammation in ME/
CFS
Recent studies indicate that dysregulation of purine 
metabolism may contribute to the pathogenesis of ME/
CFS. While the underlying mechanism remains unclear, 
our previous research [35] demonstrated that the 
impaired aerobic glycolysis followed by enhanced pen­
tose phosphate pathway activity may increase the biosyn­
thesis of purines associated with ME/CFS pathogenesis. 
In this process, IMP, a key precursor in purine biosyn­
thesis, is enzymatically converted to XMP by IMPDH2. 
Our previous findings revealed that myeloid cells exhibit 
elevated IMPDH2 expression under hypoxic conditions, 
resulting in increased conversion of IMP to XMP.
Notably, our LCMS assessment of purine metabolites, 
complemented by flow cytometry analysis of IMPDH2 
activity, showed that rapamycin treatment significantly 
reduced the activation of IMPDH2 in ME/CFS subjects. 
Although the underlying mechanism remains unclear, 
recent research indicates that inhibition of mTOR may 
contribute to the activation of the cellular phosphatase 
PP2A [79]. PP2A has been demonstrated to dephosphor­
ylate IMPDH2 at Ser122, resulting in its inactivation [55]. 
The diminished activity of IMPDH2 was further con­
firmed by the reduced conversion of IMP (substrate) to 
XMP (product) as quantified by LCMS study in T3 ME/
CFS subjects compared to baseline control (BSL). Other 
studies [45, 80, 81] suggest that mTOR inhibition can 
suppress tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) biosynthesis. Our 
results suggest that the elevated BH4 biosynthesis iden­
tified in our cohort of ME/CFS subjects is likely attrib­
uted to the increased IMPDH2 activity resulting from 
mTOR pathway activation. The relevance of elevated 
IMP level by rapamycin treatment may be attributed to 
the increased ATP biosynthesis via enzymic reaction of 
adenylosuccinate synthetase (ADSS) [82, 83]. ADSS pro­
tein is strongly expressed in muscle tissue and in fact, 
the genetic suppression of ADSS variant 1 (ADSS1), was 
found to be responsible for myopathy and neuromuscular 
abnormalities [84].
Furthermore, we found that rapamycin treatment 
decreased the bioavailability of HPX, another metabolic 
end-product of IMP. Since HPX upregulation promotes 
peroxide radical formation and oxidative stress, the 
reduction of HPX in T3 samples may indicate that the 
rapamycin-mediated suppression of HPX is critical in 
mitigating oxidative stress in ME/CFS subjects.
Fourth, we examined whether disrupted purine metabolism 
in ME/CFS subjects impacts the glycolytic index in myeloid 
cells
Inflammatory cells require substantial ATP, relying on 
glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphoryla­
tion for energy. During inflammation, oxidative stress 
impairs mitochondria, causing cells to shift toward gly­
colysis—a trend seen in M1 macrophages. However, this 
metabolic adaptation hasn’t been confirmed in ME/CFS 
pathogenesis.
Our study assessed how plasma purines from ME/
CFS subjects affect microglial glycolysis. ECAR analysis 
after mitochondrial disruption under hypoxia showed 
that BSL plasma strongly reduced glycolytic capacity 
and reserve, indicating suppressed maximal glycolysis. 
In contrast, T3 plasma restored glycolytic efficiency, 
suggesting rapamycin may improve glycolysis under 
stress.
Silencing P2 X7 with siRNAs enhanced cells’ response to 
BSL plasma, nearly restoring glycolytic capacity, though 
buffer capacity for acute energy surges remained lim­
ited. Conversely, P2X4 inhibition further decreased basal 
glycolysis without improving stress-related glycolytic 
parameters. These findings imply that elevated purines 
in ME/CFS may suppress glycolysis via P2 X7 signaling, 
but this pathway may not operate in microglia exposed to 
plasma from rapamycin-treated subjects.

Fifth, we also studied purinergic effects on mitochondrial 
respiration in hypoxic microglia
While mitochondrial respiration has been explored in 
ME/CFS, its regulation by purinergic signaling is unclear. 
We found BSL plasma significantly reduces mitochon­
drial respiration compared to HC plasma, and this sup­
pression persists after inhibiting P2 X4 or P2 X7. Inhibiting 
P2 X4 restored mitochondrial OXPHOS during stress, 
but not basal respiration. Rapamycin-treated plasma 
improved mitochondrial respiration in hypoxic microg­
lia, likely independent of P2 X7 signaling.
Study limitations and future directions
Limitations of the current study
Several limitations should be acknowledged in the pres­
ent investigation. First, the study was not designed as a 
placebo-controlled trial. Instead, each participant served 
as their own control, with baseline (BSL) measurements 
compared to those obtained after treatment at the T3 
stage. This approach may introduce certain biases and 
limit the ability to fully account for placebo effects or 
other confounding variables. Second, while the study 
provides evidence of dysregulated purine metabolism 
in ME/CFS subjects, the specific purine intermediates 
responsible for interacting with P2 X4 and P2 X7 receptors 
to drive oxidative stress and inflammation have not been 
identified. As such, a more detailed mechanistic inves­
tigation is necessary to characterize additional purine 
intermediates and their regulatory roles in this context. 
Third, although P2 X4 and P2 X7 receptors are two of the 
most extensively studied inflammatory purinergic recep­
tors, other purinergic receptors, such as members of 
the P2Y family, were not examined within the current 
scope of this research. Additional studies are needed to 
explore the potential involvement of these receptors in 
ME/CFS pathogenesis. Fourth, in some cases, results 
were obtained from responders, which introduces selec­
tion bias into the study. Fifth, the multi-modal responses 
to rapamycin—including enhancement of mitochondrial 
energy metabolism, suppression of inflammatory phe­
notypes, and alterations in purinergic signaling—may be 
associative effects rather than causal relationships. Fur­
ther studies and in-depth molecular analyses will identify 
more causal mechanisms. Finally, no prior power cal­
culations for the LCMS, FACS, or Seahorse OCR assays 
were done. These pilot experiments were designed as 
mechanistic, hypothesis-driven analyses, and the sample 
size of eight (N = 8) was accepted for the methodological 
rigor of our current study.
Future Directions
As a future direction for this project, we plan to recruit 
participants for a double blinded placebo-controlled 
step-wedge clinical trial to strengthen the validity of our 
findings. The step wedge design aims to ease enrollment 
by ensuring that all participants are guaranteed access 
to the study drug and not just the placebo; an impor­
tant factor when studying FDA-approved agents that 
are widely available. Ongoing studies are also focused on 
assessing the effects of compounded rapamycin on the 
regulation of autophagy markers, including Beclin-1 and 
S258P ATG13, to further elucidate the therapeutic poten­
tial and molecular mechanisms of rapamycin in ME/CFS. 
Rapamycin modulates immune responses by suppressing 
B cell activation [85], facilitates CD4+ ve cell expansion via 
activation of regulatory T cells [86], and promotes Th1/
Tc cell activation [87], which may collectively result in 
reduced inflammation, immunomodulation, and immune 
senescence that is beneficial not only for the suppression 
of autoimmune response [88], but also enhanced immune 
function during anti-tumor immunity [89]. To investi­
gate T cell regulation and metabolism in ME/CFS after 
rapamycin treatment, a high-resolution transcriptomics 
study will be conducted.
Collectively, our current study demonstrates evidence 
that plasma-derived purines in ME/CFS subjects may 
mediate purinergic signaling through the P2 X4/7 receptor 
pathway. This signaling appears to reduce mitochondrial 
oxidative phosphorylation under stress, which in turn 
promotes the development of an inflammatory pheno­
type in myeloid cells.
