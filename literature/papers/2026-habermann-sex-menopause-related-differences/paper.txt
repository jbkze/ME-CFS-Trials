Abstract 
Background: 
Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a multisystem disorder 
characterized by neuroimmune, autonomic, and gastrointestinal dysfunction. Previous studies 
identified coherent symptom domains involving Brain, Autonomic, Gut, and Immune 
manifestations and demonstrated pronounced sex-specific differences in neurocognitive–
sensory and autonomic symptom organization. However, whether immune-related and 
gastrointestinal symptoms form distinct or integrated latent structures in women and men with 
ME/CFS remains unclear. In addition, the potential influence of menopausal status on these 
symptom domains has not been systematically investigated. 
Methods: 
Data from 748 adults with medically diagnosed ME/CFS (608 women, 137 men) from the 
APAV-ME/CFS study were analyzed. Symptoms were coded dichotomously. Sex-stratified 
analyses included cross-tabulations, Cramér’s V, tetrachoric correlations, logistic and linear 
regression models, exploratory factor analysis, and structural equation modeling (SEM). 
Additional subgroup analyses compared pre- and postmenopausal women. Model robustness 
was evaluated using a stratified training dataset. 
Results: 
In women, Immune (flu-like symptoms, susceptibility to infections) and Gut (gastrointestinal 
complaints, food intolerances) symptoms formed two distinct but related clusters, characterized 
by moderate within-cluster correlations (tetrachoric ρ = .47–.60) and weaker cross-cluster 
associations (ρ = .30–.35). Exploratory factor analysis supported a two-factor solution, and 
SEM demonstrated superior fit for a two-factor model (CFI = .913; RMSEA = .119). 
In men, all four symptoms loaded onto a single integrated Gut/Immune factor, with consistently 
high intercorrelations across domains (ρ = .43–.68). SEM confirmed excellent fit for a one-
factor model (CFI = .981; RMSEA = .074). Susceptibility to infections emerged as the dominant 
predictor in regression analyses. 
Among women, menopausal status selectively affected immune-related symptoms. 
Premenopausal women reported flu-like symptoms more frequently than postmenopausal 
women, whereas gastrointestinal symptoms and food intolerances remained stable across 
groups. These findings suggest differential hormonal sensitivity of immune versus 
gastrointestinal symptom trajectories. 
Conclusions: 
Immune and gastrointestinal symptoms in ME/CFS exhibit sex-specific latent structures. 
Women demonstrate two partially separable but related symptom domains, whereas men show 

3 
 
a more integrated immune–gastrointestinal architecture. Furthermore, menopausal status 
appears to selectively modulate immune-related symptom expression while leaving 
gastrointestinal symptom patterns comparatively stable. Together, these findings support the 
concept of sex-dependent neuroimmune–autonomic mechanisms in ME/CFS and are consistent 
with a dynamic, hormonally modulated immune component alongside a more persistent gut-
related process. The results highlight the importance of sex- and hormone-sensitive approaches 
to phenotyping, mechanistic research, and therapeutic stratification in ME/CFS.

ME/CFS; symptom clusters; sex differences; neuroimmune dysfunction; gastrointestinal 
symptoms; menopause; dysbiosis; structural equation modeling.

Introduction  
Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a debilitating multisystem 
disease [1] characterized by post-exertional malaise (PEM) [2], neurocognitive impairment [3], 
autonomic dysfunction [4], immunological abnormalities [5, 6], and a broad spectrum of 
fluctuating somatic symptoms [7]. The disorder is typically triggered by infectious events and 
involves disturbances across multiple regulatory systems—including the nervous, endocrine, 
and immune systems—as well as impaired cellular energy metabolism and chronic low-grade 
inflammation, potentially contributing to disturbed physiological homeostasis [8, 9]. These 
findings are consistent with recent multi-omics and immunometabolic research reporting 
evidence for exaggerated innate immune responses, chronic inflammation, metabolic 
dysfunction, and gut dysbiosis in ME/CFS patients. 
Despite the multisystemic nature of ME/CFS, relatively little is known about how symptoms 
co-occur, cluster, or interact across biological domains. In a previous population-based study 
(Study 1), we showed that many symptoms appear in parallel across the disease course and that 
gastrointestinal and immunological symptoms frequently emerge early, particularly in women 
[8]. Building on these observations, Study 2 statistically validated four hypothesis-driven 
symptom clusters—Brain, Autonomic, Gut, and Immune—using exploratory and confirmatory 
factor analyses and structural equation modeling (SEM). These clusters demonstrated strong 
internal coherence and distinctiveness, supporting the hypothesis that ME/CFS symptom 
architecture may reflect underlying neuroimmune and autonomic processes [9]. 
Study 3 extended this work by examining sex-specific differences in the Brain/Brain22 and 
Autonomic clusters3. The results revealed marked sex-related divergence: women exhibited 
more homogeneous neurocognitive-sensory and autonomic clusters, whereas men showed more 
heterogeneous patterns suggesting vascular–metabolic and sympathetic involvement [10]. 
These findings align with established sex differences in immune function, where women 
typically show stronger adaptive immune responses and higher autoimmune activation, while 
men may exhibit weaker humoral immunity and greater susceptibility to certain infections [11]. 
Gastrointestinal symptoms are particularly relevant in ME/CFS. Multiple studies have 
documented alterations in the gut microbiota, reduced microbial diversity, and shifts toward 
pro-inflammatory taxa, suggesting a role for dysbiosis in disease pathophysiology. Recent

reviews highlight that dysbiosis may contribute to intestinal barrier dysfunction (“Leaky Gut”), 
microbial translocation, oxidative stress, and systemic immune activation, potentially 
exacerbating ME/CFS symptoms [12, 13]. Although causality remains unproven, these 
mechanisms are biologically plausible and consistent with broader evidence linking gut 
dysbiosis to neuroinflammation and blood–brain barrier alterations [14]. A 2024 review further 
emphasizes that gastrointestinal microbiota changes may influence immunological and 
inflammatory pathways relevant to ME/CFS, even though current evidence is largely 
associative [15]. 
Taken together, these findings suggest that immune and gastrointestinal symptoms may 
form interconnected clusters, potentially mediated by gut–immune–brain interactions. 
However, whether these clusters manifest similarly in women and men remains unclear. 
Preliminary evidence from Study 2 and Study 3 suggests that women may exhibit distinct 
Immune and Gut clusters, whereas in men these symptoms may converge into a single 
integrated cluster. Such sex-specific differences would be consistent with known biological 
patterns: women generally exhibit stronger adaptive immune responses and may show greater 
vulnerability to immune–gut interactions, whereas men may display stronger sympathetic and 
vascular–metabolic involvement as well as greater susceptibility to certain infections [11, 16-
18]. 
The present study (Study 4) therefore aims to: (1) determine whether Immune and Gut 
symptoms form two distinct clusters in women but a single integrated cluster (Combined 
Gut/Immune) in men; (2) evaluate the statistical coherence, distinctiveness, and latent structure 
of these clusters using bivariate associations, factor analysis, and SEM; and (3) situate these 
findings within a broader mechanistic framework linking immune–gut interactions to 
autonomic and neurocognitive symptom domains. 
By integrating statistical modeling with emerging biological insights, this study seeks to 
clarify sex-specific symptom architectures in ME/CFS and contribute to a hypothesis-
generating model that reflects divergent neuroimmune–autonomic pathways in women and 
men.

Methods 
Study Design and Data Source 
This study (Study 4) is part of a multi-phase research program investigating symptom 
architecture in myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS). It builds on 
findings from: (1) a population-based descriptive study of symptom frequencies (Study 1), (2) 
a factor-analytic validation of mechanistic symptom clusters (Study 2), and (3) an analysis of 
sex-specific differences in neurocognitive-sensory and autonomic clusters (Study 3). 
Study 4 focuses on the immune-related and gastrointestinal symptom domains. All analyses 
were conducted using data from the APAV-ME/CFS study, a predominantly qualitative 
investigation of the doctor–patient relationship that also collected quantitative symptom data. 
Participants 
A total of 1,238 individuals participated in the APAV-ME/CFS study. For the present analyses, 
we included only adult participants (>20 years) with a medically confirmed ME/CFS diagnosis 
(n = 748; 608 women, 137 men, 3 non-binary). Exclusion criteria were: 
▪ 
age ≤20 years, 
▪ 
absence of a medical ME/CFS diagnosis, 
▪ 
relatives of ME/CFS patients, 
▪ 
incomplete information on key demographic variables (sex, age, diagnosis). 
The sex distribution (≈81% women) is consistent with epidemiological data reporting a female 
predominance of 70–80% in ME/CFS (Figure 1).

Symptom Assessment and Coding 
Participants reported whether each symptom had occurred regularly in recent months. All 
symptoms were coded as dichotomous variables (0 = absent, 1 = present). Dichotomization 
reduces variance and may attenuate factor loadings and SEM fit indices; however, global model 
fit indices in Studies 2 and 3 demonstrated that the latent structure remains robust despite this 
limitation. 
The present study focused on four symptoms forming the hypothesized Immune and Gut 
clusters: 
▪ 
Immune: 
- flu-like symptoms 
- increased susceptibility to infections 
▪ Gut: 
- gastrointestinal complaints 
- food and other intolerances 
For integration analyses, we also considered the previously validated Autonomic and 
Brain/Brain2 clusters. 
Statistical Analyses 
All analyses were performed separately for women and men to examine sex-specific symptom 
structures. The latent variables identified in the present analyses represent statistical symptom 
structures rather than directly measured biological mechanisms. 
1. Bivariate Associations 
We first examined pairwise associations between symptoms using: 
▪ 
cross-tabulations and Pearson’s χ² tests, 
▪ 
Cramér’s V (effect size for dichotomous variables), 
▪ 
Pearson correlations, 
▪ 
tetrachoric correlations, which assume an underlying continuous latent distribution and 
are appropriate for binary indicators. 
 
 
 

8 
 
2. Logistic Regression 
Binary logistic regression models were estimated to assess the predictive relationships between 
symptoms. Depending on the analysis, either flu-like symptoms or susceptibility to infections 
served as the dependent variable. Model fit was evaluated using pseudo-R² statistics. 
3. Linear Regression 
Composite symptom scores were calculated by summing dichotomous symptom indicators to 
approximate continuous variation in symptom burden. Scores represented the sum of present 
symptom indicators and were treated as approximately continuous variables. The Immune score 
consisted of two dichotomous items (flu‑like symptoms, increased infection susceptibility), 
yielding a possible range of 0–2. The Gut score likewise comprised two dichotomous items 
(gastrointestinal complaints, food intolerances), also ranging from 0–2. The Combined 
Gut/Immune score represented the sum of all four indicators and therefore ranged from 0–4. No 
standardization or z‑transformation was applied. All analyses were conducted using raw sum 
scores to preserve interpretability. 
4. Factor Analysis 
Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with Varimax rotation was performed to evaluate the latent 
structure of the four symptoms. In women, we examined whether Immune and Gut symptoms 
separated into two distinct factors, whereas in men we tested a single-factor solution 
representing a Combined Gut/Immune construct. Given that each domain comprises only two 
indicators and the overall model includes four items, the factor analyses are considered 
exploratory and hypothesis-driven. Accordingly, we do not interpret the factor loadings in detail 
but use them to assess whether the observed symptom patterns align with the proposed latent 
structure. 
5. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) 
Confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) and SEM were conducted to test the fit of one-factor vs. 
two-factor models. All models were estimated using maximum likelihood (ML) in Stata 12.0 
because categorical SEM estimators (e.g., WLSMV) were not available in the software version 
used. Because the indicators were binary, ML estimation may yield biased standard errors and 
fit indices. However, tetrachoric correlations and cross-validation analyses suggested that the 
latent structure was sufficiently robust.  

9 
 
This limitation was addressed by: 
• 
using tetrachoric correlations, 
• 
cross-validating results with CFA convergence, 
• 
replicating all analyses in a stratified training dataset. 
Model fit was evaluated using: 
• 
χ² statistics, 
• 
Comparative Fit Index (CFI), 
• 
Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), 
• 
Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). 
However, ML can skew standard errors for dichotomous items, distort the RMSEA, and affect 
factor loadings. Results should therefore be interpreted cautiously, particularly the absolute fit 
indices. Furthermore, the male subgroup size was relatively small for latent variable modelling. 
Therefore, these findings should also be interpreted cautiously and considered preliminary. 
 
6. Stratified Training Dataset 
To assess robustness, all analyses were repeated in a stratified, randomized training dataset 
derived from the full sample. Stratification was based on sex and illness duration to ensure 
proportional representation and minimize sampling bias. Results were directionally consistent 
across datasets. 
7. Software 
All analyses were conducted using Stata 12.0. Graphical models were created using Microsoft 
PowerPoint. 
 
Results 
Sample Characteristics 
Analyses were based on 748 adults with a medically confirmed ME/CFS diagnosis (608 
women, 137 men, 3 non-binary). Because the non-binary group was too small for statistical 
modeling, results are reported for women and men only. Symptom frequencies for the Immune 
and Gut domains were consistent with previous studies: flu-like symptoms were reported by

76.8% of women and 73.0% of men; gastrointestinal complaints by 71.9% of women and 64.5% 
of men; food intolerances by 63.2% of women and 47.8% of men; and increased susceptibility 
to infections by 48.0% of women and 43.8% of men.

3. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) 
To examine the dimensional structure of Gut and Immune symptoms, three structural equation 
models (SEMs) were estimated: two separate one-factor models for Gut and Immune symptoms, 
respectively, and one combined one-factor model including all four symptom indicators.  
The first model represented a latent Gut factor indicated by gastrointestinal complaints and food 
intolerances. The factor loading for food intolerances was moderate (λ = .4231), while residual 
variance remained relatively high, suggesting a weaker association with the latent construct. 
The second model represented a latent Immune factor indicated by flu-like symptoms and 
susceptibility to infections. In this model, susceptibility to infections showed a comparatively 
strong factor loading (λ = .74) and lower residual variance, indicating a strong association with 
the latent immune-related construct. These models are just-identified (df = 0). Therefore, fit 
indices for the separate two-indicator models are not informative. 
A third model specifying a single latent factor underlying all four symptom indicators 
demonstrated substantially poorer fit to the data, χ²(2) = 23.78, p < .001. Within this combined 
model, gastrointestinal symptoms loaded strongly on the latent factor (λ = .91 and λ = 1.00), 
whereas immune-related symptoms showed considerably weaker loadings (λ = .54 and λ = .74).  

12 
 
Taken together, these findings support the distinction between two symptom domains rather 
than a single common construct. Accordingly, gastrointestinal symptoms (gastrointestinal 
complaints and food intolerances) and immune-related symptoms (susceptibility to infections 
and flu-like symptoms) were treated as separate latent dimensions in subsequent analyses. 
Overall, the findings suggest sex-specific differences in the organization of gastrointestinal and 
immune-related symptoms. In women, the data support a differentiated two-domain structure, 
whereas in men the symptom domains appear more integrated into a single latent construct 
(Tables 4 and 5). However, findings in men should be interpreted cautiously due to the smaller 
sample size.

4. Communalities and uniquenesses 
The analysis of communalities (h²) and uniquenesses (u²) revealed clear sex-specific differences 
in the underlying factor structure. Among women, the two immune-related indicators 
Susceptibility to infections and Flu-like symptoms showed very low communalities (h² = .15 
and .12, respectively), indicating that these items were only weakly accounted for by a common 
latent factor. In contrast, the gastrointestinal indicators Gastrointestinal complaints and Food 
intolerances demonstrated moderate communalities (h² = .36 and .44), reflecting a substantially 
stronger association with a GI-related factor. This pattern supports the identification of a two-
factor solution in women, comprising distinct immune and gastrointestinal dimensions. 
In men, a more homogeneous pattern emerged: all four indicators showed moderate 
communalities (h² = .26–.46), suggesting that each item contributed meaningfully to a single 
latent construct. Correspondingly, uniqueness values were in the mid-range (u² = .54–.74), with 
no indicator standing out as poorly represented. This profile is consistent with a unified, 
overarching factor integrating both immune-related and gastrointestinal symptoms. Overall, the 
communality patterns strongly support the sex-specific model differences: a two-factor 
structure in women versus a one-factor structure in men (Table 6).

7. Prevalence and latent factor scores 
 
Despite structural differences, women and men do not differ in the frequency of immune-related 
symptoms or in the mean Immune symptom score (all p > .05). These findings suggest that sex 
differences are more apparent in the structural organization of these symptoms than in overall 
symptom burden. 
 
8. Impact of hormonal transitions on immune and autonomic regulation 
 
Given the known impact of hormonal transitions on immune and autonomic regulation, we 
additionally explored whether the observed sex-specific factor structures differed between pre- 
and postmenopausal women. These analyses were considered exploratory due to limited 
subgroup sample sizes and because we could only roughly estimate which women were pre- 
and postmenopausal based on their age, since that information was not collected in the study. 
Analyses stratified by illness duration revealed distinct patterns for immune- and gut-related 
symptoms. Across the total sample, flu-like symptoms did not differ significantly between 
duration categories. However, when stratified by menopausal status, a significant association 
emerged only in postmenopausal women, with flu-like symptoms decreasing as illness duration 
increased (χ²(2)=7.01, p=.030, V=0.15). A similar pattern was observed for susceptibility to 
infections, which also declined with longer illness duration in postmenopausal women 
(χ²(2)=7.99, p=.018, V=0.16), whereas no significant association was found in premenopausal 
women. 
In contrast, gastrointestinal symptoms and food intolerances increased with illness duration in 
both groups. Gastrointestinal complaints showed a significant duration effect in premenopausal 
women (χ²(2)=8.92, p=.012, V=0.15) and a trend in postmenopausal women. Food intolerances 
demonstrated strong associations with illness duration in both groups (postmenopausal: 
χ²(2)=17.72, p<.001, V=0.23; premenopausal: χ²(2)=8.73, p=.013, V=0.14). 
These findings indicate that immune‑related symptoms are modulated by both menopausal 
status and disease stage, whereas gut‑related symptoms show a progressive, duration‑dependent 
increase independent of menopausal status (Table 7).

Symptom burden 
Across the full sample, women exhibited a significantly higher overall symptom burden 
compared to men. A linear regression model confirmed that the composite symptom score was 
higher in women (β = –0.307, p = 0.010), consistent with the group difference observed in the 
t-test (t = 2.5907). When examining the two symptom clusters separately, a clear pattern 
emerged: the Gut symptom score was significantly higher in women (β = –0.226, p = 0.002), 
whereas the Immune symptom score did not differ between sexes (β = –0.080, p = 0.255). Thus, 
the sex difference in overall symptom burden (Combined Gut/Immune) was driven exclusively 
by the Gut cluster. 

16 
 
Among women, menopausal status was associated with distinct symptom patterns. 
Postmenopausal women showed a significantly lower overall symptom burden than 
premenopausal women (β = –0.236, p = 0.018). This effect was specific to the Immune cluster, 
where postmenopausal women reported markedly fewer symptoms (β = –0.177, p = 0.003). In 
contrast, Gut symptoms did not differ by menopausal status (β = –0.059, p = 0.358). Interaction 
models including age (menopausal status × age) showed no evidence that age modified the 
association between menopausal status and either Immune or Gut symptoms (all p > 0.65). This 
indicates that the observed menopausal effect is not attributable to age differences, as the 
strength and direction of the association remained stable across the age range. 
Because fewer women were represented at older ages, the corresponding estimates show larger 
standard errors and wider confidence intervals. Together, these results demonstrate that sex 
differences in symptom burden are cluster-specific and that menopausal status selectively 
influences Immune-related symptoms. 
 
8. Summary of Sex-Specific Patterns 
Taken together, the analyses reveal a consistent pattern of sex-specific differences in the 
organization of immune-related and gastrointestinal symptoms. 
Symptom clusters across sexes. Flu-like symptoms and susceptibility to infections formed a 
coherent Immune cluster in both women and men, although associations were stronger in men, 
as reflected in higher tetrachoric correlations, higher factor loadings, and larger pseudo-R² 
values, pointing to a more tightly integrated immune symptom structure. Gastrointestinal 
complaints and (food) intolerances likewise formed a robust Gut cluster in both sexes, with 
comparable effect sizes but slightly higher bivariate correlations in men. 
Combined Gut/Immune structure. When the four symptoms were modeled jointly, the 
analyses suggested a sex difference. In men, a one-factor solution fit the data very well (χ²(2) = 
3.50, p = .174; RMSEA = .074; CFI = .981), consistent with a common immune–
gastrointestinal dimension. In women, the same model fit poorly (χ²(2) = 19.30, p = .0001; 
RMSEA = .119; CFI = .913), indicating a separation into at least two distinct factors. Figure 1 
summarizes these sex-specific factor structures.

Discussion 
This study provides robust evidence for sex-specific differences in the structure of immune 
related and gastrointestinal symptoms in ME/CFS. Across all statistical approaches—including 
tetrachoric correlations, logistic and linear regression, factor analysis, and structural equation 
modeling—women exhibited two distinct clusters (Immune and Gut), whereas men showed a 
single integrated cluster (Combined Gut/Immune) encompassing all four symptoms. 
Importantly, these structural differences do not coincide with differences in symptom 
prevalence or mean Immune factor scores. Symptom burden is comparable across sexes, but the 
latent organization of symptoms differs—it is not the frequency of symptoms but their inter 
symptom coupling that distinguishes women and men. These findings extend the results of 
Study 2, which validated the existence of Immune and Gut clusters in the overall sample [9], 

19 
 
and Study 3, which demonstrated sex-specific differences in neurocognitive sensory and 
autonomic symptom domains [11]. Together, these studies suggest that the symptom 
architecture of ME/CFS is not uniform but reflects sex-dependent neuroimmune–autonomic 
organization (Figure 2). 
However, the proposed neuroimmune-autonomic mechanisms should be interpreted as 
biologically informed models derived from symptom architecture rather than direct mechanistic 
proof. Because microbiome composition, autonomic physiology, cytokine profiles, and 
intestinal permeability markers —to name but a few—were not directly measured, the present 
findings remain hypothesis-generating and require multimodal biological validation.  
 
Sex-specific clustering patterns 
- Women: Distinct Immune and Gut clusters 
In women, immune related symptoms (flu-like symptoms, susceptibility to infections) and 
gastrointestinal symptoms (gastrointestinal complaints, food intolerances) formed two 
statistically separable clusters. Cross cluster correlations were modest, factor analysis yielded 
a clear two factor solution, and SEM confirmed a two-factor model fit the data substantially 
better than a one-factor model. Notably, when a unified one-factor Combined Gut/Immune 
model was tested in women, it showed poor fit, indicating the need for at least two latent 
dimensions. This structural divergence suggests that immunological and gastrointestinal 
symptoms are more differentiated in women and may be modulated by distinct hormonal, 
immunological, or neuro gastrointestinal mechanisms. These findings indicate that immune and 
gastrointestinal symptoms represent related but distinct biological axes in women. 
This pattern aligns with known sex differences in immune function [19]. Women exhibit 
stronger adaptive immune responses, higher rates of autoimmune activation, and more 
pronounced cytokine reactivity than men [11]. They also show greater susceptibility to 
dysbiosis related gastrointestinal symptoms and higher prevalence of food intolerances, 
consistent with the stronger Gut cluster observed here. Study 1 similarly found that 
gastrointestinal symptoms and intolerances increased markedly during the early disease course 
in women, supporting the notion that these symptoms represent a distinct and clinically 
meaningful domain [8]. 

20 
 
- Men: A Combined Gut/Immune Cluster 
In contrast, men showed a markedly different pattern. All four symptoms loaded onto a single 
latent factor, with high tetrachoric correlations across domains and excellent SEM fit for a one-
factor model. Across all analytic approaches, men consistently showed stronger associations 
than women: higher tetrachoric correlations, larger effect sizes in regression models, and higher 
factor loadings. The immunological symptom pattern (flu-like symptoms and susceptibility to 
infections) 
appeared 
particularly 
homogeneous, 
suggesting 
a more 
synchronized 
immunological response profile, and a similar—though slightly less pronounced—pattern 
emerged for gastrointestinal symptoms. Susceptibility to infections emerged as the dominant 
predictor in both logistic and linear regression models, suggesting that immune vulnerability 
may serve as a central organizing feature of symptom expression in men. 
This Combined Gut/Immune cluster is consistent with biological evidence that men exhibit 
weaker humoral immunity, higher susceptibility to infections, and stronger sympathetic 
activation than women. Sympathetic overactivation can amplify inflammatory responses, 
impair gut barrier function, and promote gastrointestinal dysmotility—mechanisms that may 
couple immune and gut symptoms more tightly in men. Study 3 similarly found that men's 
autonomic symptom structure was dominated by respiratory and cardiovascular dysregulation, 
reflecting a sympathetic–metabolic axis rather than the vagal–inflammatory axis observed in 
women [10]. 
Mechanistic interpretation: Dysbiosis, intestinal permeability, and 
neuroimmune signaling 
The sex-specific clustering patterns observed here may be interpreted within a mechanistic 
framework involving gut dysbiosis, intestinal barrier dysfunction (often reffered to as ‘Leaky 
Gut’), and neuroimmune–autonomic signaling. 
- Dysbiosis and intestinal barrier dysfunction 
Multiple studies have documented reduced microbial diversity, loss of butyrate-producing 
species, and increased abundance of pro-inflammatory taxa in ME/CFS [20, 21]. Dysbiosis can 
impair epithelial tight junctions, increase intestinal permeability, and facilitate translocation of 
microbial products such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS). These processes can trigger systemic 
immune activation, oxidative stress, and cytokine release—mechanisms that plausibly 
contribute to both immune and gastrointestinal symptoms [22]. 

21 
 
- Sex-specific pathways 
The findings are consistent with a model in which women may exhibit a vagal–hormonal–
inflammatory pathway: In women estrogen modulates and may enhance immune responses, 
epithelial barrier integrity, and vagal tone [23]. Dysbiosis-induced permeability may lead to 
LPS translocation and activation of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory reflex. This could 
contribute to why Immune and Gut symptoms remain distinct but interconnected in women 
[22]. 
The findings are consistent with a model in which men may exhibit a sympathetic–metabolic–
vascular pathway: In men sympathetic dominance can impair gut motility, reduce mucosal 
perfusion, and promote inflammatory signaling [24, 25]. Vascular–metabolic stress may 
amplify immune vulnerability and gastrointestinal symptoms simultaneously [26]. This is 
compatible with the Combined Gut/Immune cluster observed in men. 
These mechanisms are consistent with findings from Study 3, where women showed stronger 
vagal involvement in autonomic clusters, while men showed stronger sympathetic involvement 
[10]. 
- The role of menopause 
In this study, we observed that flu-like symptoms were more prevalent among premenopausal 
women, whereas gastrointestinal symptoms and food intolerances did not differ between 
pre- and postmenopausal participants. These findings suggest that menopausal status modulates 
specific symptom domains but does not uniformly affect the broader symptom architecture. The 
pattern aligns with known immuno-endocrine mechanisms: estrogen modulates and may 
enhance antiviral and inflammatory immune responses [11, 18, 27-30], increase mast-cell 
reactivity and amplify central sensitivity to peripheral cytokine signals [31-33]. As a result, 
premenopausal women may exhibit a lower threshold for mounting a pronounced sickness 
response, which is characterized by fatigue, myalgia, and flu-like malaise [34]. After 
menopause, declining estrogen levels attenuate these immune-driven responses, which is 
compatible with the lower frequency of flu-like symptoms in older women. 
Importantly, disease duration likely contributes to these differences. In our sample, 
postmenopausal women were older and had been ill for a longer period of time. Early disease 
stages in ME/CFS are typically marked by stronger antiviral and inflammatory signatures, 

22 
 
including interferon activity, cytokine peaks, and mast-cell activation—all of which drive 
flu-like symptoms [35-39].  
One possible interpretation is that, over time, these acute immune features diminish in at least 
a subset of patients and are replaced by more chronic metabolic, autonomic, and vascular 
dysfunctions [40-42]. Thus, the combination of higher estrogen-dependent immune reactivity 
and shorter disease duration may predispose premenopausal women to more pronounced 
flu-like symptoms, whereas postmenopausal women may show attenuated immune-driven 
symptom expression due to both hormonal decline and longer disease chronicity. 
In contrast, gastrointestinal symptoms and food intolerances were remarkably stable across 
menopausal status. This stability suggests that gut-related pathology may represent a more 
persistent and less hormonally responsive process. Current evidence indicates that once gut-
barrier dysfunction is established—characterized by epithelial impairment, dysbiosis, and 
microbial translocation—it may become self-sustaining through ongoing immune activation 
and microbiome alterations. Menopause has been associated with long-lasting shifts in gut 
microbiome composition, reduced microbial diversity, and persistent markers of barrier 
dysfunction and microbial translocation [40, 43-46]. These findings support the possibility that 
gut dysfunction may persist beyond menopausal transition and may be less sensitive to 
hormonal modulation than immune-related symptoms. This may help explain why 
gastrointestinal symptoms did not differ between pre- and postmenopausal women in our cohort 
despite clear hormonal modulation of immune-related symptoms. 
Our additional analyses revealed that disease duration modulated immune‑related symptoms 
only in postmenopausal women. In this group, both flu‑like symptoms and susceptibility to 
infections decreased with longer illness duration, whereas no such pattern was observed in 
premenopausal women. This potentially aligns with the previously described notion that 
immune‑driven symptoms are strongest in early disease stages and attenuate over time, 
particularly once estrogen‑dependent immune amplification is lost. In contrast, gastrointestinal 
symptoms and food intolerances increased with disease duration in both groups, indicating a 
progressive and hormonally independent process consistent with persistent gut‑barrier 
dysfunction. Together, these findings reinforce a dual‑process model in which immune‑related 
symptoms are shaped by both hormonal status and disease stage, whereas Gut‑related 
symptoms reflect a stable, chronic pathology that progresses irrespective of menopausal 
transition. 

23 
 
Taken together, our findings suggest a dual-process model: immune-related symptoms are 
dynamic and hormonally modulated, whereas Gut-related symptoms reflect a stable, structural 
pathology that persists independently of menopausal status. This distinction may have 
implications for personalized treatment strategies, highlighting the need to consider both 
hormonal context and disease stage when interpreting symptom profiles in women with 
ME/CFS. 
The present findings also align with the broader symptom architecture identified in Studies 2 
and 3 [9, 10]. 
- Women: A multi-axis, interconnected system 
Women show distinct Immune and Gut clusters, a coherent Autonomic cluster, a homogeneous 
Brain/Brain2 cluster, and moderate correlations across clusters. 
This pattern suggests a vagal–hormonal–inflammatory network, in which immune, 
gastrointestinal, autonomic, and neurocognitive symptoms are interconnected but retain distinct 
latent structures. Women exhibit a pattern of partially segregated but interconnected symptom 
clusters, consistent with recent systems‑immunology and neuroendocrine research. 
Contemporary work shows that women display more coherent yet distinctly modular immune 
and gut signatures, shaped by estrogen‑dependent transcriptional programs and X‑linked 
immune regulation [23, 47]. At the same time, multimodal analyses are consistent with 
moderately correlated but structurally separable immune, metabolic, autonomic, and 
neurocognitive modules, reflecting sex‑specific integration across inflammatory, hormonal, and 
neurovisceral axes [48]. Neurocognitive domains in women also tend to form more 
homogeneous clusters, consistent with hormone‑sensitive neuroimmune coupling [49]. 
Together, these findings support the interpretation of a vagal–hormonal–inflammatory network 
in which immune, gastrointestinal, autonomic, and brain‑related symptoms remain distinct in 
their latent structure while being functionally linked through shared regulatory pathways. 
- Men: A more integrated, sympathetic-driven system 
Men show a Combined Gut/Immune cluster, an Autonomic cluster dominated by respiratory and 
cardiovascular symptoms, and a more heterogeneous Brain/Brain2 cluster. 
This pattern suggests a sympathetic–metabolic–vascular network, in which immune and 
gastrointestinal symptoms are tightly coupled and interact strongly with autonomic 

24 
 
dysregulation. Recent neuroimmune and autonomic research supports the interpretation that 
men exhibit a more integrated sympathetic–metabolic–vascular network, consistent with a 
unified Combined Gut/Immune cluster and stronger coupling between immune and 
gastrointestinal symptoms. Contemporary work demonstrates that sympathetic activation 
tightly links metabolic stress, vascular tone, and inflammatory signaling, producing coherent 
immune–gastrointestinal modules that interact strongly with autonomic dysregulation [50, 51]. 
Parallel findings in gastrointestinal neuroimmune disorders show that immune and gut 
pathways become increasingly synchronized under sympathetic dominance, reinforcing the 
emergence of a single immune–gastrointestinal cluster [52]. Moreover, studies of the gut–
immune–brain axis indicate that men display more heterogeneous neurocognitive patterns 
alongside stronger immune–autonomic coupling, aligning with a more variable Brain/Brain2 
cluster and a distinct autonomic cluster dominated by respiratory and cardiovascular symptoms 
[53]. Together, these findings support a model in which immune, gastrointestinal, autonomic, 
and neurocognitive symptoms in men are organized by a sympathetic–metabolic–vascular 
architecture, producing tighter interconnections than those observed in women (Table 1). The 
exploratory factor analysis further reinforces these sex-specific patterns. In women, as 
described above, immune and gastrointestinal symptoms separated cleanly into two latent 
factors, with moderate loadings for immune symptoms and stronger, more coherent loadings 
for Gut symptoms. This structure indicates that immune and gastrointestinal domains represent 
distinct but interconnected biological axes, consistent with a modular, vagal–hormonal–
inflammatory architecture. In men, by contrast, all four symptoms loaded onto a single 
integrated factor, reflecting a tightly coupled immune–gastrointestinal dimension. The high 
cross domain correlations and unified factor structure support the interpretation that men exhibit 
a sympathetic–metabolic–vascular pathway, in which immune vulnerability, gastrointestinal 
dysregulation, and autonomic activation converge into one latent construct. Together, these 
EFA findings align with the broader SEM results and underscore that the organization of 
Immune and Gut symptoms in ME/CFS is fundamentally sex-dependent. 
Clinical and methodological implications 
Taken together, these results underscore the importance of sex-specific modeling in ME/CFS 
research (Figure 3). Because symptom prevalence and mean factor scores are comparable 
between sexes while the latent symptom architecture differs fundamentally, analyses that pool 
men and women risk obscuring clinically relevant structural patterns. The findings therefore 
provide a foundation for more nuanced diagnostic approaches and sex-tailored therapeutic 

25 
 
strategies that target the distinct neuroimmune–autonomic configurations observed in women 
and men. 
Clinical and translational implications 
These findings have several implications: 
(a) Sex-specific diagnostic patterns 
Pre- and postmenopausal women may benefit from separate assessment of immune and 
gastrointestinal symptoms. In men, these symptoms may represent a single underlying process. 
Sex‑specific differences in symptomatology and immune function suggest that tailored 
diagnostic assessment may increase diagnostic precision. In cardiology, sex‑specific 
high‑sensitivity troponin thresholds have been shown to reduce underdiagnosis of myocardial 
infarction in women and to identify high‑risk patients who would otherwise be missed [54-56]. 
Up to now, sex‑specific differences in symptom patterns, immune and hormonal profiles in 
ME/CFS are increasingly well documented [6, 57-59], but prospective studies demonstrating a 
direct clinical benefit of sex‑stratified diagnostic pathways are still lacking.

b) Potential biomarkers 
The sex-specific symptom architectures identified in this study may also have implications for 
biomarker research in ME/CFS. The distinct separation of immune and gastrointestinal 
symptom clusters in women, compared with the integrated Combined Gut/Immune cluster 
observed in men, suggests that different biological pathways may underlie symptom 
organization across sexes and may therefore require sex-stratified biomarker approaches. 
In women, biomarkers related to intestinal barrier dysfunction, microbiome composition, and 
vagal–immune regulation may be particularly relevant. Potential candidates include short-chain 
fatty acid (SCFA) profiles, markers of microbial translocation and intestinal permeability such 
as zonulin, lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP), and soluble CD14 (sCD14), as well as 
estrogen-sensitive cytokine signatures and mast-cell–associated mediators. Because the female 
symptom architecture was characterized by partially distinct but interconnected immune and 
gastrointestinal domains, autonomic measures reflecting vagal regulation—particularly heart 
rate variability (HRV)—may also provide useful indicators of neuroimmune integration. 
In men, the tightly coupled Combined Gut/Immune cluster and stronger association with 
autonomic dysregulation suggest that biomarkers related to sympathetic activation, vascular 
stress, and metabolic dysfunction may be more informative. Potential candidates include 
catecholamine-associated autonomic markers, sympathetic HRV indices, endothelial 
dysfunction markers, inflammatory-metabolic stress markers, and immune profiles associated 
with infection susceptibility or chronic low-grade inflammation. The stronger convergence of 
immune and gastrointestinal symptoms in men may reflect a more integrated neuroimmune–
metabolic response pattern. Altered natural killer cell function and immune dysregulation have 
repeatedly been described in ME/CFS and may contribute to the immune-related symptom 
architecture observed here [60}. 
Importantly, these proposed biomarkers should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than 
disease-specific indicators. Because the present study did not directly assess microbiome 
composition, autonomic physiology, cytokine profiles, or intestinal permeability, the suggested 
biomarker pathways remain inferential and require validation in multimodal longitudinal 
studies. Future research integrating symptom-network analysis with immune, autonomic, 
microbiome, metabolic, and vascular biomarkers may help identify biologically meaningful 
sex-specific endophenotypes in ME/CFS.

(c) Therapeutic strategies 
The present findings suggest that sex-specific symptom architectures in ME/CFS may also have 
implications for the development of more individualized therapeutic approaches. Because 
women and men showed fundamentally different patterns of immune–gastrointestinal coupling, 
therapeutic strategies targeting neuroimmune, autonomic, and gastrointestinal pathways may 
need to be adapted according to sex-specific biological organization. 
In women, the separation of Immune and Gut clusters together with the broader vagal–
hormonal–inflammatory pattern may indicate that interventions targeting gut-barrier integrity, 
microbiome composition, and neuroimmune regulation could be particularly relevant. Potential 
approaches may include microbiota-targeted interventions, dietary modulation, anti-
inflammatory therapies, mast-cell–stabilizing strategies, and treatments aimed at improving 
vagal regulation, such as breathing-based autonomic interventions or vagal nerve stimulation. 
Because immune-related symptoms in women are consistent with a model in which these are 
influenced by menopausal status and disease duration, hormonal context and disease stage may 
be important considerations when evaluating therapeutic responses. 
In men, the integrated Combined Gut/Immune cluster and stronger association with 
sympathetic–metabolic–vascular dysregulation suggest that therapies targeting autonomic 
imbalance, vascular function, and metabolic stress may warrant further investigation. Potential 
approaches could include autonomic regulation strategies, breathing and pacing interventions, 
treatments aimed at reducing sympathetic overactivation, vascular-supportive therapies, and 
infection-related or immunomodulatory interventions in selected patients. The tighter coupling 
between immune and gastrointestinal symptoms observed in men may indicate that combined 
neuroimmune–metabolic interventions are particularly relevant in this subgroup. 
Importantly, these proposed therapeutic implications remain exploratory and should not be 
interpreted as evidence-based treatment recommendations. The present study did not directly 
assess autonomic physiology, microbiome composition, inflammatory biomarkers, or treatment 
responses. Consequently, the suggested therapeutic pathways are derived from symptom-
network architecture and mechanistic interpretation rather than direct interventional evidence. 
Nevertheless, the findings support the broader concept that ME/CFS may comprise biologically 
distinct, sex-dependent neuroimmune–autonomic phenotypes that could benefit from more 
stratified and personalized treatment approaches. 

29 
 
Future prospective studies combining symptom-network analysis with biological profiling and 
longitudinal treatment outcomes may help determine whether sex-specific therapeutic strategies 
improve clinical response, symptom burden, or disease progression in ME/CFS. 
 
Limitations 
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, menopausal 
status was operationalized using an age-based cutoff (<50 vs. ≥50 years), which provides only 
a proxy for biological menopausal transition and does not capture perimenopausal fluctuations, 
hormonal variability, or the potential influence of hormone replacement therapy. Because 
detailed menopausal information was not collected, classification into pre- and postmenopausal 
groups remained approximate. 
Second, the analyses relied on cross-sectional data, precluding conclusions about temporal 
dynamics or causal relationships between hormonal status and symptom expression. The 
identified latent variables therefore represent statistical symptom structures rather than directly 
observed biological mechanisms. 
Third, all symptoms were assessed via self-report and dichotomized prior to analysis. 
Dichotomization reduces variance and may attenuate factor loadings, while self-report 
measures are additionally susceptible to recall and reporting biases. Because each latent domain 
was represented by only two indicators and the overall model included four binary items, the 
factor analyses should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-driven. Accordingly, the 
factor loadings are interpreted primarily as indicators of whether the observed symptom patterns 
are consistent with the proposed latent structure, rather than as precise estimates of underlying 
biological processes. 
Fourth, confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) and SEM were estimated using maximum 
likelihood (ML) in Stata 12.0 because categorical estimators such as WLSMV were not 
available in the software version used. Given the binary nature of the indicators, ML estimation 
may produce biased standard errors and fit indices. However, supplementary analyses based on 
tetrachoric correlations and cross-validation suggested that the latent structure was reasonably 
robust. 

30 
 
Fifth, subgroup sample sizes, while adequate for the primary analyses, limited statistical power 
to detect subtle structural differences between pre- and postmenopausal women and prevented 
formal tests of measurement invariance across groups. 
Finally, no biological markers were collected. Consequently, proposed mechanisms involving 
autonomic regulation, microbiome alterations, inflammatory pathways, or related physiological 
processes remain inferential and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. In addition, the 
analyses focused on a relatively restricted set of immune-related and gastrointestinal symptoms; 
broader symptom domains such as autonomic, neurological, or musculoskeletal symptoms may 
reveal additional menopausal effects not captured in the present study. 
 
Conclusions 
Immune and gastrointestinal symptoms in ME/CFS demonstrate fundamentally different 
clustering patterns in women and men. Women exhibit two partially separable symptom 
clusters, whereas men show a more integrated symptom architecture. These sex-specific 
patterns may reflect divergent neuroimmune–autonomic mechanisms and support the 
assumption that biological sex substantially shapes symptom organization in ME/CFS. 
Accordingly, the present findings provide further support for the development of sex-sensitive 
diagnostic frameworks, mechanistic models, and therapeutic approaches. In combination with 
Studies 1–3, the current results contribute to an emerging translational model of ME/CFS as a 
sex-modulated neuroimmune–autonomic disorder characterized by distinct immunological and 
gastrointestinal symptom trajectories. 
Furthermore, our findings indicate that menopausal status selectively modulates immune-
related symptom expression, whereas gastrointestinal symptoms remain remarkably stable 
across pre- and postmenopausal women. Flu-like symptoms were more prevalent in 
premenopausal participants, consistent with evidence for estrogen-dependent amplification of 
antiviral and pro-inflammatory immune pathways as well as the heightened neuroimmune 
reactivity typically associated with earlier or more active disease stages. By contrast, food 
intolerances and related gastrointestinal symptom patterns did not differ significantly between 
pre- and postmenopausal groups. This observation may support the hypothesis that 
gastrointestinal manifestations in ME/CFS are linked to persistent alterations of intestinal 

31 
 
barrier integrity and gut–immune interactions that, once established, become relatively chronic 
and less dependent on hormonal status. 
Taken together, these findings support a dual-process model in which immune-related 
symptoms appear comparatively dynamic and hormonally modulated, whereas gastrointestinal 
symptoms may represent a more stable and self-perpetuating pathophysiological process. The 
apparent dissociation between immune and gastrointestinal symptom trajectories further 
suggests that partially distinct biological mechanisms contribute to symptom persistence in 
ME/CFS. Recognizing these differential trajectories may improve clinical phenotyping, 
facilitate stratification in future research, and support the development of more targeted and 
individualized therapeutic interventions, particularly for women across different hormonal 
stages. 
Several limitations should nevertheless be considered. The cross-sectional design precludes 
causal inference regarding the temporal relationship between hormonal changes and symptom 
development. In addition, menopausal status was assessed categorically rather than through 
direct hormonal measurements, and symptom data relied on self-report measures. Future 
longitudinal studies integrating endocrine, immunological, autonomic, and microbiome-related 
biomarkers will be important to further clarify the mechanistic interplay between sex hormones, 
neuroimmune regulation, and gastrointestinal dysfunction in ME/CFS.
