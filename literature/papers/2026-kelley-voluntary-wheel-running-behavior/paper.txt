Voluntary wheel running behavior is reduced by SARS-CoV-2 S1 
administration and mitigates hypothalamic neuroinflammatory processes

A B S T R A C T
Post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC) is a major public health consequence of COVID-19. Persistent PASC symptoms affect multiple physiological 
systems, including the nervous system. The etiology of PASC is unknown; however, brain-mediated symptoms may arise from dysregulated neuroinflammatory 
processes. We have reported that intra-cisterna magna (ICM) administration of the SARS-CoV-2 spike (S)1 antigen in rats induces behavioral alterations, prolonged 
neuroinflammation, and reduced brain corticosterone. Inappropriate neuroinflammation triggered by immune challenge suppresses motivated behaviors, including 
voluntary wheel running (VWR), and disrupts diurnal rhythms. Accordingly, this study tested the hypothesis that S1 reduces VWR distance and changes diurnal 
activity patterns. Given evidence that regular physical activity reduces inflammation, we also determined whether VWR attenuates the neuroinflammatory effects of 
S1. Male Sprague-Dawley rats were housed with either locked (sedentary) or unlocked (VWR) running wheels. After 12 days, rats received ICM S1 or vehicle and 
remained undisturbed while VWR was recorded for 17 days. S1-treated rats ran less and showed disrupted diurnal VWR patterns compared with vehicle controls. 
Eighteen days post-ICM injections, sedentary S1-treated rats, but not VWR rats, exhibited persistent elevation of hypothalamic gene expression of IL1β, MhcII, Tlr2, 
and Tlr4. In contrast, VWR S1-treated rats, but not sedentary S1-treated rats, had elevated hypothalamic corticosterone 18 days post-injection. Taken together, S1 in 
the brain produces behavioral symptoms of PASC and prolonged neuroinflammation. Despite reductions in total running distance after S1, VWR was sufficient to 
block the persistent upregulation of neuroinflammatory genes. Brain corticosterone may contribute to the impacts of S1 and VWR.
1. Introduction
Long COVID, or post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 (PASC), is a 
persisting, remitting syndrome characterized by a constellation of 
symptoms that endure beyond the acute phase of SARS-CoV-2 infection 
(Al-Aly et al., 2024). In approximately 10% of patients (Davis et al., 
2023), a diagnosis of PASC is made after partial or complete recovery 
from an acute infection, with symptoms persisting for at least 3 months 
(Huerne et al., 2023). Symptoms of PASC are heterogeneous and affect 
multiple physiological systems, including the nervous system.
Brain-related PASC symptoms span cognitive and behavioral do­
mains. Cognitive symptoms, including deficits in attention and working 
memory, have been shown to be associated with reduced frontal-parietal 
and frontal-occipital connectivity (Niemczak et al., 2025; Ran et al., 
2026). Behavioral symptoms, the focus of this study, include exercise 
intolerance, post-exertional malaise, fatigue, and diurnal activity dis­
ruptions (An et al., 2024; Das et al., 2024; Gil et al., 2023a; Kolenicova 
et al., 2023; Stussman et al., 2025; Wilson et al., 2025). In general, pa­
tients with PASC experience a decline in physical activity, which may 
contribute to additional health problems, including reduced cardiopul­
monary fitness (Vogel et al., 2026).
Exercise intolerance and its more severe form, post-exertional mal­
aise (PEM), are characterized by extreme fatigue after physical activity 
that was previously nonchallenging. Both exercise intolerance and PEM 
are aversive and likely contribute to the notable decrease in physical 
activity among patients with PASC (Aschman et al., 2023; Durstenfeld 
et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2024). Gil et al. report that there is a 57% 
higher chance of physical inactivity among patients with PASC (Gil 
et al., 2023b). Furthermore, V´elez-Santamaría et al. found a signifi­
cant association between PASC and lower physical activity, with 44.7% 
of patients reporting a decline in physical activity since their PASC 
diagnosis (V´elez-Santamaría et al., 2023).
PASC patients also report daytime sleepiness and altered wake/sleep 
cycles (Batool-Anwar et al., 2025; Coelho et al., 2024; Wilson et al., 
2025). Both reduced physical activity (Say et al., 2025; Turner and 
Reed, 2022) and disrupted diurnal activity patterns (Almeneessier and 
BaHammam, 2018; Batool-Anwar et al., 2025; Nishimon et al., 2019)

Findings from animal studies and clinical evidence reveal a causal 
link between neuroinflammation and brain-mediated outcomes 
(Fontes-Dantas et al., 2023; Frank et al., 2022, 2023b, 2024). Evidence 
indicates that SARS-CoV-2 antigens can enter the brain, trigger inap­
propriate inflammation, and affect behavior. Rhea et al. demonstrated 
that SARS-CoV-2 antigens injected into the bloodstream can cross the 
blood-brain barrier and infiltrate brain tissue (Rhea et al., 2021). 
Erickson et al. determined that S1 directly affects endothelial cells and 
reduces blood-brain barrier integrity (Erickson et al., 2021). Frank et al. 
reported that intra-cisterna magna (ICM) injection of S1 into male rats 
increases microglial neuroinflammatory gene expression and protein 
levels in a TLR2-and TLR4-dependent manner (Frank et al., 2022, 
2023a, 2024). Furthermore, ICM injection of S1 produced anxiety-like 
behavior, disrupted sleep, and altered naturalistic home-cage behav­
iors in male rats (Frank et al., 2022, 2024). Finally, SARS-CoV-2 antigen 
also reduces serum and brain corticosterone, potentially sustaining 
neuroinflammatory processes (Frank et al., 2024).
Clinical and preclinical studies suggest that regular physical activity 
reduces neuroinflammation (Alves et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026; 
Kinoshita et al., 2026; Kruijff et al., 2026; Littlefield et al., 2015; Sahoo 
et al., 2026; Speaker et al., 2014; Zhou et al., 2026). VWR is sufficient to 
reduce inappropriate neuroinflammation following exposure to neuro­
inflammatory triggers, including lipopolysaccharide (LPS), stress, and 
aging (Alves et al., 2025; Grabowski et al., 2025; Grace et al., 2016; 
Littlefield et al., 2015; Martin et al., 2013; Speaker et al., 2014). 
Furthermore, several clinical studies have reported decreased serum 
glucocorticoid levels (e.g., cortisol) in patients with PASC (Klein et al., 
2023; Marques et al., 2023; Porntharukchareon et al., 2024). Hypo­
cortisolism can cause fatigue (Garcia-Gonzalez et al., 2024) and allow 
inflammation to go unchecked. Taken together, the clinical and pre­
clinical literature suggests that SARS-CoV-2 antigens present in the 
serum could readily cross the blood-brain barrier and trigger neuro­
inflammatory 
processes.

It is therefore important to establish an animal model that enables 
future studies to explore the links among physical inactivity, brain 
cortisol, and sustained neuroinflammation, and to develop interventions 
that restore these key anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Voluntary wheel 
running (VWR) is a non-stressful, innately motivated, and rewarding 
behavior that increases physical activity in rats (Dishman et al., 2006; 
Grace et al., 2016; Greenwood and Fleshner, 2008, 2019; Greenwood 
et al., 2009, 2011; Herrera et al., 2016; Kennedy et al., 2005; Sasse 
et al., 2008; Thompson et al., 2016a). Furthermore, VWR is an ideal 
method for assessing disruptions in diurnal activity patterns (Bowers 
et al., 2020; Clark et al., 2014; Greenwood et al., 2013, 2014; 
Thompson et al., 2013, 2016b).
The current study therefore tested the hypothesis that S1 leads to 
persistent reductions in VWR behavior and changes in diurnal activity 
patterns. Given evidence that regular physical activity reduces inap­
propriate inflammation, we also tested whether VWR attenuates S1's 
effects on neuroinflammatory processes and corticosterone in the hy­
pothalamus, a region previously shown to be responsive to S1 and to 
orchestrate the behavioral outcomes assessed in this study.
2. Methods
2.1. Animals
Male Sprague-Dawley rats (60-90 days of age; 200-250 g; Envigo, 
Indianapolis, IN) were single-housed on a 12-h light-dark cycle (lights 
on at 0700h) and housed in a specific-pathogen-free barrier facility. 
Animal care was provided once per week, and food (standard laboratory 
chow) and water were available ad libitum. Rats were acclimated to vi­
varium conditions for one week before experimentation. All experiments 
were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of 
the University of Colorado Boulder (Protocol Number 03004) in 
compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and 
Use of Laboratory Animals as well as the ARRIVE guidelines for animal 
use.
2.2. Experimental Design (see Fig. 1)
On experimental day −7, male Sprague-Dawley rats (N = 30) arrived 
at our facility and were immediately single-housed with a locked 
running wheel. After a 1-week acclimation period in the vivarium, rats 
were randomly assigned to either a sedentary (N = 10) or a voluntary 
wheel-running (VWR; N = 20) group (experimental day 0). We assigned 
more animals to the VWR group to account for known variability in 
wheel-running behavior (Garvey et al., 2015). Sedentary rats remained 
in home cages with locked running wheels for the remainder of the 
study. Running wheels were unlocked for VWR rats on experimental day 
0, and rats were allowed to run for 12 days to stabilize running patterns. 
On experimental day 12, all rats were randomly assigned to receive 
either S1 antigen (S1; 1.0 μg) or saline (Veh; sterile 1x PBS) via 
intra-cisterna magna injection (N = 5 Veh/Sed; N = 5 S1/Sed; N = 10 
Veh/VWR; N = 10 S1/VWR). Rats were then left undisturbed for the 
remainder of the study. Wheel-running behavior of VWR rats was 
evaluated through the end of the study. On experimental day 30 (18 
days post-treatment), rats were saline-perfused, and the hypothalamus 
was dissected.
2.3. VWR
All rats were housed individually in standard Nalgene Plexiglas cages 
(45 × 25.2 × 14.7 cm) with a stainless-steel open running wheel 
(49.5 × 26.4 × 36 cm) attached. Wheels were locked during the accli­
matization period (Exp. Day −7 – Exp. Day 0). For sedentary animals, 
wheels remained locked throughout the experiment. Following accli­
matization (Exp. Day 0), wheels for VWR animals were unlocked. VWR 
animals were allowed to run ad libitum for the remainder of the study. 
Daily wheel revolutions were recorded using VitalView Automated 
Data-Acquisition software (Starr Life Sciences, Oakmont, PA, USA). 
Distance (m) was calculated by multiplying the wheel circumference 
(1.081 m) by the number of revolutions recorded for each animal.
2.4. SARS-CoV-2 S1 subunit antigen
The full-length S1 subunit of recombinant SARS-CoV-2 was obtained 
from Raybiotech (cat# 230-30161). The S1 antigen (Val16-Gln690) is 
produced in HEK293 cells, free of the immunogen lipopolysaccharide, 
and is sequence-matched to the original Wuhan-Hu-1 isolate (Wu et al., 
2020). The injected S1 antigen is identical to that used by Rhea et al., 
who demonstrated that it crosses the blood-brain barrier into the brain 
parenchyma after intravenous (IV) injection (Rhea et al., 2021).
2.5. Intra-cisterna magna (ICM) injection
To avoid peripheral inflammatory impacts that could be induced by 
IV injection and to directly assess effects in the CNS, we injected the S1 
subunit (1.0 μg) or vehicle (2.5 μg, 0.2 μm filtered, sterile 1x PBS, pH 
7.4) ICM. The S1 subunit dose is based on prior studies demonstrating 
the neuroinflammatory effects of the S1 subunit (Frank et al., 2022, 
2024). This procedure does not produce detectable inflammatory re­
sponses, and substances injected ICM reach forebrain regions in the CNS 
(Frank et al., 2012). Proescholdt and colleagues also reported that ICM 
injection of substances produces widespread distribution throughout the

2.6. Tissue dissection
At the conclusion of the study, animals received a lethal intraperi­
toneal (IP) dose of sodium pentobarbital (150.0 mg/kg). After full 
anesthesia, cardiac blood was collected, followed by a 3-min trans­
cardiac perfusion with cold saline (0.9%) to remove peripheral immune 
leukocytes from the CNS vasculature. Brains were rapidly extracted, and 
the hypothalamus was bisected for protein and mRNA analyses. Tissues 
were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 ◦C.
2.7. Tissue processing for protein assay
Hypothalamic samples were sonicated on ice in a tissue extraction 
reagent (Invitrogen, cat#: FNN0071) supplemented with a protease in­
hibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, cat#: P2714). Homogenates were 
centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 ◦C, and supernatants were 
collected and stored at −80 ◦C. Total protein was quantified using a 
Bradford assay.
2.8. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
An ELISA for rat corticosterone (Enzo, cat#: ADI-900–097) was 
performed on hypothalamic protein lysates. The ELISA was run ac­
cording to the manufacturer's instructions, and protein and steroid levels 
were normalized to total protein.
2.9. Real-time RT-PCR measurement of gene expression
Total RNA was isolated from the hypothalamus using TRI Reagent 
(MilliPore Sigma, cat#: 93289) and a standard phenol:chloroform 
extraction (Chomczynski and Sacchi, 1987). Total RNA was quantified 
using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher). cDNA 
synthesis was performed using the SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase 
kit (ThermoFisher, cat#: 18064014). A detailed description of the PCR 
amplification protocol has been published previously (Frank et al., 
2006). cDNA sequences were obtained from GenBank at the National 
Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih 
.gov). Primer sequences are provided in Supplemental Table 1. Primer 
specificity was tested using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool at 
NCBI (Altschul et al., 1997). Primers were obtained from ThermoFisher. 
Melt curve analyses verified primer specificity. All primers were 
designed to span exon-exon boundaries and thus exclude amplification 
of genomic DNA. PCR amplification of cDNA was performed using the 
Quantitect SYBR Green PCR Kit (Qiagen, cat#: 204145). The formation 
of the PCR product was monitored in real time using the CFX96 Touch 
Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Relative gene expression 
was determined using Actb as the housekeeping gene and the 2−ΔΔCT 
method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Neuroinflammatory state was 
assessed by measuring the following: proinflammatory cytokine (IL1β), 
microglia/brain macrophage activation markers (MhcII), inflammasome 
(Nlrp3), TLR2 (Tlr2), and TLR4 (TLR4). The genes were chosen based on 
prior evidence showing the largest and most enduring S1-induced 
changes in the hypothalamus (Frank et al., 2022).
2.10. Statistical analysis
Running distance analyses are presented as mean ± SEM and were 
analyzed using a repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni-Holm post 
hoc tests to correct for multiple comparisons. Where appropriate, 
running distance was computed as a percentage of baseline running, and 
data were analyzed using a repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni- 
Holm post hoc tests to correct for multiple comparisons. Area under the 
curve (AUC) was computed for daily running distances as a percent of 
baseline running, and unpaired t-tests were performed between the 
vehicle and S1 groups. The threshold for statistical significance was set 
at α = 0.05. Sample sizes are provided in figure captions.
ClockLab (Actimetrics), as previously described (Thompson et al., 
2021), was used to assess diurnal running-wheel patterns and to deter­
mine the acrophase, bathyphase, onset, and offset of running behavior. 
Acrophase and bathyphase are automatically calculated in the ClockLab 
analysis by fitting a sine function to the daily activity. Acrophase and 
bathyphase indicate the peak (maximum) and trough (minimum) of the 
resulting sine wave. Onset and offset times are estimated using a 
least-squares fit algorithm on the daily activity data. For each marker, 
the data are initially exported at the circadian hour of the event based on 
a 24-h period. Diurnal phase markers, acrophase (peak), bathyphase 
(trough), onset, and offset of activity were assessed as a percent change 
from baseline. Area under the curve (AUC) was computed for the 
percent of baseline for all diurnal markers and unpaired t-tests were 
performed between the vehicle and S1 groups.
All neuroimmune measures are presented as % of vehicle control and 
were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA with unprotected Fisher's LSD 
post hoc tests to correct for multiple comparisons. Analyses were con­
ducted using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, LLC).
3. Results
3.1. SARS-CoV-2 S1 subunit treatment reduces VWR distance
The average total running distance (m) across the study was lower in 
S1- than in vehicle-injected animals. The reduction in running distance 
emerged 7 days after injection (Supplemental Fig. 1). When analyzed as 
a percent of baseline running, VWR was reduced 1 week after S1 
compared with vehicle and remained lower throughout the study 
(F(1,18) = 6.73, p = 0.018; Fig. 2A). Area under the curve analysis 
revealed a significant difference in total distance run between groups 
(df = 16, t = 3.738, p = 0.0018; Fig. 2B).
3.2. SARS-CoV-2 S1 subunit alters the diurnal pattern of VWR
3.2.1. Acrophase and bathyphase
Compared with vehicle-treated rats, S1 induced a misalignment in 
the acrophase or peak of running behavior (Fig. 3A). Following S1 
administration, S1 animals displayed, on average, a 6.13% shift in the 
time of peak activity, from 21:52 (9:52 p.m.) to 22:49 (10:49 p.m.). In 
contrast, vehicle animals displayed, on average, a 1.92% shift in peak 
activity from 21:52 (9:52 p.m.) to 21:55 (9:55 p.m.). AUC was computed 
for vehicle and S1 treatment groups to provide a summary measure 
across time (17 days) post-injection (df = 18, t = 2.335, p = 0.0313; 
Fig. 3B).
Prior S1 treatment also produced a misalignment in the bathyphase, 
the lowest level or trough of running behavior (Fig. 3C). Following S1 
administration, S1 animals displayed, on average, a 15.27% shift in the 
activity trough from 09:51 (9:51 a.m.) to 10:58 (10:58 a.m.). In contrast, 
vehicle animals displayed, on average, a 5.42% shift in the activity 
trough from 09:51 (9:51 a.m.) to 10:01 (10:01 a.m.). AUC was computed 
for vehicle and S1 treatment groups to provide a summary measure 
across time (17 days) post-injection (df = 18, t = 2.278, p = 0.0351; 
Fig. 3D). S1-injected animals exhibited a shift toward a later acrophase 
and bathyphase, demonstrating a significant effect of S1 injection on 
diurnal running patterns.

4. Discussion
ICM S1 reduces VWR, disrupts diurnal rhythms, and upregulates 
markers of microglial priming and activation in the hypothalamus in 
sedentary rats. Physical activity and brain cortisol regulate neuro­
inflammatory processes. Despite reduced total running distance, VWR 
was sufficient to prevent persistent upregulation of neuroinflammatory 
gene expression and to increase hypothalamic corticosterone 18 days 
after S1. The results of this study suggest that S1 induces inappropriate 
neuroinflammatory processes, which may be sustained by the loss of key 
anti-inflammatory factors, including physical activity and brain cortisol 
(Aoyagi et al., 2025; Kruijff et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 
2026; Zhou et al., 2026).
4.1. S1 reduces VWR
Our previous work established that S1 is sufficient to produce 
anxiety-like behavior, disrupted sleep, and changes in naturalistic home- 
cage behaviors, including reduced total activity, water intake, and self- 
grooming (Frank et al., 2022). Biological and behavioral symptoms

The sickness response is a constellation of adaptive behavioral and 
physiological responses to infection or injury, common to humans and 
animals (Dantzer et al., 2008; Saxena and Maze, 2018). A key aspect of 
the sickness response is the reorganization of motivational states from 
reward-seeking to coping behaviors after immunological challenges 
(Dantzer, 2001).
VWR, like human running, is intrinsically motivating and rewarding 
(Cadegiani and Kater, 2017; Heyse et al., 2015; Saul et al., 2017; Say 
et al., 2025). Rats will bar-press to access a running wheel, further 
underscoring the intrinsic motivation for VWR (Iversen, 1993). Animal 
studies show that VWR engages reward-related neurocircuitry and that 
conditioned place preference can develop for locations associated with a 
running wheel (Greenwood et al., 2011; Herrera et al., 2016).
Neuroinflammatory processes are well established to play a causal 
role in the sickness response (Dantzer et al., 2008). Harden and col­
leagues demonstrated that inflammatory cytokines in the brain 
contributed to LPS-evoked sickness responses, including fever, 
decreased body mass, decreased food intake, and VWR (Harden et al., 
2011). Reductions in home-cage locomotion and VWR occur within 
hours and persist for 24-48 h after LPS (Alencar et al., 2026; Harden 
et al., 2011). In contrast, reduced VWR emerges after 5-7 days and 
persists for 18 days following ICM S1 injection (Supplemental Fig. 1). 
This pattern does not align with a typical acute sickness response to a 
proinflammatory challenge such as LPS (Dantzer et al., 2008), suggest­
ing that S1 triggers atypical sickness responses and that reduced VWR 
may be more reflective of exercise intolerance than sickness.

animals, specifically hypothalamic IL1β, MhcII, Tlr2, and Tlr4. We found 
no detectable differences in Nlrp3 gene expression at this time point in 
sedentary animals.
We previously reported that ICM S1 dose-dependently upregulates 
genes associated with microglial priming and activation (Frank et al., 
2022) Twenty-four hours after rats were injected with S1, microglia/­
brain macrophage activation markers (Iba1, Cd11b, MhcII), an astrocyte 
activation marker (Gfap), Tlr4, Nlrp3, Il1b, and Il6 gene expression, and 
inflammatory protein concentrations (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF, CXCL1, IL-10) 
were elevated in the hypothalamus compared with vehicle controls. 
These findings suggest that S1 acutely induces microglial activation 
after 8 and 24 h. Furthermore, we demonstrated evidence of persistent 
microglial priming (Frank et al., 2024). Rats received either ICM S1 or 
ICM vehicle. Twenty-one days after S1, microglia were isolated and 
stimulated with LPS. Microglia from rats previously treated with S1, 
compared with vehicle-treated rats, produced more IL-1β and IL-6 pro­
tein in culture. The current findings extend our previous work and 
support the conclusion that ICM S1 activates long-lived, inappropriate 
neuroinflammatory processes.
4.3. Neuroinflammation and behavior
As previously described, Frank et al. reported evidence that ICM S1 
induces persistent microglial priming. S1 priming resulted in greater 
proinflammatory responses and greater disruptions in home-cage 
behavior following LPS than in vehicle rats. Behavioral changes 
included reduced activity, drinking, and self-grooming (Frank et al., 
2024). Other groups are revealing potential mechanisms underlying 
behavioral deficits produced by SARS-CoV-2 antigen in the brain. Yuan 
et al., using conditional knockout mice, recently showed that 
depressive-like behaviors after injection of the spike receptor-binding 
domain into the brain depend on astrocytic Cx43-mediated disruption 
of intercellular gap junction communication (Yuan et al., 2026). Using 
whole-cell patch-clamp recordings, Lee et al. demonstrated that inap­
propriate neuroinflammation and depressive-like behavior following S1 
injection into the brain result from upregulation of microglial Kv1.3 
channel activity (Lee et al., 2026). Interestingly, fatigue measures ob­
tained using the Rotorad handgrip test were unaffected by S1. Inap­
propriate neuroinflammation also disrupts diurnal rhythms, including 
VWR (Bowers et al., 2022; Greenwood and Fleshner, 2019; Thompson

4.4. PASC and hypocortisolism
Glucocorticoid dysregulation may also contribute to the behavioral 
symptoms of PASC (Peter et al., 2025; Troscher et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 
2024). Recent reports have documented decreased serum glucocorticoid 
levels (e.g., cortisol) in patients with PASC, prompting growing interest 
in adrenal disruption as a contributing factor (Klein et al., 2023; 
Marques et al., 2023; Porntharukchareon et al., 2024). Hypocortisolism 
can cause fatigue, and in Addison's disease or adrenal insufficiency, fa­
tigue can be debilitating (Garcia-Gonzalez et al., 2024). Furthermore, 
similar to PASC, patients with myalgia encephalomyelitis/chronic fa­
tigue syndrome (ME/CFS) who experience extreme fatigue after phys­
ical activity that was not previously challenging (Berardi et al., 2026) 
exhibit hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) dysfunction (Woo et al., 
2026; Zhang and Wang, 2024).
Animal experiments have established that SARS-CoV-2 infection or 
antigen injection reduces serum corticosterone/cortisol concentrations 
and, more importantly, brain concentrations of these hormones. Frank 
et al. reported that 9 days after ICM S1, rats had lower brain cortico­
sterone concentrations than vehicle controls (Frank et al., 2024). Fischer 
et al. found that cortisol levels in the cerebrospinal fluid of African green 
monkeys were reduced 18 weeks after SARS-CoV-2 injection (Fischer 
et al., 2025). Here, we show that 18 days post-S1, sedentary rats 
exhibit a trend toward lower hypothalamic corticosterone levels than 
sedentary vehicle controls.
Several clinical studies failed to detect low basal cortisol levels in 
blood or saliva among PASC patients, leading some to conclude that 
hypocortisolism plays no role in PASC (Klein et al., 2023; Peter et al., 
2025; Quach et al., 2025; Troscher et al., 2024). However, it is 
feasible that brain cortisol, rather than blood cortisol, plays a role by 
sustaining neuroinflammation and reducing motivated, rewarding 
behavior (Yu et al., 2015). VWR activates reward neurocircuitry, and 
evidence suggests that D1/D3 receptors and corticosterone mediate 
motivation for VWR (Ebada et al., 2016).
4.5. Physical activity, neuroinflammation, and brain corticosterone
Regular physical exercise is a potent modulator of neuro­
inflammation across multiple contexts (Alves et al., 2025; Connolly 
et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2021). Furthermore, VWR is 
sufficient to improve outcomes in models of inflammatory disease, 
including multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and stroke (Aoyagi 
et al., 2025; Courel-Ibanez et al., 2026; Kinoshita et al., 2026; Zhang 
et al., 2026).
Physical activity could affect PASC-associated neuroinflammation 
and symptoms in numerous ways. We previously reported that S1 re­
duces corticosterone levels across multiple brain regions at 7 and 9 days 
post-treatment (Frank et al., 2024). Here, we demonstrate that the 
S1-associated reduction in brain corticosterone is diminished 18 days 
post-S1 in sedentary rats, whereas in VWR rats, S1 elevates brain 
corticosterone.

a

disruption 
of 
glucocorticoid-mediated anti-inflammatory effects that is reversed by 
exercise. VWR may alter glucocorticoid receptor distribution in the 
hypothalamus, thereby facilitating greater corticosterone-mediated 
anti-inflammatory effects. Moreover, exercise may alter the activity or 
availability of 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1, an intracel­
lular enzyme that converts inactive cortisone to active cortisol (Seckl, 
1997). This study was not designed to unravel mechanisms. Future 
work is needed to clarify the contribution of corticosterone in the brain 
to the regulation of neuroinflammation, physical activity, and diurnal 
activity cycles.
4.6. Limitations
This study has limitations. We tested only male rats, and evidence 
suggests that the neural mechanisms underlying reduced home-cage 
activity after LPS differ between males and females (Alencar et al., 
2026). Further, mechanistic interpretations and clinical extensions of 
our results, including exercise intolerance, dopaminergic and gluco­
corticoid involvement, and activity 11β-HSD1, are speculative. Future 
studies are encouraged to build on our findings with direct mechanistic 
manipulations. Additionally, euthanasia in mice and rats (pentobar­
bital) can affect neuroinflammatory endpoints. Finally, while our find­
ings have implications for PASC, the animals were never infected with 
SARS-CoV-2. Nonetheless, SARS-CoV-2 antigens, including S1, are pre­
sent in blood, are immunogenic, and produce long-lasting changes in 
gene expression indicative of microglial priming and activation, as well 
as reduced or disrupted diurnal wheel-running behavior.
4.7. Clinical implications
Several groups are developing exercise training methods as a therapy 
for PASC (Cavigli et al., 2022; Jie Chee and Dalan, 2023). Rocha et al. 
report a protective effect of continuous physical activity among PASC 
patients with musculoskeletal, neurological, and respiratory impacts 
(Rocha et al., 2023). Centorbi et al. surveyed 1004 participants aged 40 
or older and reported that those who engage in regular physical activity 
are better protected against PASC symptoms (Centorbi et al., 2024). 
Additionally, Sarmento et al. found lower levels of fatigue, chest pain, 
and dizziness among individuals with PASC symptoms who walked 5000 
steps or more the previous day (Sarmento et al., 2025). However, a key 
challenge in implementing exercise therapies is overcoming exercise 
intolerance in PASC patients. The present evidence suggests that exer­
cise might alleviate some symptoms of PASC by mitigating neuro­
inflammatory processes and may inform investigations into effective 
interventions designed to reduce the brain-mediated symptoms of PASC.
