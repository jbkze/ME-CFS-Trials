## Summary

Researchers at the US National Institutes of Health tested spinal fluid from 16 people with ME/CFS, 34 with long COVID, 32 with Parkinson's disease (included for comparison, since it's a known noradrenaline-system disease), and 40 healthy volunteers, looking at two brain chemical messengers: noradrenaline (which drives alertness and the body's 'fight or flight' response) and dopamine. People with ME/CFS had clearly lower noradrenaline activity than healthy people — even lower than the long-COVID and Parkinson's groups — while their dopamine levels were normal. This noradrenaline shortfall was linked to weaker hand-grip endurance, worse general health, and was even lower in those who experience post-exertional malaise (PEM, a crash after activity).

## Why this matters

Identifies a specific, measurable brain-chemical shortfall in ME/CFS, distinct from a general 'stress' response, and ties it directly to muscle fatigability and symptom severity. The authors suggest it may happen because making noradrenaline costs cells more energy than an ME/CFS-affected 'energy crisis' can supply — offering a concrete, testable mechanism and a possible new drug target (boosting noradrenaline signalling).
