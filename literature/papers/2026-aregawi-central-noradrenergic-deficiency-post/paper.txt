Central noradrenergic deficiency in 
post-infectious chronic fatigue: neurobehavioral 
correlates
Lillian Aregawi,1 Brian Walitt,1 Patti Sullivan,2 Gina Norato,3 Rohit Ninan Benjamin1
and 
David S. Goldstein2
Fatigue, brain fog and post-exertional malaise are major features of post-infectious myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome and post- 
acute sequelae of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2. To date, no specific neurotransmitter abnormalities have been found in either 
condition. We examined the possibility of central catecholaminergic involvement and clinical correlates in post-infectious myalgic encephalomyeli­
tis/chronic fatigue syndrome and post-acute sequelae of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 groups compared to healthy volunteers and, 
as positive controls, Parkinson’s disease patients. In an observational, cross-sectional cohort study conducted at the National Institutes of Health 
Clinical Center we measured CSF levels of catecholamines and metabolites in the four groups and assessed correlations with neurobehavioral mea­
sures. CSF indices of the central Norepinephrine Pathway (norepinephrine + 3,4-dihydroxyphenylglycol + 3-methoxy-4-hydroxyphenylglycol) and 
Dopamine Pathway (dopamine + 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid + homovanillic acid) were measured and related to patient-recorded outcomes and 
physiological assessments. Mean values for Norepinephrine Pathway activity (in pmol/mL) were lower in the post-infectious myalgic encephalomy­
elitis/chronic fatigue syndrome, post-acute sequelae of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2, and Parkinson’s disease groups compared to 
the healthy volunteer cohort (post-infectious myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (−15.9, 95% confidence interval [−26.1, −5.7], P  
= 0.0006); post-acute sequelae of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (−9.62, [−17.9, −1.4], P = 0.015); Parkinson’s disease (−19.4, 
[−27.5, −11.3], P < 0.0001)). Post-acute sequelae of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 participants with post-exertional malaise 
had evidence of central noradrenergic deficiency compared to healthy volunteers (−18.3 [−31.3, −5.3], P = 0.0018). The post-infectious myalgic 
encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome and post-acute sequelae of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 groups did not differ 
from the healthy group in values for the Dopamine Pathway index. Across all participants, Norepinephrine Pathway activity correlated positively 
with handgrip duration and general health and negatively with fatigue. We conclude that post-infectious myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue 
syndrome and post-acute sequelae of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 feature a specific central neurotransmitter pattern involving 
noradrenergic but not dopaminergic deficiency. The noradrenergic abnormality is associated with major symptoms such as post-exertional malaise.

Graphical Abstract
Introduction
Post-Infectious myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syn­
drome (PI-ME/CFS) and post-acute sequelae of severe acute re­
spiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (PASC, also known as Long 
COVID) are significant current public health challenges.1,2 Both 
conditions are defined by specific clinical diagnostic criteria and 
share several common features. These include a history of prior in­
fection, similar characteristic symptoms, and the absence of a clear 
mechanistic explanation or definable biomarkers.
PI-ME/CFS is characterized by fatigue, malaise, and cognitive 
difficulties that worsen significantly after physical or mental exer­
tion, a phenomenon known as post-exertional malaise. PASC pa­
tients can also have post-exertional malaise, suggesting a potential 
common underlying mechanism.
Measurements of CSF concentrations of catecholamines and their 
metabolites provide information about central dopaminergic and 
noradrenergic systems.3 In particular, central norepinephrine defi­
ciency is reflected by low CSF levels of 3,4-dihydroxyphenylglycol 
(DHPG), the main neuronal metabolite of norepinephrine, and of 
3-methoxy-4-hydroxyphenylglycol (MHPG), the main end-product 
of central norepinephrine metabolism; while central dopamine defi­
ciency is reflected by low CSF levels of 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic 
acid (DOPAC), the main neuronal metabolite of dopamine, and 
of homovanillic acid (HVA), the main end-product of central dopa­
mine metabolism (see Fig. 1 concept diagram).
We recently reported that PI-ME/CFS entails decreased CSF le­
vels of DOPAC and DHPG, as well as of the catecholamine precur­
sor DOPA4,5; however, no previous report has described CSF levels 
of MHPG or HVA in PI-ME/CFS or PASC.

In this study, we explored whether PI-ME/CFS or PASC cohorts 
demonstrate common central catecholaminergic abnormalities. 
Healthy volunteers served as negative controls and Parkinson’s dis­
ease patients as positive controls, since Parkinson’s disease patients 
exhibit both central noradrenergic and dopaminergic deficiencies.3
To analyse the neurochemical data, we devised, validated, and ap­
plied novel indices of central dopaminergic and noradrenergic path­
way activities. For the former, we used the sum of DA + DOPAC +  
HVA, and for the latter, NE + DHPG + MHPG (Fig. 1). If index 
levels differed as a function of subject group, we analysed correlation 
matrices of neurochemical data versus patient-reported indices of 
neurobehavioral and physiological functions and overall health.
One may ask, would it not be sufficient to report on CSF norepin­
ephrine alone as the most direct measure of central noradrenergic ac­
tivity, since circulating norepinephrine does not pass through the 
blood-brain barrier (BBB)? Although it is correct that there is an effi­
cient BBB for circulating catecholamines,6 this does not mean that 
CSF norepinephrine straightforwardly reflects ‘activity’ in the sense 
of exocytotic release in response to pathway traffic. This is because 
most of the released norepinephrine is recycled by reuptake into the 
nerve terminals and metabolized before the norepinephrine reaches 
the CSF space. For instance, if there were decreased activity of the 
cell membrane norepinephrine transporter (NET), CSF norepineph­
rine would overestimate the rate of exocytosis. Simultaneous measure­
ment of DHPG (the main neuronal metabolite of NE) avoids this sort 
of misinterpretation. Thus, in humans, treatment with atomoxetine, 
which attenuates NET activity, increases CSF norepinephrine but 
also increases the CSF NE/DHPG ratio,7 indicating that the increase 
in CSF norepinephrine is at least partly the result of decreased neur­
onal reuptake into the cytoplasm and consequently decreased avail­
ability of norepinephrine for metabolism by monoamine oxidase to 
form DHPG. If there were an actual increase in exocytosis, then 
both norepinephrine and DHPG would be increased in the extracel­
lular fluid.8 By the same reasoning, if there were a decrease in exocyt­
osis, then CSF levels of norepinephrine and DHPG would both be 
decreased.9It is also important to recognize that the main determinant 
of norepinephrine turnover is not exocytosis (with escape of reuptake) 
but leakage of vesicular norepinephrine into the cytoplasm.10 This 
leakage occurs continuously, relatively independently of exocytosis. 
This is why, if one graphs CSF DHPG versus norepinephrine, the 
y-intercept value is above the origin.11 In the present report, by 
Norepinephrine Pathway ‘activity’ we do not mean exocytotic release 
but the turnover of norepinephrine, which under steady-state condi­
tions is balanced by synthesis. CSF norepinephrine, DHPG and 
MHPG provide related but distinctive and complementary informa­
tion about the overall status of the central noradrenergic system, just 
as CSF dopamine, DOPAC, and HVA provide related but distinctive 
information about the central dopaminergic system.3
As will be seen, in this study, we analysed values for numerous 
dependent behavioural and neurochemical variables that theoretic­
ally are related to each other but also are distinct. This multiplicity 
enabled reaching strong conclusions by convergence—but at the 
cost of rather daunting complexity. We wished to summarize the 
neurochemical information by validating and applying overall indi­
ces of central dopaminergic and noradrenergic function that readers 
could comprehend. Since the chosen indices were novel, we in­
cluded data from positive controls (a Parkinson’s disease group 3) 
and negative controls (a healthy volunteer group).
Materials and methods
Ethics statement
All the subjects in this study had participated in clinical research 
protocols approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of 
the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke at 
the National Institutes of Health (NIH) or by the NIH Central 
IRB (Supplementary Table 1). Written informed consent had 
been obtained before any procedures were done.
Study design
This was a retrospective analysis of observational data from cohorts 
of selected patients with PI-ME/CFS, PASC, or Parkinson’s disease 
who had been evaluated at the NIH Clinical Center.

Selection criteria
PI-ME/CFS
Inclusion required participants to have been previously healthy, fol­
lowed by evidence of infection within the past 5 years and ongoing 
severe fatigue accompanied by post-exertional malaise. Participants 
were screened through eligibility interviews and medical record re­
views, and their data were included for analysis after an adjudication 
process. The selection criteria and outcomes for this protocol 
(NCT02669212) were previously published.1
PASC
Data from two ongoing protocols (NCT04573062 for PASC, 
NCT04564287 for PASC with neurological symptoms (Neuro- 
PASC)) were analysed. The patients were required to have a 
positive SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid test and incomplete recovery 
with ongoing symptoms after the acute illness. Similar to the 
PI-ME/CFS group, participants were screened through eligibility 
interviews and medical record reviews. Adjudication for data inclu­
sion required consensus among three board-certified physicians.
Parkinson’s disease
Parkinson’s disease patients had been referred for evaluation by the 
Autonomic Medicine Section of the Division of Intramural 
Research, National Institute of Neurological Disorders and 
Stroke 
(NINDS) 
under 
NIH 
protocols 
NCT00059033, 
NCT00775853, NCT03104725, or NCT03648905. There was 
no recruitment through advertisement. For each participant, med­
ical documentation and a referral letter from their physician were 
reviewed, eligibility was determined by the Principal Investigator, 
and the participants were prioritized for inpatient or outpatient 
evaluation.
Healthy volunteers had been studied as inpatients at the NIH 
Clinical Center under several intramural NINDS clinical research 
protocols (Supplementary Table 1).
Lumbar puncture
Participants underwent lumbar puncture either directly or under 
fluoroscopic guidance. CSF was collected into pre-chilled 1.6 cc 
plastic tubes, placed immediately in dry ice, and stored at or below 
−80°C until thawed for assay.3
Norepinephrine and dopamine 
pathway indices
We simultaneously measured CSF levels of norepinephrine and its 
metabolites DHPG and MHPG, and of dopamine and its metabo­
lites DOPAC and HVA by liquid chromatography with series post- 
column electrochemical detection, as described previously by our 
group.3
We used the sum of CSF norepinephrine + DHPG + MHPG 
(referred to here as the Norepinephrine Pathway) as an index of 
central noradrenergic function and CSF dopamine + DOPAC +  
HVA (referred to as the Dopamine Pathway) as an index of central 
dopaminergic function (Fig. 1).
Clinical outcome measures
Questionnaires
The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information 
System (PROMIS) is a validated assessment tool evaluating various 
dimensions of health status and well-being (Fatigue, Pain Intensity, 
Depression, Anxiety, Sleep Disturbances, Pain Interference, Pain 
Behaviour, Global Health, Sleep-Related Impairments).12 Test re­
sults utilize a T-score metric with a mean of 50 and a standard de­
viation of 10. Higher scores indicate more of the measured item. 
PROMIS Global Health is split into Physical and Mental Health 
components.
The Short Form-36 (SF-36) is a self-reported assessment of qual­
ity of life that has been validated across various patient popula­
tions.13 The SF-36 has eight domains—physical functioning, 
physical role limitations, social functioning, emotional role limita­
tions, bodily pain, vitality, general health and mental health. 
These entries are combined into two components, physical and 
mental. Higher scores indicate fewer health complaints.
The Polysymptomatic Distress (PSD) Scale is a self-administered 
instrument that assesses the location and severity of pain and ten­
derness on a person’s body and can be used to evaluate fibromyal­
gia.14 The PSD provides a total score along with two sub-scores, 
symptom severity and widespread pain. Higher scores indicate 
greater reported pain.
The McGill Pain Questionnaire asks patients to pick from vari­
ous 20-word descriptors of their pain.15 Higher scores indicate 
more pain.
The Multiple Ability Self-Report Questionnaire (MASQ) exam­
ines five cognitive domains—language, visuo-perceptual, verbal 
memory, spatial memory, and attention.16 Higher scores indicate 
greater cognitive difficulties.
The Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI) is a self-reported 
20-item instrument that measures fatigue across the following 
subscales—general fatigue, physical fatigue, reduced activity, re­
duced motivation, and mental fatigue. Higher scores indicate great­
er fatigue.
The Patient Health Questionnaire-15 (PHQ-15) is a validated 
self-administered instrument to identify somatic symptoms, symp­
tom severity, and potential somatisation.17 The PHQ-15 gives a sin­
gle total score, with a higher score indicating more severe somatic 
symptoms.
The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is a self-administered 
questionnaire evaluating sleep quality over a one-month period.18
Higher scores indicate worse sleep quality.
The Neuropathic Pain Scale (NPS) is a validated self- 
administered scale used to measure and assess neuropathic pain.19
Higher scores indicate greater neuropathic pain.
Neuropsychological testing
The following neuropsychological assessments were administered 
by a trained psychometrist:
The Controlled Oral Word Association Test (COWAT) assesses 
verbal fluency by cueing patients to spontaneously produce words 
belonging to a given category or starting with a certain letter.20
Scores are given as T-scores, with a mean of 50 and a standard de­
viation of 10. Higher scores indicate better performance.

The Brief Visual Memory Test-Revised (BVMT) has partici­
pants memorize and reproduce a list of designs.21 In the BVMT 
Delayed Recall (BVMT-DR), participants recall previously memor­
ized designs after a 25-minute delay. Scores are given as T-scores, 
with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10. Higher scores in­
dicate better performance.
The Hopkins Verbal Learning Test-Revised (HVLT) has parti­
cipants memorize and reproduce a list of words.22 In the HVLT 
Delayed Recall (HVLT-DR), the person must recall the list of pre­
viously memorized words. Scores are given as T-scores, with a mean 
of 50 and a standard deviation of 10. Higher scores indicate better 
performance.
The Effort-Expenditure for Rewards Task (EefRT)23 examines 
participant task-related fatigue, effort expenditure, and sensitivity 
to rewards.
Grip strength
Grip strength, a measure that assesses overall health and fatigabil­
ity,5 was assessed in each hand using a dynamometer. To calculate 
maximum grip force, participants underwent two trials in which 
they gripped with maximal possible force for 5 s while seated. 
Maximum grip force was averaged across both trials. After the max­
imum grip force was calculated, the participants were instructed to 
grip the dynamometer again until the force achieved fell below 50% 
of the maximum grip strength.
Post-exertional malaise qualitative interview
All PASC participants underwent a qualitative interview to assess 
for post-exertional malaise.24 As required by the most recent diag­
nostic criteria for ME/CFS,25 all PI-ME/CFS participants were 
confirmed to have post-exertional malaise as a symptom.
Orthostatic measurements
Orthostatic blood pressure changes were assessed through measure­
ments of systolic and diastolic blood pressure by a brachial cuff with 
the patient lying supine and then standing for 5 min. Orthostatic 
hypotension was defined by a decrease in systolic pressure of 
≥ 20 mmHg or in diastolic pressure of≥ 10 mmHg.
The occurrence of excessive orthostatic tachycardia, as is 
found in postural tachycardia syndrome, was examined using a mo­
torized tilt table in protocols NCT02669212 and NCT0457306. 
Participants were tilted head-up at a 70-degree angle. An increase 
in heart rate of ≥30 BPM or to ≥120 BPM at 10 min of tilting 
was considered indicative of postural tachycardia syndrome.
Data analysis and statistics
For each subject group, means, standard deviations, medians and 
ranges of values were calculated for age, body mass index (BMI) 
and elapsed time from infection. Race, ethnicity and birth sex 
were summarized by numbers and percentages for the healthy vol­
unteer group and the PI-ME/CFS, PASC, and Parkinson’s disease 
groups. One-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted 
on numerical variables. Significant ANOVAs were then examined 
using Tukey’s post hoc test. Chi-squared tests of independence 
were run on categorical variables to assess differences in frequencies 
across groups.
Correlation coefficients were calculated among the neurochem­
ical and neurobehavioral variables in each group using Spearman 
pair-wise correlations at 95% confidence. Correlations were done 
separately for the following groups: (1) PI/ME-CFS, PASC and 
healthy volunteers and (2) PI/ME-CFS and PASC.
Statistical analyses used R version 4.4.1 for Windows (R 
Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Results 
were given as (estimate [95% confidence interval], P-value). No cor­
rection for multiple comparisons was applied across the various 
statistical measurements. There was no sample size calculation, as 
this was not the primary analysis for the protocols. Figures were 
generated using the ggplot2 package.26
Results
Participant characteristics
The groups were similar in sex composition and body mass indices 
(Table 1). The Parkinson’s disease group was older than the other 
groups, with a mean age of 64.1 years (SD 10.6; ≈<0.0001 by 
Tukey’s test), and the healthy volunteer group was older than the 
PI-ME/CFS group (49.2 versus 38.7 years; P = 0.027). The 
PI-ME/CFS group had roughly double the elapsed time since infec­
tion compared to the PASC group (33.2 versus 16.6 months; P <  
0.0001 by t-test).
CSF catecholamines and metabolites 
levels
Individual values and group mean levels of CSF catecholamines and 
metabolites by group are shown in Fig. 2.
Central norepinephrine and dopamine pathways
Across the healthy volunteer, PI-ME/CFS, PASC, and Parkinson’s 
disease cohorts, values for indices of the Norepinephrine and 
Dopamine Pathways varied as a function of group (F = 14.22, 
df = 3, P < 0.0001; F = 17.61, df = 3, P < 0.0001; Fig. 2A, B). The 
PI-ME/CFS, PASC, and Parkinson’s disease groups had lower 
mean Norepinephrine Pathway activity than did the healthy volun­
teer group (PI-ME/CFS: −15.9 [−26.1, −5.7], P = 0.00055; PASC: 
−9.62 [−17.9, −1.4], P = 0.015; Parkinson’s disease: −19.4 [−27.5, 
−11.3], P < 0.0001; Fig. 2A). The PASC and PI-ME/CFS groups 
did not differ in Norepinephrine Pathway indices. Mean 
Norepinephrine Pathway activity in the PI-ME/CFS group did 
not differ from the Parkinson’s disease group. The Parkinson’s dis­
ease group had lower levels of Norepinephrine Pathway activity 
than did the PASC group (P = 0.017 by Tukey’s test).
CSF MHPG levels were lower in the PI-ME/CFS (12.6 [4.6, 
20.6], P = 0.00044), PASC (7.8 [1.4, 14.3], P = 0.01035), and 
Parkinson’s disease (16.0 [9.7, 22.3], P < 0.0001) groups compared 
to the healthy volunteer group.
When the PASC group was divided into two subgroups, one 
with and one without post-exertional malaise, the subgroup with 
post-exertional malaise had lower values for Norepinephrine 
Pathway activity than did the healthy volunteer group (−18.3

Clinical correlates of norepinephrine 
pathway activity
All participants
Across all subjects (N = 63), individual values for Norepinephrine 
Pathway activity correlated positively with handgrip duration (right 
hand: ρ = 0.54 [0.32, 0.70], P < 0.0001, left hand: ρ = 0.40 [0.15, 
0.60], P = 0.0027; Supplementary Table 2). Norepinephrine 
Pathway activity also correlated positively with PROMIS mental 
and physical health scores (ρ = 0.33; ρ = 0.38) and physical func­
tioning, physical role limitations, social functioning, vitality, gen­
eral health, and mental and physical component sub-scores of the 
SF-36 (correlation coefficients 0.32–0.45, Supplementary Table 2).
Statistically significant negative correlations between values for the 
Norepinephrine Pathway index and neurobehavioral measures are 
also shown in Supplementary Table 2. The main negative correla­
tions were for the PSD scale, some PROMIS measures, and the MFI.
PI-ME/CFS and PASC
Among the PI-ME/CFS and PASC patients considered as a single 
group (n = 38), Norepinephrine Pathway activity correlated posi­
tively with handgrip duration (right hand: ρ = 0.62 [0.35, 0.80], 
P = 0.00011; left hand: ρ = 0.48 [0.15, 0.71], P = 0.0058; 
Table 2). Norepinephrine Pathway activity also correlated positive­
ly with the social functioning, vitality, and general health sub-scores 
of the SF-36, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.36 to 0.45 
(Table 2).
Values for the Norepinephrine Pathway index correlated nega­
tively with general fatigue and mental fatigue sub-scores of the 
MFI, the emotional role limitations sub-score of the SF-36, and 
the PROMIS fatigue score, with correlation coefficients ranging 
from −0.43 to −0.36 (Table 2).
Orthostatic measurements
Among the PI-ME/CFS and PASC patients considered as a single 
group, Norepinephrine Pathway index values were unrelated to the 
frequencies of orthostatic hypotension (P = 0.56) or excessive 
orthostatic tachycardia (P = 0.35). Within the PI-ME/CFS and 
PASC groups, there were too few instances of orthostatic hypoten­
sion or excessive orthostatic tachycardia to conduct meaningful cal­
culations of correlation coefficients.

Effects of medications
We reviewed all the medications that patients were on and identi­
fied the participants who were on a ‘Query’ medication at the 
time the CSF was obtained. By Query we mean a tricyclic of any 
sort (including cyclobenzaprine, the chemical structure of which is 
tricyclic), an amphetamine, venlafaxine, or duloxetine. There were 
10 patients on a Query drug (Supplementary File 1). The Query sub­
group had higher NE/DHPG ratios than did the non-Query sub­
group and healthy volunteers (Supplementary Fig. 1). Both the 
Query subgroup and the non-Query subgroup had lower mean va­
lues for the index of central Norepinephrine Pathway activity (CSF 
NE + DHPG + MHPG) than did the healthy volunteers.
Discussion
The present data indicate that PI-ME/CFS and PASC have in com­
mon a central neurochemical abnormality—noradrenergic defi­
ciency. This abnormality seems to be neurotransmitter-specific, 
since we found no evidence for central dopaminergic deficiency 
in either condition. Low activity of the Norepinephrine Pathway 
was associated with decreased scores on general health indices, such 
as physical and mental functioning, vitality and with decreased motor 
scores such as shortened handgrip duration. In the PI-ME/CFS and 
PASC groups, Norepinephrine Pathway activity did not correlate 
with other symptoms such as pain, altered cognition, decreased mem­
ory, anxiety, or depression. Within the PASC group, Norepinephrine 
Pathway activity was associated with post-exertional malaise.
MHPG is the main end-product of norepinephrine metabolism 
in the brain and is the predominant analyte in the index 
of Norepinephrine Pathway activity, CSF norepinephrine + 
DHPG + MHPG. We observed decreased CSF MHPG levels in 
both PI-ME/CFS and PASC compared to healthy volunteers. 
Low CSF MHPG levels have been reported in patients with pure 
autonomic failure27 or multiple system atrophy,28 but CSF 
MHPG has not been reported previously in PI-ME/CFS or PASC.
Various forms of autonomic dysfunction have been noted in 
PI-ME/CFS29-31 and PASC;32-34 however, we found low frequen­
cies of both orthostatic hypotension and excessive orthostatic 
tachycardia in these cohorts considered as a single group.
What could explain central noradrenergic but not dopaminergic 
deficiency in PI-ME/CFS and PASC? Norepinephrine synthesis re­
quires vesicular uptake of dopamine from the cytoplasm, because 
dopamine-beta-hydroxylase, the enzyme that catalyses the conver­
sion of dopamine to norepinephrine, is localized to the vesicles 
(Fig. 1), and vesicular uptake and retention of catecholamines rely 
heavily on an ATP-dependent proton pump. In contrast, steps in

When we compared the subgroups of patients who were on or 
were not on a Query drug at the time of CSF sampling, the 
Query drug subgroup had higher NE/DHPG ratios than did the

would expect from the drugs inhibiting neuronal reuptake of nor­

Limitations
This study involved relatively small cohorts studied in a highly com­
prehensive manner at a single centre. Generalisability of the results 
to the broader populations of PI-ME/CFS and PASC patients is 
unknown.
CSF levels of catecholamines and their metabolites vary exten­
sively across individuals. In this study, the CSF samples were col­
lected and processed immediately in a standardized way, 
minimising variability.
While the PI-ME/CFS cohort was very well defined,4 eligibility 
of the PASC participants was based on neurologic symptoms, and 
symptomatic assessments are subject to biases. To minimize 
influences of such biases, personnel assaying and analysing the 
CSF data were strictly blinded as to the symptomatic indices until 
after data tabulation.
There is no clear standard of measurement for post-exertional 
malaise. We assessed post-exertional malaise using a validated semi- 
structured qualitative interview that queried symptoms at baseline 
and after a cardiac stress test.24
Association does not imply causation, and the present results 
should not be taken to imply that central noradrenergic deficiency 
is causally related to the neurobehavioral outcome measures. 
Evaluating causation could be done by examining the effects of ma­
nipulations of central norepinephrine release, reuptake, or metabol­
ism on neurobehavioral measures.
We did not systematically control the timing of CSF collection. 
Our group reported previously on the circadian rhythm of CSF 
NE.35 During waking hours, there is relatively little change. 
Moreover, the central noradrenergic index we used was dominated 
by CSF MHPG, and there is no diurnal pattern of CSF MHPG 
levels.36
Summary and implications
The results of this study provide support for a specific neurotrans­
mitter abnormality, central noradrenergic deficiency, as a shared 
feature of PI-ME/CFS and PASC. In these disorders, values for a 
measure of central noradrenergic deficiency (CSF norepinephrine 
+ DHPG + MHPG, referred to as the Norepinephrine Pathway) 
were related to several patient-reported indices of overall health, 
the duration of handgrip, and post-exertional malaise. Our findings 
have implications for potential treatments of these forms of 
infection-associated chronic illness, depending on whether future 
research confirms that central noradrenergic deficiency in these con­
ditions is pathophysiologically significant.
