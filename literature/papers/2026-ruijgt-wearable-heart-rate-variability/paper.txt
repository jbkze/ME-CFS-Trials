Abstract
Objectives  Patients with long coronavirus disease (COVID) experience disabling fatigue, autonomic dysfunction, reduced 
exercise capacity and post-exertional malaise (PEM). Heart rate variability (HRV) can evaluate autonomic function and 
monitor overexertion, potentially helping to mitigate PEM. This study aimed to use continuous multi-day HRV recordings 
to monitor overexertion and study autonomic function in long COVID.
Method  Heart rate and HRV were continuously measured in 121 patients with long COVID (43 ± 11 years, 32% male) 
and 21 healthy controls (42 ± 13 years, 48% male), with daily life activities tracked in a logbook. Participants underwent a 
(sub)maximal cardiopulmonary exercise test to determine heart rate at the first ventilatory threshold (VT1) to study HRV 
responses to exercise at different intensities.
Results and Discussion  HRV was lower in patients with long COVID compared with healthy controls during various daily 
activities and sleep (p = 0.027). Across all exercise intensities surrounding the VT1, HRV remained lower for 24 h in patients 
compared with controls (p = 0.010). Nighttime HRV decreased with intense exercise and longer durations in patients with long 
COVID (p = 0.018), indicative of exercise-induced diurnal disturbances of the autonomic nervous system in long COVID.
Conclusion  Heart rate variability, assessed by wearables, suggests autonomic dysfunction in patients with long COVID. 
The delayed recovery of the sympathovagal balance after exercise close to and above VT1, suggests that the risk of PEM 
rises above VT1.
Application  These results confirm the applicability of wearables to assess autonomic function and manage overexertion in 
patients with long COVID.
1  Introduction
Long coronavirus disease (COVID) is associated with sev-
eral symptoms, including severe fatigue, a lower exercise 
capacity and unrefreshing sleep. Other commonly reported 
manifestations include autonomic dysfunction, such as pos-
tural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) and post-
exertional malaise (PEM), which is the worsening of symp-
toms after physical or mental exertion above an unknown 
patient-dependent threshold [1–4]. Many patients experience 
PEM following physical or mental exertion during daily life 
activities, leading to temporary worsening of symptoms and 
reduced functional capacity [1, 5]. Autonomic dysfunction is 
implicated in the pathophysiology, although the exact cause 
remains unknown [6–8]. Suggested interventions for patients 
with long COVID involve heart rate pacing and keeping 
exercise intensity below the risk zone for PEM, which is 
not precisely known. Also, triggers for PEM induction dif-
fer between patients [6, 9]. Since PEM is patient-dependent, 
a personalised approach in prescribing exercise and moni-
toring overexertion and recovery during daily activities is 
needed to improve patient well-being [5].

Key Points 
What is already known on this topic
Patients with long COVID often experience fatigue, 
autonomic dysfunction and post-exertional malaise 
(PEM). HRV can be used as a non-invasive tool to meas-
ure autonomic function and recovery. Some evidence 
suggests lower HRV in patients with long COVID, but 
multi-day measurements are scarce.
What this study adds
This study demonstrates that continuous HRV monitor-
ing through wearables can effectively identify overexer-
tion and autonomic dysfunction during daily activities in 
patients with long COVID. Patients with long COVID 
have lower heart rate variability during sleep, and HRV 
remained significantly lower for a longer period after 
moderate-to-heavy exercise, that is generally associated 
with the induction of post-exertional malaise.
How this study might affect research, practice or policy
This study supports the use of wearables for assessing 
autonomic function and overexertion in daily life, help-
ing patients with long COVID in pacing daily activities 
to mitigate symptoms of post-exertional malaise. HRV 
tracking after exercise suggests that exercise near or 
above VT1 provides an elevated risk for PEM. Sports 
physicians and physiotherapists can incorporate HRV 
biofeedback measures into pacing advice to patients. 
Additional research is needed to further investigate the 
effect of such an intervention.
measure of autonomic function [8, 10–12]. It represents the 
dynamic modulation of heart rate by the sympathetic and 
parasympathetic branches of the autonomic nervous sys-
tem. A commonly used time-domain measure is the root 
mean square of successive differences between RR-inter-
vals (RMSSD), which primarily reflects short-term, vagally 
mediated fluctuations in heart rate [13, 14]. While rMSSD 
is sometimes discussed in the context of sympathovagal bal-
ance, it most directly reflects parasympathetic modulation 
[12, 13]. Higher HRV is generally associated with greater 
autonomic flexibility and better recovery, although substan-
tial inter-individual variability exists, partly related to age 
and sex [15–17]. Lower HRV is linked to reduced parasym-
pathetic activity and increased physiological stress, making 
it a useful marker for autonomic function and recovery [18].
Patients with long COVID show mixed findings regard-
ing heart rate variability (HRV). Some studies report lower 
HRV at rest or during sleep [8, 19], while others report higher 
HRV [20, 21], highlighting inconsistent findings across the 
literature. A recent systematic review confirmed that lower 
HRV is often reported in long COVID, but highlighted poor 
methodological quality, including some studies using very 
short measurement durations of only 1 min and lack of 
assessment in relation to specific daily life activities or sleep 
[22]. While these findings suggest autonomic dysfunction, 
characterised by diminished parasympathetic activity [8, 19], 
more studies are needed to study HRV in patients with long 
COVID.
Post-exertional malaise is a prominent feature in long 
COVID and is also commonly observed in patients with 
myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/
CFS) [23]. After exercise, HRV in patients with ME/CFS 
remained lower, while HRV increased in healthy controls 
[23], illustrating how autonomic dysfunction may relate 
to PEM. While these findings may provide relevant physi-
ological context, the mechanisms underlying PEM in long 
COVID are unknown. Accordingly, the exact role of HRV 
in the pathophysiology of PEM in long COVID remains 
unclear.
Exercise intensity and duration significantly modulate the 
responses of the (para)sympathetic autonomic nervous sys-
tem during exercise [24]. The parasympathetic nervous sys-
tem is typically more active at rest and during low-intensity 
exercise, while sympathetic activity increases during high-
intensity or prolonged exercise near or above VT1 [25]. How 
heart rate variability responds to acute exercise, and what the 
recovery responses after exercise are in patients with long 
COVID is, however, currently unknown.
Gaining a better insight into HRV responses during sleep, 
daily life activities and the recovery after moderate-to-heavy 
exercise will not only provide more fundamental insights 
into the pathophysiology of PEM and long COVID but 
also help patients with structuring daily activities and pre-
vent overexertion and PEM. Therefore, this study aimed to 
investigate HRV over multiple days, during sleep, daily life 
activities and recovery after exercise near or above VT1 in 
patients with long COVID.
2  Methods
2.1  Participants
This retrospective study included patients with long COVID 
and healthy controls referred to a sports and exercise medi-
cine clinic (DeSportarts, Utrecht, the Netherlands) between 
1 January and 11 September 2024, who met the inclusion 
and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria for patients were 
(1) minimal one SaRS-CoV-2 infection, as diagnosed by 
a general practitioner and (2) being treated by a general 
practitioner for long COVID-related symptoms, including 
severe fatigue, brain fog, sleep disturbances and particularly 

Wearable Heart Rate Variability in Long COVID
post-exertional malaise. Control participants could have had 
a SaRS-CoV-2 infection but did not have long COVID symp-
toms. Exclusion criteria for the control group were chronic 
illnesses and related medication use or being an elite athlete.
Patients included in this retrospective study were receiv-
ing routine, medically specialised movement care at the 
sports and exercise medicine clinic (DeSportarts, Utrecht, 
the Netherlands). Current optimal treatment to avoid PEM 
in patients with long COVID includes avoiding overexertion 
and, as such, patients with long COVID were instructed, as 
part of routine clinical care, to avoid exercise above the heart 
rate at the first ventilatory threshold (VT1), using commer-
cially available devices such as Garmin or Fitbit monitors 
to track heart rate. It is important to emphasize that pacing 
is a broader concept that encompasses more than heart rate 
limits; it also includes activity planning, scheduled rest and 
prioritization. In this study, we focused specifically on heart 
rate-based pacing as one component of these broader pacing 
strategies, with the aim of strictly controlling exercise inten-
sity. Heart rate pacing was not advised in the control group.
The medical ethical committee of the Amsterdam UMC 
waived ethical oversight (2025.0092) in accordance with 
local legislation, as the heart rate variability (HRV) moni-
toring was part of standard care in a sports medical practice 
with extensive experience using HRV in patients and top 
athletes. The study design, involving the use of HRV devices 
for 3–7 days, was considered part of routine clinical practice. 
The study was deemed not to fall under the scope of the 
Dutch Research Involving Human Subjects Act by the Medi-
cal  Ethics Committee of Amsterdam UMC. Local approval 
was obtained from DeSportarts Utrecht. All data presented 
here were derived from individuals who consented to pro-
vide their data for research purposes on the Firstbeat appli-
cation. Before data analysis, participants were asked again 
for explicit consent to anonymous data analysis for the pur-
poses of this research. No identifiable personal information 
was used during the analysis phase, ensuring participants’ 
privacy in accordance with ethical and regulatory standards 
for minimal-risk research.
2.2  Patient and Public Involvement
Patients were not directly involved in the recruitment or con-
duct of the study, but their input was included during the 
development phase to refine the study design and ensure rel-
evance to patient experiences and priorities. We held informal 
meetings with patients and their representatives and consulted 
them on the usability and practical challenges of heart rate-
guided pacing, as well as the use of smartwatches for heart 
rate variability measurements and pacing strategies. We inte-
grated their experiences into the interpretation of our data, and 
practical applicability of our results for real patients.
2.3  Determination of Exercise Intensity Domains 
and Patient Severity
There is currently no scientific consensus about PEM induc-
tion, but anecdotal, clinical evidence suggests that the first 
ventilatory threshold (VT1) might provide some indications 
of such a risk zone [26]. VT1 reflects the point where the 
body begins to accumulate low levels of lactate in the blood, 
and marks the limit between aerobic and anaerobic exercise.
The first ventilatory threshold (VT1) was determined in 
a submaximal exercise test on a bike, 3–6 weeks prior to 
the HRV assessment. Owing to the retrospective design, the 
timing of the CPET relative to HRV assessment was not 
standardized. Termination criteria were pre-determined in 
consultation with the participant. If serious PEM symptoms 
were anticipated, the test was terminated after reaching the 
first ventilatory threshold (VT1), which was the case for 
most patients with long COVID. Otherwise, the test contin-
ued until the second ventilatory threshold (VT2) or maximal 
exhaustion.
The exercise test began with 2 min of rest on the ergome-
ter followed by a 3-min baseline cycling period, followed by 
a ramped, linear increase in power output at a rate of 10–20 
W/min in patients, 30W/min for healthy women and 40W/
min for healthy men. Participants were instructed to main-
tain their cadence above 60 revolutions/min. Throughout the 
test, electrocardiogram (ECG), heart rate, blood pressure, ­O2 
saturation levels and Borg exertion levels were monitored. 
Pulmonary gas exchange and ventilation were measured on 
a breath-by-breath basis (Cosmed Quark CPET; Cosmed, 
Rome, Italy), and displayed during the test. VT1 was deter-
mined using the validated V-slope and ventilatory equivalent 
methods ( ̇VE/̇VO2) and was assessed by two experts [27]. 
Heart rate at the first ventilatory threshold was defined as 
the start of the patient-specific PEM high risk zone for pac-
ing strategies.
Exploratory post hoc analyses allowed us to classify 
patients with long COVID based on daily life impairments 
into mild and moderately impaired patients. We reasoned 
that approximately 15 ml ­O2/kg/min is needed for daily 
life activities [28], and that patients with a VT1 below this 
threshold may struggle more with daily tasks, as these would 
already bring them into the PEM risk zone. Patients with 
VT1 values ≥ 15 ml ­O2/kg/min were classified as mildly 
impaired, and patients with VT1 values < 15 ml ­O2/kg/min 
were classified as moderately impaired.
2.4  Heart Rate Variability Assessments and Data 
Analysis
All participants were instructed to wear a Bodyguard 3 
device (Firstbeat, Finland), a clinical grade ECG-based 
HRV wearable, to measure heart rate variability (HRV). The

device was attached using electrodes and placed beneath the 
right collarbone and below the left chest (CardinalHealth, 
Kendall, 57 × 34 mm; Fig. 1A). The Bodyguard uses a 1 
channel ECG and measures RR-intervals with 1-ms inter-
vals. Participants were instructed to wear the device for 5–7 
consecutive days, excluding water contact (shower, bath 
etc.). Collected data were uploaded anonymously into the 
Firstbeat cloud. However, some participants removed the 
device earlier owing to personal reasons, such as vacations 
or skin irritation from the electrodes, resulting in an actual 
wear time of 3–7 days. Participants documented their daily 
activities in a logbook using the Firstbeat Life app. Retro-
spectively, these activities were further analysed and clas-
sified into strict domains: sleep, eating, travel, relaxation, 
work, walking, exercise, household chores, reading, relaxing 
exercises, getting ready and watching TV. Many of these 
categories were already available as standard options in the 
Firstbeat Life app, but additional refinement allowed for a 
more precise characterization of participants’ daily behav-
iours. HRV RMSSD, averaged over a 5-min periods by the 
Firstbeat software, was used for the analysis of daily life 
activities. HRV data for each category was averaged per 
participant across all measurement days to provide a per-
sonal, averaged HRV per daily life activity. This approach 
was chosen to allow a direct link between logbook recorded 
activities and HRV values calculated by Firstbeat, making 
it readily applicable in real-world settings. We calculated 
the daily variability in HRV as the standard deviation per 
participant for the different measurement days, using data 
collected between waking time and 20:00 in the evening.
For the HRV during recovery after exercise analysis, data 
analysis was conducted using Matlab R2023b. RR-intervals 
were filtered with a slightly adjusted version of HRVTool 
(v.1.07, https://​marcu​svoll​mer.​github.​io/​HRV/). In short, a 
threshold-based beat correction algorithm was applied, using 
a 0.20 s threshold corresponding to the ‘medium-strong’ 
filter of the Kubios software. RR-intervals deviating by more 
than 0.20 s from the local average (median of 30 surrounding 
RR-intervals) were corrected using cubic spline interpola-
tion. This threshold was adjusted on the basis of mean heart 
rate over a 20-s period; i.e. a heart rate of 120 bpm resulted 
in a filter threshold of 0.1 s. HRV was calculated using the 
root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) from 
300 RR-intervals. Heart rate was averaged over 20-s periods. 
A typical figure of HRV and HR throughout the day in both 
groups is shown in Fig. 1B.
2.4.1  Heart Rate Variability During Recovery After Exercise
Exercise intensities were categorised into three levels: mild 
(80% ≥ HR < 90% of VT1), moderate (90% ≥ HR < 100% 
of VT1) and intense (HR ≥ VT1). For every hour after 
exercise completion (up to 24 h), a 15-min average HRV 
was computed. Exercise was defined as maintaining HR 
above the predefined intensity for ≥ 20 min, and this was 
often accompanied by a comment in the diary. While it is 
unlikely that psychological factors such as stress, pain or 
fear would sustain for such periods, we do not exclude that 
cognitive exertion could have contributed in some instances 
[29]. Relative short segments of 15 min were used to capture 
transient post-exercise autonomic changes, which may have 
been masked by averaging over a full hour. The total time 
spent in each exercise level during the day was classified as 
short (< 20 min), medium (20–60 min) and long (> 60 min). 
Because not all healthy controls underwent a submaximal 
exercise test, VT1 was estimated using 75% of age-predicted 
maximal heart rate in 12 out of 21 participants (57%). In the 
subset of participants who completed the CPET (N = 9), VT1 
occurred at a median of 0.80 of predicted maximal heart rate 
(interquartile range (IQR) 0.14), which falls within the limits 
of agreement and is consistent with the reference threshold 
of 0.75 [30].
Additionally, for each participant, the highest nighttime 
HRV across all measurement nights was used as a refer-
ence value. Similar to the concept of resting heart rate, we 
reasoned that the highest HRV value is the best value that 
participants could reach during the measurement period. We 
do not know exactly what activities were performed during 
the day and how this would impact HRV during the follow-
ing night in patients with long COVID and, as such, average 
of baseline HRV was not used. The percentile difference for 
each night relative to this reference value was then calcu-
lated to evaluate the impact of exercise-induced stress on 
nighttime HRV.
2.5  Statistical Analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.4.1). 
Descriptive data were reported as mean ± standard devia-
tion, unless noted otherwise. Normality was checked using 
Shapiro–Wilk tests and visually inspected with Q–Q plots. 
Significance was accepted at p < 0.05.
Differences in daily-life HRV (RMSSD) were analysed 
using a non-parametric rank-based regression model (R-fit), 
with group (control versus long COVID) as the main fac-
tor, and age and sex included as covariates owing to their 
established associations with HRV (Supplementary Fig. 1). 
The normally distributed heart rate data were analysed using 
a linear model with the same covariates. Effect sizes were 
expressed as the raw mean differences (unstandardized coef-
ficients) between both groups, derived from the respective 
models.
Differences in post-exercise HRV recovery were analysed 
using a rank-transformed mixed linear model to account for 
repeated measures, with severity group (control, mildly 
impaired, moderately impaired), time (recovery hours) and

at VT1) as fixed effects, and age and sex as covariates.

relative change in nighttime HRV compared with each 
participant’s best night. Group, time spent per exercise inten-

fixed effects. Only absolute HRV measures were corrected

3  Results
This study included 121 patients with long COVID and 21 
control individuals (patient characteristics in Table 1). Long 
COVID participants had, on average, a higher BMI compared 
with the age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Patients 
with long COVID had a lower power output and oxygen 
uptake consumption ( ̇VO2) at VT1 compared with controls 
(p < 0.001). Patients with long COVID were classified as 
moderately impaired, with VT1 < 15 ml/kg/min (which is the 
oxygen consumption needed for daily life activities; N = 34, 
24% male, 43 ± 11 years, ̇VO2: 13.1 ± 1.2 15 ml/kg/min) or 
mildly impaired (VT1 ≥ 15 15 ml/kg/min; N = 87, 36% male, 
41 ± 10 years, ̇VO2: 21.6 ± 5.1 15 ml/kg/min).
3.1  Adverse Effects from the CPET
Three patients from this cohort experienced self-reported 
PEM for more than 5 days after the submaximal CPET. 
More than 5 days was considered an adverse event, because 
of the prolonged negative impact on daily life. These patients 
were not excluded from the study owing to the significant 
time between the exercise test and the HRV measurement.
3.2  HRV During Daily Life Activities
The daily variability in HRV RMSSD, calculated as the aver-
age standard deviation over the day, was lower in patients than 
in healthy controls. This indicates smaller fluctuations in HRV 
over the day in patients with long COVID. HRV RMSSD 
was lower in the long COVID group during sleep, eating, 
travelling and the uncategorised (and combined) activities 
(p = 0.005–0.027; Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1 and 2). 
Effect sizes and confidence intervals of the various daily life 
activities are mentioned in Table 2. Notably, 45% of reported 
activities were classified under this non-specific category, as 
these activities consisted of those that did not fall directly into 
one of the 12 predefined categories (e.g. shopping) or were a 
combination of two activities (e.g. eating in front of TV). A 
negative correlation between HR and HRV is shown in Sup-
plementary Fig. 2. Heart rate was significantly higher during 
sleep and the uncategorized activities in patients with long 
COVID, but no such difference was observed during eating 
or traveling. The daily life activities with higher absolute heart 
rates and subsequently lower HRV values, such as exercise, 
showed no significant group differences in HRV. 
3.3  Recovery of HRV After Exercise Cessation
As heart rate pacing advice is the current optimal treatment 
for patients to avoid PEM, we first studied how many patients 
recorded heart rates above the VT1. In 43% of patients with 
long COVID, we recorded heart rates above the anaerobic 
threshold during daily life activities. Thus, the adherence to 
the heart rate pacing advice given after the CPET was 57%. 
We stratified exercise into three exercise categories: mild 
(80% ≥ HR < 90% of VT1), moderate (90% ≥ HR < 100% of 
VT1) or intense (HR ≥ value at VT1) exercise, and we stud-
ied the recovery of the heart rate variability after exercise 
cessation. For this analysis, we made a subdivision between 
mildly and moderately impaired patients on the basis of oxy-
gen consumption at VT1. Figure 3 illustrates the time course 
of HRV during the recovery period after mild, moderate 
and intense exercise. During the hours following exercise, 
patients with long COVID showed significantly lower HRV 
values compared with healthy controls (p = 0.010), irre-
spective of exercise intensity, with no differences observed 
between mildly and moderately impaired patients. Com-
pared with controls, HRV reduced the most after intense 
exercise in the mildly affected patients (p < 0.001), but not 
in the moderately impaired patients where HRV was consist-
ently low. HRV increased relatively rapidly after exercise 
cessation in healthy controls (3–6 h post-exercise; Fig. 3 
blue markers). This recovery-associated increase in HRV 
(which is independent of heart rate) was significantly faster 
in healthy controls compared with mildly and moderately 
impaired patients with long COVID (p = 0.002), indicating 
delayed autonomic recovery in patients with long COVID.

HRV values during the subsequent night. The interaction 
between exercise duration and intensity (p = 0.010) suggests 
that nighttime HRV depends on the combined effect of these 
two factors.
To further illustrate these effects, we analysed the relative 
reduction in nighttime HRV (%) compared with the refer-
ence value, defined as the highest nighttime HRV by each 
participant (Fig. 4B). In the long COVID group, both exer-
cise intensity and duration significantly influenced the mag-
nitude of nighttime HRV reduction. Specifically, engaging in 
more than 60 min of exercise, regardless of intensity, led to a 
greater reduction in nighttime HRV compared with perform-
ing < 20 min of exercise at the same intensity (p = 0.033). 
Additionally, a significant difference was observed between 
mild and intense exercise when performed for < 60 min, with 
intense exercise causing a greater reduction in nighttime 
HRV (p = 0.038). The significant interaction effect between 
time and intensity (p = 0.018) underscores the combined 
impact of both factors on nighttime HRV recovery, particu-
larly in patients with long COVID. These results illustrate 
that exercise with increasing intensity and/or duration causes 
a delayed adjustment of HRV in patients with long COVID.
4  Discussion
The aim of this real-life study was to use HRV during daily 
life activities to study autonomic function and the recovery 
responses to exercise in patients with long COVID. The 
adherence to the heart rate pacing advice was substantial, 
57% of patients did not exceed their VT1 threshold dur-
ing the HRV measurement. We observed that HRV was 
lower in patients with long COVID in multiple daily life 
activities compared with healthy controls and did not fluc-
tuate as much during the day. HRV in patients with long 
COVID remained low during the recovery period after 
mild, moderate or intense exercise when compared with 
healthy controls. Lastly, long-lasting exercise around or 
above the VT1 resulted in a lower HRV during the subse-
quent night in patients with long COVID.
4.1  Autonomic Dysfunction in Long COVID
Our findings of a lower HRV in patients with long COVID 
are consistent with earlier research [16–18]. The lower HRV 
during sleep and many daily life activities, and fluctuations 
during the day in patients compared with healthy controls, 
suggest that patients with long COVID may experience

acute exercise modulates the response of the immune system

this links to the onset of post-exertional malaise in patients

4.2  Recovery After Moderate to Heavy Exercise
As the parasympathetic nervous system is more active dur-
ing lower-intensity exercise, the first ventilatory threshold 
has been described as a point where sympathetic activa-
tion begins to increase, as reflected in HRV dynamics 
[35]. Recovery of parasympathetic activity is typically 
delayed (up to 60–120 min) after exercise above VT1, even 
in highly-trained athletes [35]. In our real-life study, we 
observed that in healthy individuals, HRV values recover 
within a few hours after the completion of exercise, as 
the ratio of sympathetic and parasympathetic activities 
shift back towards mainly parasympathetic activities, 
which aids in recovery. However, this increase in HRV 
during the recovery of exercise was delayed for a signifi-
cantly longer period in patients with long COVID and 
did not return to the same values as in healthy controls, 
especially after exercise performed above VT1 and most 
notably in the moderately impaired group. This blunted 
HRV recovery after exercise suggests an insufficient or 
delayed response in the reduction in sympathetic nervous 
system activity, and/or an increase in the parasympathetic 
activity after exercise. These inabilities to return heart rate 
variability back to baseline values indicate that exercise 
recovery is impaired in patients with long COVID, par-
ticularly in those with moderate impairment, characterized 
by a VT1 below 15 ml ­O2/min/kg. This could contribute 
to prolonged physiological stress and a delayed recovery 
period. Above the VT1, the sympathetic activity and stress 
hormone concentrations (i.e. cortisol and catecholamine) 
typically increase [36], but we did not measure stress hor-
mone concentrations and time courses during and after 
high-intensity exercise in patients with long COVID.
Additionally, we observed lower night-time HRV in 
patients with long COVID following day-time exercise 
of long duration and/or moderate to high intensity. This 
suggests that moderate-to-intense physical activity may 
continue to suppress parasympathetic activity during sub-
sequent sleep, potentially resulting in reduced recovery.
Our findings, using real-life data from patients, provide 
further insights into the pathophysiology of post-exer-
tional malaise (PEM), in which exertion leads to worsen-
ing of symptoms and longer recovery times [1]. We do 
not know which processes underlie the induction of PEM 
after physical exercise. Since PEM symptoms are not lim-
ited to skeletal muscle-associated abnormalities [1], we 
provide evidence that acute exercise leaves an autonomic 
nervous system signature that could, at least in theory, 
initiate multi-organ symptomology including unrefreshing 
sleep and worsening of other symptoms. The underlying 
pathophysiology of PEM and the link with abnormal auto-
nomic nervous system activities is unknown, as we do not 
have recordings of PEM episodes in this real-life study. 
As such, more controlled studies need to be performed to 
provide a deepened mechanistic understanding of the role 
of the autonomic nervous system in PEM and the pathol-
ogy of long COVID.
Currently, no curative treatment for PEM is available, 
and patients are advised to limit exercise in the risk zone 
for PEM induction. This risk zone for PEM induction is 
currently unknown, but as HRV recovery responses were 
particularly disrupted with exercise of a longer duration 
(> 60 min) and at intensities near VT1, we hypothesize 
that a process occurring around VT1 is the initiator of 
PEM, and that as such, a heart rate above VT1 can be 
used as a surrogate measure for an elevated risk for PEM 
induction. Anecdotal evidence from patients with long 
COVID suggests that this risk for PEM induction is time- 
and patient-dependent, and is not only caused by muscular 
activities, but also by cognitive and mental exertion. As 
VT1 is mechanistically related to altered peripheral lactate 
production, this should be interpreted as a practical, sur-
rogate marker for the risk zone for PEM induction. VT1 
is also associated with increased sympathetic activity and 
release of cortisol and catecholamines (cortisol and (nor)
adrenaline) into the blood stream [36], but it is unknown 
whether the increase in sympathetic activity or the cir-
culating catecholamines, or other hormonal alterations 
provide a more mechanistic underpinning of PEM induc-
tion. Whether an abnormal physiological stress response 
or disturbed relaxation response underlies the acute onset 
of PEM deserves further study.
4.3  Clinical Implications
4.3.1  Biomedical Basis for Long COVID
Our findings provide further evidence of physiological 
alterations during and after physical exercise in patients 
with long COVID that are distinct from healthy controls. 
Autonomic dysfunction and impaired recovery in HRV in 
patients with long COVID contribute to our understanding 
of abnormal exercise responses in long COVID, and likely 
also patients with post-acute infectious syndrome (PAIS) or 
myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/
CFS) who also suffer from PEM.
4.3.2  Application of Wearables
Wearable devices can be used for monitoring HRV over mul-
tiple days in both patients and controls during sleep, daily 
life activities and post-exercise recovery. These devices 
can be used both in clinical practice and by patients them-
selves (preferably guided by experts) to identify stressful

moments, overexertion and recognize personalized patterns 
that impact recovery. By utilizing this data, clinicians can 
offer more tailored advice on pacing and exercise, ensuring 
that patients avoid overexertion and exacerbation of symp-
toms. This approach also empowers patients by providing 
real-time insights into their physical and recovery states, 
helping them make informed decisions about their activities 
and avoid actions that could lead to worsening symptoms.
4.3.3  Guidelines for Exercise Intensities
The impaired HRV recovery seen in patients with long 
COVID emphasises the importance of managing exercise 
intensities and duration carefully, as overexertion not only 
impacts immediate recovery, but can also disrupt sleep 
and subsequent recovery. Our results indicate that physical 
exercise with increasing intensity and duration significantly 
reduces subsequent HRV during the night, impairing heart 
rate variability recovery during sleep. Sports physicians 
can conduct a submaximal exercise test to determine the 
heart rate at VT1, which helps patients in providing a usable 
marker for the risk zone for PEM induction during physical 
exercise. We consider VT1 to represent a surrogate marker 
for the post-exertional malaise (PEM) risk zone, which is 
likely associated with a physiological process undergoing 
change around VT1, such as sympathetic nervous system 
activation, hormonal fluctuations or the release of stress-
related mediators. Cognitive or mental exertion may trigger 
a similar physiological alteration as during physical exertion 
above VT1. Thus, VT1 should be interpreted as a practical 
and measurable surrogate for the underlying biological risk 
zone for PEM induction. Continuous heart rate and HRV 
monitoring throughout the day can be done using weara-
bles, which help improve recovery and mitigate symptoms 
of post-exertional malaise.
4.4  Limitations
This real-life study using HRV monitors in patients has 
several limitations. Firstly, the real-life setting of this 
study meant that a strict experimental protocol could not 
be applied. A wide range of (combined) daily life activities 
were recorded in the logbook, which increased subject-to-
subject variability. Some activities were reported by only a 
small number of participants, limiting the statistical power. 
Current best treatment for patients with long COVID include 
the avoidance of PEM-inducing exercise, and as such, we 
have not induced PEM in a controlled manner in this study. 
The goal of heart rate pacing below VT1 was to prevent 
PEM. Without heart rate pacing, the exertion in the long 
COVID group might have been higher, leading to even more 
pronounced differences compared with healthy controls. 
In this retrospective field study, we could not control the 
timing of the exercise during the day, and the nature of the 
post-exercise period. Nonetheless, regardless of the inherent 
variability in the post-exercise time period, we observed lon-
gitudinal and cross-sectional differences in the HRV recov-
ery profiles. Future prospective and laboratory-controlled 
studies are required to validate these findings. We acknowl-
edge that orthostatic tachycardia is a common issue in long 
COVID and could influence heart rate measurements, par-
ticularly during upright activities. Lastly, we lacked infor-
mation on participants’ pre-disease exercise capacity and 
their COVID-related burden on quality of life, making it 
difficult to disentangle true disease-related impairment from 
pre-existing fitness levels, particularly in patients with mild 
and moderate impairment. Symptom questionnaires would 
have allowed us to correlate abnormal HRV recovery after 
exercise with PEM symptomology. The severity and time 
since the initial COVID-19 infection in long COVID par-
ticipants was unknown, which may have influenced HRV 
values. We acknowledge the relatively small control group 
with a completed exercise test. These potential limitations 
should be accounted for when interpreting heart rate vari-
ability in future research.
5  Conclusions
In this study, we provide evidence that heart rate variabil-
ity is lower during daily life activities in patients with long 
COVID and remains lower for longer after exercise. The 
continuous measurement of HRV provides an easy-to-use 
non-invasive method that may help limit physical overexer-
tion and prevent induction of PEM in patients with long 
COVID. HRV can be easily measured using wearables dur-
ing daily life activities in patients, and can also be used for 
future clinical intervention therapies on treating or reducing 
PEM in patients.
