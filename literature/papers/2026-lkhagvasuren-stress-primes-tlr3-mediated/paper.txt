A B S T R A C T
Background: This study examined the long-term effects of polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (poly I:C), a synthetic 
double-stranded RNA and Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) agonist, on behavioral and immune outcomes in chroni­
cally stressed mice.
Methods: Male C57BL/6J mice were exposed to 21 days of wet bedding stress followed by a poly I:C injection. 
Post viral fatigue syndrome (PVFS)-like symptoms were evaluated over 7 days post-injection using grip strength 
testing, the forced swim test, von Frey filament testing, the Morris water maze, the open field test, and the social 
interaction test. Body temperature and locomotor activity were continuously monitored via intraperitoneally 
implanted dataloggers. Serum concentrations of interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-10, and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 
(CXCL10) were quantified. In separate cohorts, minocycline (a microglial activation inhibitor) or RU486 (a 
glucocorticoid receptor antagonist) was administered prior to poly I:C injection.
Results: Following IP injection of poly I:C, body temperatures in both stressed and unstressed mice were 
significantly elevated, indicating a polyphasic febrile response. At 7 days post-injection, stressed mice treated 
with poly I:C exhibited persistent fatigue, mechanical allodynia, depressive-like behavior, impaired spatial 
memory, increased anxiety-like behavior, and reduced social interaction. Serum levels of IL-6 and CXCL10 
remained elevated and correlated with behavioral outcomes. Pretreatment with minocycline partially attenuated 
both the behavioral and immune responses, whereas RU486 pretreatment did not.
Conclusion: These findings demonstrate that TLR3-mediated systemic inflammation induced by poly I:C produces 
persistent, multi-domain PVFS-like symptoms in chronically stressed mice. The attenuation by minocycline 
implicates neuroinflammation as a possible mechanism.
Introduction
Post-viral fatigue syndrome (PVFS) is characterized by persistent 
fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, pain, and sleep disturbances that endure 
for months to years following recovery from acute viral infection 
(Komaroff & Lipkin, 2021). The phenomenon is not unique to any single 
pathogen. Historically, approximately 10–12% of patients recovering 
from Epstein-Barr virus, Ross River virus, and Coxiella burnetii infections 
developed chronic fatigue syndrome regardless of the specific infectious 
agent, with initial illness severity rather than psychological factors, 
predicting prolonged outcomes (Hickie et al., 2006). The COVID-19 
pandemic has brought renewed urgency to PVFS research, as the post- 
acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection, commonly termed long 
COVID, have emerged as a global health crisis affecting an estimated 65 
million individuals worldwide with more than 200 reported symptoms 
spanning multiple organ systems (Davis et al., 2023; Nalbandian et al., 
2021). The neuropsychiatric manifestations of long COVID are partic­
ularly prominent and persistent. Large-scale electronic health record 
analyses have revealed that approximately one-third of COVID-19 sur­
vivors received a neurological or psychiatric diagnosis within 6 months

The immune pathogenesis of COVID-19 involves the activation of 
innate immune receptors, including Toll-like receptors (TLR3, TLR7, 
TLR9), the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 
(NLRP3) inflammasome, and the C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 3 
(CXCR3) pathway, which collectively orchestrate the cytokine storm 
(Merad et al., 2022). Activation of these receptors engages downstream 
signaling cascades, including nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB), caspase-1, 
and Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/ 
STAT), culminating in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines 
such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and C-X-C 
motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10/IP-10), while concurrently 
impairing the type I interferon (IFN-I) response (Merad et al., 2022; 
Mehandru & Merad, 2022). Critically, immunological dysfunction has 
been shown to persist for at least 8 months following even mild-to- 
moderate SARS-CoV-2 infection, with elevated IFN-β, IFN-λ1, IL-6, and 
activated CD14⁺CD16⁺ monocytes constituting a persistent inflamma­
tory signature in long COVID patients (Phetsouphanh et al., 2022). 
Recent multi-omic profiling has confirmed that long COVID involves 
sustained activation of proinflammatory and immune exhaustion path­
ways, with IL-6 signaling remaining upregulated beyond 180 days post- 
infection (Aid et al., 2026).
Beyond the systemic inflammatory response, the central nervous 
system exhibits distinct pathological changes following viral infection. 
Even in the absence of direct neuroinvasion, peripheral inflammatory 
signals compromise blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity and activate 
resident microglia and astrocytes, which in turn secrete brain-specific 
pro-inflammatory cytokines (Vanderheiden & Klein, 2022). Seminal 
work has demonstrated that mild respiratory SARS-CoV-2 infection in­
duces white-matter-selective microglial reactivity, impaired hippocam­
pal neurogenesis, oligodendrocyte loss, and myelin damage persisting 7 
weeks post-infection, with elevated cerebrospinal fluid CCL11 identified 
as a key mediator (Fern´andez-Casta˜neda et al., 2022). Human brain 
organoid studies have further revealed a threefold increase in microglial 
phagocytosis of synapses following SARS-CoV-2 exposure, accompanied 
by transcriptomic profiles overlapping with neurodegenerative disor­
ders (Samudyata et al., 2022). This chronic neuroinflammation driven 
by activated microglia is hypothesized to underlie the fatigue, sleep 
disturbances, cognitive deficits, pain hypersensitivity, and depression 
that characterize long COVID (Dantzer et al., 2008; Dantzer & Kelley, 
2007).
Furthermore, clinical evidence indicates that pre-existing psycho­
logical distress substantially amplifies the risk of developing long 
COVID. A large prospective cohort study (n = 54,960) from the Nurses’ 
Health Study demonstrated that depression, anxiety, worry, perceived 
stress, and loneliness prior to SARS-CoV-2 infection were each inde­
pendently associated with a 32–46% increased risk of post-COVID 
conditions (Wang et al., 2022). A comprehensive meta-analysis of 113 
studies (n = 312,831) confirmed that depression and anxiety were 
significantly associated with long COVID risk (Engelmann et al., 2024). 
Complementary multinational data showed that patients with severe 
acute COVID-19 were 50–60% more likely to develop depression and 
anxiety persisting up to 16 months (Magnúsd´ottir et al., 2022). These 
clinical observations raise a mechanistic question: does chronic stress 
prior to viral infection prolong the cytokine storm-induced sickness 
response and thereby promote the transition from acute illness to 
persistent PVFS?
Polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (poly I:C) is a synthetic analog of 
double-stranded RNA that activates TLR3, inducing NF-κB-dependent 
production of type I interferons and pro-inflammatory cytokines, 
thereby mimicking the innate immune response to viral infection 
(Alexopoulou et al., 2001). In our previous study, intraperitoneal 
administration of poly I:C in rodents produced a well-characterized 
sickness 
behavior 
syndrome 
comprising 
fever, 
depressive-like 
behavior, and mechanical allodynia (Chijiwa et al., 2015). Poly I:C 
also caused reduced locomotor activity, anorexia, social withdrawal, 
and, accompanied by central neuroinflammation including elevated IL- 
1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the cortex and hippocampus, kynurenine pathway 
activation via indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase, and reduced brain-derived 
neurotrophic factor expression (Gibney et al., 2013; Dantzer et al., 
2008). Notably, prior chronic stress has been shown to moderate poly I: 
C–induced sickness behaviors, plasma cytokine responses (IL-6, IL-10), 
corticosterone secretion, and central monoamine activity, attenuating 
the measured immune response on the day of poly I:C administration 
(Gandhi et al., 2007).
Moreover, existing animal models of long COVID, including hamster 
and humanized mouse models, have demonstrated persistent neuro­
inflammation, microglial activation, and behavioral deficits following 
SARS-CoV-2 infection (Frere et al., 2022; Soung et al., 2022). However, 
these models require biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) facilities and live virus 
handling, substantially limiting their accessibility and throughput for 
mechanistic studies and therapeutic screening (Usai et al., 2023; Sch¨afer 
et al., 2024). There is thus a pressing need for practical, widely acces­
sible animal models that recapitulate the PVFS without requiring 
containment infrastructure. Our previous work using a TLR3 agonist in 
repeatedly stressed rats demonstrated robust and prolonged sickness 
behavior resembling PVFS symptoms (Chijiwa et al., 2015), suggesting 
that the interaction between chronic stress and TLR3-mediated immune 
activation may capture the core pathophysiology of PVFS.
Based on the convergence of clinical evidence implicating 
stress–immune interactions in long COVID, and the mechanistic plau­
sibility of TLR3-mediated neuroinflammation as a driver of persistent 
behavioral symptoms, we hypothesized that systemic inflammation 
induced by poly I:C in chronically stressed mice would produce persis­
tent PVFS-like symptoms, including fatigue, mechanical allodynia, 
depressive-like behavior, cognitive impairment, and social withdrawal, 
through a neuroinflammatory mechanism involving microglial activa­
tion. To test this hypothesis, we developed a two-hit model combining 
21 days of chronic wet bedding stress with a single intraperitoneal in­
jection of poly I:C in C57BL/6J mice. The primary aims of this study 
were: (1) to determine whether poly I:C induces persistent, multi- 
domain behavioral symptoms selectively in chronically stressed mice; 
(2) to characterize the systemic inflammatory profile (IL-6, IL-10, 
CXCL10) and its relationship to behavioral outcomes; and (3) to 
dissect the mechanistic involvement of microglial activation versus 
glucocorticoid receptor (GR) signaling through pharmacological pre­
treatment with minocycline and RU486, respectively.
Experimental procedures
Animals and ethics statement
Male C57BL/6J mice (6–7 weeks of age at arrival; n = 96) were 
obtained from Japan SLC, Hamamatsu, Japan. Mice were housed indi­
vidually in standard polycarbonate cages under specific pathogen-free 
(SPF) conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 8:00 AM) at 
a controlled ambient temperature of 23 ± 1◦C and relative humidity of 
50 ± 10%. Standard rodent chow and water were available ad libitum. 
Upon arrival, mice were allowed 7 days for acclimatization before any 
experimental procedures. All experiments were performed during the 
light phase (between 10:00 AM and 5:00 PM) unless otherwise specified. 
The total sample size was determined by power analysis based on effect 
sizes from our previous study (Chijiwa et al., 2015), with n = 5–10 mice 
per group.
All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the 
Guidelines for Animal Care and the Institute of Laboratory Animals and 
were approved by the Ethics Committee of Kyushu University (Approval 
No. A24-350–1). Every effort was made to minimize the number of an­
imals used and their suffering.

Surgery and monitoring of locomotor activity and body temperature
After acclimatization, mice were anesthetized with a combination of 
medetomidine (0.3 mg/kg), midazolam (4.0 mg/kg), and butorphanol 
(5.0 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally. Under aseptic conditions, a 
miniature datalogger (nano tag®; weight: 2.7 g, diameter: 17.9 mm; 
Muromachi Kikai Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was implanted via a 15-mm 
midline incision into the peritoneal cavity. This implantable device 
continuously monitors and records body temperature (Tb; ±0.1◦C res­
olution) and locomotor activity (La; accelerometer-based activity 
counts) according to a preprogrammed schedule at 5-min intervals 
throughout the experimental period. The surgical wound was closed 
with a 4–0 silk suture. Following surgery, mice received a single sub­
cutaneous injection of ampicillin sodium (100 mg/kg; Meiji Seika 
Pharma Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for prophylactic antibiosis and were 
placed on a warm pad (35◦C) until full recovery from anesthesia. Mice 
were allowed 7 days for postoperative recovery and daily handling 
before the onset of the stress paradigm.
Chemicals and reagents
Poly I:C (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, Cat. No. P1530) was 
dissolved in sterile, endotoxin-free physiological saline on the day of 
injection, and administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 4 mg/kg body 
weight in a volume of 10 mL/kg (Chijiwa et al., 2015). Control animals 
received an equivalent volume of sterile physiological saline. Minocy­
cline hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, Cat. No. 
M9511) was dissolved in sterile saline and administered intraperitone­
ally at a dose of 50 mg/kg (Henry et al., 2008; Kobayashi et al., 2013) 
once daily for 10 consecutive days prior to poly I:C injection. Mifepris­
tone (RU486; Sigma-Aldrich St. Louis, MO, USA, Cat. No. M8046) was 
dissolved in vehicle and administered at a 50 mg/kg (Chijiwa et al., 
2015) once daily for 10 consecutive days prior to poly I:C injection. 
Medetomidine hydrochloride (Domitor®), midazolam (Dormicum®), 
and butorphanol tartrate (Vetorphale®) were obtained from commercial 
veterinary suppliers.

Wet bedding stress
Chronic wet bedding stress was performed as described previously 
(Arima et al., 2017) with modifications. Mice assigned to the stress 
groups were exposed to wet bedding for 4 h per day (10:00 AM–2:00 
PM) for 21 consecutive days. Wet bedding was prepared by adding 
approximately 100 mL of room-temperature tap water (~23◦C) in a new 
cage, creating a shallow water layer approximately 5 mm deep sufficient 
to wet ventral fur without forcing the animal to swim. Following each 4- 
h stress session, mice were transferred to their home cages. Non-stressed 
control mice remained undisturbed in their home cages. Body weight 
was measured daily immediately before each stress session to monitor 
the physiological impact of chronic stress and to ensure animal welfare.
Physiological and biochemical assessments
Grip strength. Forelimb grip strength was measured as a proxy for 
muscular fatigue using a grip strength meter (RX-5; Aikoh Engineering, 
Nagoya, Japan) in the SPF experiment room (Deacon, 2013). Each 
mouse was held by the tail and allowed to grasp a wire mesh grid with its 
forelimbs. The mouse was then gently pulled horizontally at a constant 
speed until release. Peak force (in grams) was automatically recorded. 
Five consecutive trials were performed per mouse with 30-s inter-trial 
intervals, and the mean of the three highest values was used for anal­
ysis. Measurements were obtained at four time points: Day –22, Day −1, 
Day 1, and Day 7. All testing was performed between 10:00 AM and 5:00 
PM by an experimenter blinded to group assignment.
Von Frey test. Mechanical allodynia was assessed by measuring the 
paw withdrawal response frequency using calibrated Semmes-Weinstein 
monofilaments (Stoelting Co., Wood Dale, IL, USA) as described previ­
ously (Chaplan et al., 1994). Each mouse was placed individually in a 
transparent Plexiglas cubicle (9 × 9 × 14 cm) on a wire mesh platform 
elevated 30 cm above the bench surface and allowed 30 min to accli­
matize. A von Frey filament of predetermined force (1.4 g) was applied 
perpendicular to the mid-plantar surface of the left hind paw. The fila­
ment was applied 10 times with 5-s intervals, and the withdrawal fre­
quency was recorded. Measurements were obtained at the same four 
time points as grip strength testing by a blinded experimenter.
Circadian locomotor activity and body temperature. Body tem­
perature and locomotor activity were continuously recorded via the 
intraperitoneally implanted nano tag® dataloggers throughout the 
experimental period starting on Day −5. Recording was performed 
continuously from Day −
5 to Day 7, yielding 12 consecutive days of 
body temperature and locomotor activity data per animal. Within each 
cohort, all mice received their assigned injection (poly I:C or saline) on 
the same day (Day 0) and at the same time of day, such that the post- 
injection monitoring window was identical across groups. Data were 
sampled at 5-min intervals. For analysis of circadian locomotor activity, 
hourly cumulative activity counts were calculated for each day and 
visualized as heat-map actograms. Body temperature data were 
analyzed to detect febrile responses following poly I:C injection. Fever 
index was calculated as the cumulative area under the curve (AUC) of 
the change in baseline body temperature (a 1-hour average before in­
jection) between 2 and 8 h after injection.
Serum cytokine and chemokine measurements. Blood samples 
were collected via cardiac puncture under deep isoflurane anesthesia at 
the time of euthanasia (Day 7 post-injection). Blood was allowed to clot 
at room temperature for 30 min, centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 15 min at 
4◦C, and serum was stored at −
80◦C until analysis. Serum concentra­
tions of IL-6, IL-10, and CXCL10 were quantified using commercially 
available ELISA kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) according to 
the manufacturer’s instructions. All samples were assayed in duplicate.
Behavioral tests
All behavioral tests were conducted during the light phase (10:00 
AM–5:00 PM) in the SPF experimental room during Days 1–6 post- 
injection. Test order was consistent across groups, and apparatuses 
were cleaned with 70% ethanol between animals. All behavioral scoring 
was performed by an experimenter blinded to group assignment using 
video-tracking software (ANY-maze; Muromachi Kikai, Japan).
Open field test. General locomotor activity and anxiety-like 
behavior were assessed using the open field test (OFT). Each mouse 
was placed in the center of an open-field arena and allowed to explore 
freely for 10 min. The arena was virtually divided into a center zone 
(25% of total area) and peripheral/corner zones. Time spent in the 
center zone (%) and time spent in the corners (%) were quantified as 
indices of anxiety-related behavior; reduced center time and increased 
corner time were interpreted as increased anxiety.
Social interaction test. Social behavior was assessed using a one- 
trial social interaction test (SIT). Each subject mouse was placed in an 
arena containing a wire mesh enclosure housing a novel, unfamiliar 
stimulus mouse of the same strain, age, and sex. The subject mouse was 
allowed to explore freely for 10 min. Time spent in the interaction zone 
(defined as the area immediately surrounding the stimulus enclosure) 
was recorded as a percentage of total test time. Reduced interaction time 
was interpreted as social withdrawal.
Morris water maze test. Spatial learning and memory were assessed 
using the Morris water maze (MWM). The apparatus consisted of a cir­
cular pool (120 cm diameter, 45 cm height) filled with opaque water (23 
± 1◦C). A transparent escape platform (10 cm diameter) was submerged 
1 cm below the water surface in the target quadrant. During the 
acquisition phase (Days 1–4 post-injection), each mouse received 4 trials 
per day for 4 consecutive days. On each trial, the mouse was released 
from one of four start positions and allowed a maximum of 60 s to locate 
the hidden platform. Escape latency (s) was recorded. A probe trial 
(platform removed) was conducted on Day 5: the mouse swam freely for 
60 s, and time spent in the target quadrant (s) was used as an index of 
spatial memory retention.
Forced swim test. Depressive-like behavior was assessed using the 
forced swim test (FST) as described by Porsolt et al. (1977) and adapted 
for mice (Can et al., 2012). Each mouse was placed individually in a 
transparent cylindrical container (20 cm diameter × 25 cm height) filled 
with water (23 ± 1◦C) to a depth of 18 cm. Mice were forced to swim for 
10 min, and immobility duration (s) was scored during the 5-min in­
terval between minutes 1 and 6. Increased immobility time was inter­
preted as depressive-like behavior.
Pharmacological pretreatment experiments
To investigate the mechanistic involvement of microglial activation, 
a separate cohort of chronically stressed mice received minocycline 
pretreatment (50 mg/kg, IP) or saline once daily for 10 consecutive days 
immediately preceding poly I:C injection (Henry et al., 2008; Bassett 
et al., 2021). Minocycline crosses the blood–brain barrier and selectively 
inhibits M1 microglial polarization while sparing M2 anti-inflammatory 
polarization (Kobayashi et al., 2013; Garrido-Mesa et al., 2013).

Statistical analysis
All data are expressed as mean ± standard error mean (SEM) or 
presented as box-and-whisker plots showing individual data points. The 
primary analysis employed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) 
across the four experimental groups. Circadian locomotor activity was 
analyzed using a cumulative AUC of the total activity over the 12-hour 
period followed by one-way ANOVA. For repeated-measures data (body 
temperature, locomotor activity, grip strength and allodynia across time 
points, MWM acquisition), a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was 
used with time or time points as the within-subjects factor. When sig­
nificant main effects were detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were 
conducted using Tukey’s honestly significant difference test or Bonfer­
roni correction, as appropriate. The relationship between serum cyto­
kine concentrations and behavioral outcomes was assessed using 
Pearson correlation analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statisti­
cally significant. Significance levels are indicated as: *p < 0.05, **p <
0.01, ***p < 0.001. All statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi 
version 2.6 (jamovi.org) or SPSS version 21 (IBM, New York, USA).
Results
Poly I:C induces a polyphasic febrile response and acute locomotor 
suppression
The experimental timeline is depicted in Fig. 1a. To confirm that 
intraperitoneal injection of poly I:C (4 mg/kg) elicited a systemic innate 
immune response, core body temperature and locomotor activity were 
continuously monitored via intraperitoneally implanted dataloggers 
(Fig. 1b–d). Following the poly I:C injection on Day 0, daily locomotor 
activity during the dark period was suppressed in both poly I:C-treated 
groups (between subject effects: F3 = 4.41, p = 0.012, η2G = 0.106), 
with the Control/Poly I:C group exhibiting a significant reduction 
compared to the Control/Saline group (post hoc: p = 0.037; Fig. 1b). 
Saline-treated animals maintained stable activity levels throughout the 
monitoring period.
Poly I:C injection induced a prominent polyphasic febrile response in 
both the Control/Poly I:C and Stress/Poly I:C groups (between subject 
effects: F3 = 9.53, p < 0.001, η2G = 0.304), whereas saline-treated an­
imals maintained normal circadian temperature oscillations (Fig. 1c). 
Post hoc comparisons showed that both poly I:C-treated groups exhibi­
ted significantly elevated body temperatures compared to saline-treated 
controls (p = 0.028 and p = 0.014, respectively). The febrile response 
was quantified as the fever index (cumulative AUC of temperature 
change between 2 and 8 h post-injection). A one-way ANOVA revealed a 
highly significant overall group effect on fever index (F3 = 114.48, p <
0.001, η2G = 0.9; Fig. 1d). Post hoc comparisons confirmed that both 
poly I:C-treated groups exhibited significantly elevated fever indices 
compared to saline-treated controls (post hoc: both p < 0.001). These 
data confirm that poly I:C at 4 mg/kg reliably activated the TLR3- 
mediated innate immune pathway, producing a robust polyphasic 
fever consistent with systemic cytokine release (Chijiwa et al., 2015).
In addition to body temperature, body weight was monitored 
throughout the experimental period (Supplementary File 1). All groups 
showed progressive weight gain from before stress through Day 7 post- 
injection, with no statistically significant differences between groups at 
any time point. This indicates that the observed behavioral and immune 
phenotypes were not secondary to differential nutritional status, general 
health deterioration, or body weight loss.
Poly I:C induces persistent, multi-domain PVFS-like symptoms selectively 
in stressed mice
A comprehensive behavioral battery was administered over the 7- 
day post-injection period to assess multiple PVFS-relevant symptom 
domains (Fig. 2a–j).
Persistent fatigue. Forelimb grip strength was measured at four 
time points: Day –22, −1, 1, and 7 (Fig. 2a, n = 7–8). All four groups 
displayed comparable grip strength at baseline (before stress) and the 
grip strength was decreased in both poly I:C-treated groups (F3 = 5.19, p 
= 0.003, η2G = 0.154), with the Control/Poly I:C group exhibiting a 
significant decline compared to the Control/Saline and Stress/Saline 
groups (post hoc: p = 0.020 and p = 0.005, respectively). When

Poly I:C disrupts circadian locomotor activity in stressed mice
Circadian locomotor activity patterns were visualized using heat- 
map actograms generated from the continuous datalogger recordings 
(Fig. 3a–d).
In the Control/Saline group, control mice displayed clear circadian 
rhythmicity throughout the experimental period, with high locomotor 
activity (warm colors) concentrated during the dark phase and low ac­
tivity (cool colors) during the light phase (Fig. 3a). A similar pattern was 
observed in the other groups during the 5-day period before injection 
(Fig. 3b-d). However, a transient disruption of circadian activity was 
observed immediately following poly I:C injection on Day 0, with re­
covery of normal circadian patterns within several days (F3 = 3.151, p =
0.034). These disruptions persisted throughout the post-injection 
monitoring period in poly I:C treated and stress-exposed groups, 
whereas circadian rhythmicity was unaffected in the Control/Saline 
group, as a one-way ANOVA indicated significant group differences on 
Day 7 (F3 = 5.666, p = 0.04). This circadian rhythm disruption parallels 
the sleep and circadian rhythm disturbances identified as essential fea­
tures of long COVID (Merikanto et al., 2022; Livieratos et al., 2025).

To determine whether microglial activation contributed to the PVFS- 
like phenotype, a separate cohort of chronically stressed mice received 
minocycline pretreatment (50 mg/kg/day, IP, for 10 days) prior to poly 
I:C injection (Fig. 5a–d).
Minocycline pretreatment attenuated the poly I:C-induced grip 
strength decline in stressed mice (between subject effects: F3 = 5.21, p =
0.009, η2G = 0.259; Fig. 5a). The Minocycline + Stress/Poly I:C group 
showed significantly better preservation of grip strength compared to 
the Saline + Stress/Poly I:C group (post hoc: p = 0.02; Fig. 5b). 
Minocycline pretreatment also significantly reduced FST immobility 
duration in stressed mice receiving poly I:C (F3 = 7.78, p < 0.001; 
Fig. 5c), indicating attenuation of the depressive-like phenotype (post 
hoc: Minocycline + Stress/Poly I:C vs. Saline + Stress/Poly I:C, p =
0.004). Similarly, minocycline pretreatment increased the time per­
centage spent in the interaction area (F3 = 4.53, p = 0.015; Fig. 5d), 
suggesting improving social interaction (post hoc: Minocycline + Stress/ 
Poly I:C vs. Saline + Stress/Poly I:C, p = 0.01). Furthermore, minocy­
cline pretreatment significantly reduced the persistent elevation of 
serum IL-6 in the Stress/Poly I:C group (F3 = 3.43, p = 0.032; Fig. 5e), 
indicating reducing inflammation (post hoc: Minocycline + Stress/Poly 
I:C vs. Saline + Stress/Poly I:C, p = 0.029).
However, minocycline did not normalize the other behavioral end­
points to control levels, particularly increased allodynia, impaired 
spatial memory, and increased anxiety-like behaviors suggesting that 
additional mechanisms beyond microglial activation may contribute to 
the PVFS-like phenotype.

Discussion
The present study demonstrates that a single intraperitoneal injec­
tion of poly I:C in chronically stressed mice produces a persistent, multi- 
domain syndrome recapitulating core clinical characteristics of post- 
viral fatigue syndrome, including long COVID. At 7 days post- 
injection, beyond the expected resolution of acute sickness behavior, 
the Stress/Poly I:C group selectively exhibited persistent fatigue, allo­
dynia, depressive-like behavior, impaired spatial memory, reduced so­
cial interaction, disrupted circadian locomotor activity, and sustained 
elevation of serum IL-6 and CXCL10. Neither poly I:C alone nor chronic 
stress alone was sufficient to produce this multi-symptom phenotype, 
indicating that the interaction between chronic stress priming and TLR3- 
mediated immune activation is critical for the transition from acute 
sickness to persistent PVFS. The selective attenuation by minocycline, 
but not RU486, implicates microglial activation and neuroinflammation, 
rather than HPA axis–glucocorticoid signaling, as the primary mecha­
nistic driver.
The stress × poly I:C interaction is grounded in clinical evidence that 
pre-existing psychological distress amplifies long COVID risk by 32–46% 
(Wang et al., 2022; Engelmann et al., 2024). Chronic stress activates 
microglia and promotes monocyte trafficking to the brain (Wohleb et al., 
2013). Frank et al. (2024) recently demonstrated that SARS-CoV-2 spike 
protein produces protracted neuroinflammatory priming with exagger­
ated responses to subsequent immune challenge, suggesting a mecha­
nism directly analogous to the stress-mediated priming observed here. 
The present findings extend this literature by demonstrating that stress 
priming converts a normally self-limiting innate immune response into a 
persistent, multi-domain behavioral syndrome, PVFS.
The multi-domain behavioral phenotype distinguishes this model 
from single-endpoint sickness behavior paradigms. Long COVID patients 
present with concurrent fatigue, pain, cognitive dysfunction, mood 
disturbances, circadian disruption, and social withdrawal (Davis et al., 
2023; Komaroff & Lipkin, 2023). Our model recapitulates these do­
mains, and the significant correlations between cytokine concentrations 
and behavioral outcomes provide evidence that these symptoms reflect 
an integrated pathophysiological process driven by sustained systemic 
inflammation.
The persistent elevation of IL-6 and CXCL10 selectively in the Stress/ 
Poly I:C group is noteworthy. Acute poly I:C-induced cytokine responses 
typically resolve within 24–48 h in unstressed animals (Gibney et al., 
2013), yet chronic stress converted this transient response into a sus­
tained inflammatory state. Both cytokines are consistently elevated in 
long COVID patients beyond 6 months post-infection (Dornas et al., 
2025), with the IL-6–JAK/STAT signaling axis remaining activated for 
over 180 days (Aid et al., 2026).
The minocycline–RU486 pharmacological dissociation provides a 
specific insight. Minocycline, which crosses the BBB and selectively in­
hibits M1 microglial polarization (Kobayashi et al., 2013), partially 
rescued grip strength, substantially reduced FST immobility, restored 
social interaction, and attenuated IL-6 elevation, consistent with recent 
evidence that minocycline attenuates enduring neurological conse­
quences following systemic inflammation (Hosseini et al., 2024; Zou 
et al., 2025). Conversely, RU486 failed to improve any endpoint, 
consistent with evidence that GR signaling in neuroinflammation is 
context-dependent and can paradoxically enhance central inflammation 
under chronic stress (Taylor & Kokiko-Cochran, 2024; Meyer et al., 
2024). The partial attenuation by minocycline suggests that additional 
mechanisms contribute, potentially including peripheral immune cell 
trafficking, kynurenine pathway activation, or autonomic dysfunction 
(Dantzer et al., 2008; Wohleb et al., 2013).
The circadian disruption in the Stress/Poly I:C group is clinically 
relevant, as sleep and circadian disturbances are now recognized as 
essential features of long COVID (Merikanto et al., 2022; Livieratos 
et al., 2025). Pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-6, disrupt 
circadian clock gene oscillations in the hypothalamus and hippocampus 
(Cheng et al., 2024), providing a mechanistic link between the sustained 
cytokine elevation and circadian disruption in this model.
The present approach is best understood in relation to existing pre­
clinical models of post-acute viral sequelae. Live-virus models in Syrian 
hamsters (Frere et al., 2022) and susceptible or humanized mice (Soung 
et al., 2022) have been demonstrated to reproduce SARS-CoV-2–specific 
central cytokine induction, impaired hippocampal neurogenesis, and 
lasting systemic perturbations. Conversely, mild respiratory SARS-CoV- 
2 infection has been shown to produce white-matter–selective micro­
glial reactivity and myelin dysregulation (Fern´andez-Casta˜neda et al., 
2022), and human brain organoids exposed to SARS-CoV-2 have been 
observed to show increased microglial elimination of synapses 
(Samudyata et al., 2022). Although these systems capture virus-specific 
neuropathology with high construct validity, they require BSL-3 
containment, transgenic or humanized hosts, or adapted viral strains 
(Sch¨afer et al., 2024; Usai et al., 2023), and typically interrogate a single 
mechanistic endpoint. Component-based approaches have shown to 
reduce these constraints, i.e., administration of the SARS-CoV-2 S1 
subunit produces a protracted neuroinflammatory priming with exag­
gerated responses to subsequent immune challenges (Frank et al., 2024). 
This finding aligns mechanistically with the chronic-stress priming re­
ported in this study. The present model is distinguished by three fea­
tures. Firstly, it isolates the pathogen-independent, TLR3-driven 
component of post-viral biology, consistent with comparable rates of 
post-infective fatigue across diverse pathogens (Hickie et al., 2006). 
Secondly, it assays concurrent multi-domain symptomatology, including 
fatigue, mechanical allodynia, depressive-like behavior, spatial-memory 
impairment, circadian disruption, and social withdrawal, within a single 
design rather than an isolated readout. Thirdly, it incorporates chronic 
stress as a clinically grounded priming factor that independently ele­
vates the risk of long COVID (Wang et al., 2022; Engelmann et al., 2024). 
Its BSL-1 compatibility, reliance on commercially available reagents, 
and use of the well-characterized C57BL/6J background further 
enhance accessibility and reproducibility. The model complements, 
rather than replaces, virus-based systems, as it does not recapitulate 
viral tropism, immune evasion, or persistent viral reservoirs. Instead, it 
provides a tractable platform for dissecting the stress–inflammation 
interaction that drives the transition from acute sickness to persistent 
post-viral symptoms.
The findings open several translational directions. First, the model 
provides a platform for screening microglial-targeted therapeutics 
beyond minocycline, including PLX5622 (a CSF1R inhibitor that pre­
vents neuroinflammatory sequelae following systemic inflammation at 
low doses; Mein et al., 2023) and NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitors such 
as dapansutrile, which are advancing through clinical trials for neuro­
inflammatory conditions (Vande Walle & Lamkanfi, 2024). Second, the 
central role of stress priming suggests that stress-reduction interventions 
could serve as PVFS prophylaxis; cognitive behavioral therapy probably 
reduces long COVID fatigue (Zeraatkar et al., 2024), and pre-infection 
perceived stress predicts ongoing symptomatic COVID-19 (Fazekas 
et al., 2025). Third, the persistent IL-6 elevation provides a rationale

for testing anti-IL-6 agents (i.e., tocilizumab) or JAK inhibitors (e.g., 
baricitinib), which reduce acute COVID-19 mortality by 33% and may 
mitigate subsequent neuropsychiatric sequelae (Amstutz et al., 2025). 
This model could serve as a preclinical screening platform for such in­
terventions before clinical trials.
Several limitations should be acknowledged. Cross-sectional sam­
pling of serum cytokines and chemokines cannot resolve sustained, 
oscillatory, or delayed-secondary patterns. Peripheral cytokines rather 
than central neuroinflammatory markers were assessed. Central neuro­
inflammation was therefore inferred functionally, from the sensitivity of 
the phenotype to minocycline and from prior work demonstrating cen­
tral cytokine elevation after poly I:C (Gibney et al., 2013). Direct 
quantification of brain-region cytokines, microglial markers, and his­
tological indices of microglial activation is a priority for confirming the 
proposed neuroinflammatory mechanism. The seven-day follow-up does 
not establish whether the phenotype is truly chronic. Sequential 
behavioral testing during the same post-injection period cannot fully 
exclude carry-over effects. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that 
persistent fatigue can depress performance in the FST, OFT, MWM, and 
SIT independently of the affective, cognitive, and social processes that 
these tasks are taken to index. Consequently, the corresponding mea­
sures are best interpreted as a convergent syndrome rather than as 
dissociable constructs. As the behavioral tests and the terminal 
biochemical measures were obtained from the same animals, the 
behavioral and cytokine readouts are not statistically independent, and 
serial testing may have influenced the later assays. This design was 
adopted to reduce animal numbers and to permit within-subject longi­
tudinal analysis, with a fixed test order arranged from least to most 
stressful and inter-test recovery intervals to mitigate carry-over. 
Nevertheless, replication of the key endpoints in independent, single- 
assay cohorts is warranted. Repeated handling and cumulative novelty 
exposure may alter immobility, exploration, and spatial learning on later 
tests. Only male mice were used; sex differences in stress responses and 
neuroimmune function warrant future studies including female mice. 
Poly I:C is not a SARS-CoV-2 surrogate; the model recapitulates the post- 
viral inflammatory–behavioral phenotype rather than virus-specific 
tropism, immune evasion, or persistent viral reservoirs. Stimulation 
with the SARS-CoV-2 spike or S1 subunit, or with live virus, would be 
required to test virus-specific contributions and to extend these 
pathogen-independent findings (Frank et al., 2024). Minocycline has 
pleiotropic effects; more specific tools such as PLX5622 or conditional 
genetic approaches would strengthen mechanistic conclusions.
In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that TLR3-mediated 
systemic inflammation induced by poly I:C produces persistent, multi- 
domain PVFS-like symptoms in chronically stressed mice, recapitu­
lating core features of PVFS relevant to long COVID. The mechanistic 
dissociation between minocycline sensitivity and RU486 insensitivity 
suggests neuroinflammation rather than HPA axis dysregulation as a 
primary driver of PVFS-like pathology. This model provides a platform 
for investigating the transition from acute sickness behavior to PVFS and 
for screening candidate therapeutics, including microglial-targeted 
agents, NLRP3 inhibitors, and stress-reduction strategies.
