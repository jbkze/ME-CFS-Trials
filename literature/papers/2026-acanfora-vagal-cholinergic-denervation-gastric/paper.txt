Abstract 
 
Objectives 
Dysautonomia has been increasingly recognized as a key feature of Long-COVID-19, 
potentially contributing to persistent gastrointestinal and systemic symptoms. Reduced 
vagal activity observed in these patients may reflect structural damage to autonomic fibers. 
         
         

3 
 
We hypothesized that dysautonomic manifestations in Long-COVID-19 are associated with 
impaired cholinergic innervation of the gastric mucosa. 
 
Methods 
We conducted a case–control study including 12 patients with Long-COVID-19 and 8 
control subjects undergoing routine gastroscopy. Gastric mucosal biopsies were analyzed 
using immunohistochemistry with the pan-neuronal marker protein gene product 9.5 (PGP 
9.5) and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) as a marker of cholinergic fibers. Nerve fiber 
density was quantified in both fundus and antrum samples. 
 
Results 
Compared with controls, Long-COVID-19 patients exhibited a significant reduction in 
mucosal innervation density: 2.1 vs 3.9 nm/µm³ (p<0.01) in the fundus and 1.9 vs 3.9 
nm/µm³ (p<0.05) in the antrum. The reduction in cholinergic innervation was more 
pronounced in the fundus (p<0.01) andalso evident in the antrum (p=0.01). Gastric nerve 
density correlated with HRV parameters (LF/HF ratio: R=0.50, p<0.05) and NT-proBNP 
levels (R=0.52, p<0.01). 
 
Conclusions 
Patients with Long-COVID-19 exhibit reduced gastric mucosal cholinergic innervation. This 
structural autonomic impairment may represent an anatomical substrate underlying 
dysautonomia in Long-COVID-19.

Introduction 
Since the end of 2019, a coronavirus pandemic (SARS-CoV-2), responsible for severe 
acute respiratory syndrome, has spread worldwide. This pandemic, due to the spread of 
SARS-CoV-2, is similar to two previous human coronavirus (HCoV) outbreaks, acute and 
severe respiratory syndrome (SARS) and Middle Eastern respiratory syndrome (MERS) 
[1]. SARS-CoV-2 has spread far more quickly and is more contagious than the other two 
types of HCoVs. All these outbreaks are characterized by respiratory symptoms ranging 
from sore throat, cough, and dyspnea to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)[2]. In 
the acute phase, SARS-CoV-2 penetrates through a link with the angiotensin-converting 
enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptors[3]. In infected individuals, SARS-CoV-2 invasion is ubiquitous 
as ACE2 receptor is expressed in all organs and systems. 
There is consolidated evidence that SARS-CoV-2 mainly affects the respiratory and 
cardiovascular systems. Nevertheless, it can be associated with, neurological, and 
gastrointestinal symptoms [4]. The involvement of the nervous system (both central and 
peripheral) is common in patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and the 
finding of elevated plasmatic levels of proteins expressing neural damage such as 
neurofilament light chain protein (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAp), 
demonstrate the presence of  an impairment of the nervous system in COVID-19 patients 
[[5],[6]].Nervous system manifestations have been reported in approximately 37% of 
COVID-19 patients, often in the early stages of the disease and sometimes even before 
the occurrence of respiratory symptoms[7].Early symptoms include anosmia, ageusia, 
myalgia, cognitive fog, anxiety, headache, depression, hallucinations and other psychiatric 
         
         

5 
 
symptoms[8]. However, the pathophysiology of neurological manifestations in patients with 
COVID-19 remains controversial. 
Neurological symptoms have been linked to COVID-19 through various mechanisms, such 
as neuroinvasion, vascular inflammation, and the blood‒brain barrier breakdown. Some 
authors believe that neurological symptoms may be initiated by a direct damage caused by 
COVID-19 to the olfactory pathways and maintained by the centripetal spread of the 
disease to central nervous system structures. Alternatively, the virus may reach the central 
nervous system via the trigeminal nerve after coming into contact with the ocular 
conjunctiva[[9],[10]]. Other authors correlate the clinical manifestations of patients with 
COVID-19 and Long-COVID-19 to direct involvement of the vagus nerve (VN)[[6],[11],[12], 
[13]]. It is a mixed nerve with 20% efferent fibers and 80% afferent fibers and is an 
important component of the parasympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system 
(ANS)[14] that innervates the respiratory system, heart, digestive system, kidneys, 
bladder, uterus and testicles. Therefore, VN may represents a large route of virus 
translocation from periphery to the central nervous system.  
Long-COVID-19 refers to the signs and symptoms that continue or develop after the acute 
COVID-19 phase and is characterized by a proinflammatory, procoagulative state. Patients 
who have recovered from acute COVID-19 have a higher risk of rehospitalization, 
multiorgan dysfunction, and mortality [[15],[16]].  
In Long-COVID-19, a dysfunction of the ANS has been reported [11] that can possibly be 
accounted for some of the extrarespiratory late manifestations of SARS-CoV-2 infection 
[4]. Immunohistochemical analysis of autonomic innervation has been largely used in 
recent decades to study normal autonomic innervation of end organs and to characterize 
peripheral involvement of adrenergic and cholinergic systems in different pathological 
conditions in humans [[17],[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Due to the availability of tissues, most 
related work has been performed by processing skin samples[[17],[18]]. However, the 
         
         

6 
 
technique has been applied to study visceral autonomic innervation of epicardial adipose 
tissue and myocardium [[19], [20]] and of gastrointestinal mucosa[[21]]. 
To test the occurrence and extent of autonomic involvement in Long-COVID-19 patients, 
we performed a power spectral analysis of heart rate variability (HRV) via 24-hour 12-lead 
ECG recording in patients and controls. We also performed histological examination of the 
nerves in the mucosal layer of the gastrointestinal tract. We chose to study the stomach 
mucosa because this tissue can be safely acquired via standard biopsy techniques during 
routine endoscopy. We also hypothesize that there is a relationship between gastric 
mucosal nerve fiber density and the symptoms, procoagulative state and impairment of 
vagal activity associated with Long-COVID-19. 
Therefore, we will correlate autonomic nerve density with clinical characteristics and HRV 
parameters in Long-COVID-19 patients. 
 
Materials and Methods 
Patients 
We consecutively enrolled 12 patients admitted to our Department from 12 July 2021 to 23 
February 2024 with persistent symptoms, including dyspnea, fatigue, chest pain, joint pain, 
myalgia, cough, headache, loss of appetite, dyspepsia, nausea, vomiting and bloating 
following SARS-CoV-2 infection; symptoms persisted for more than 12 weeks, fulfilling the 
criteria for the diagnosis of  Long-COVID-19 syndrome[[22]]. Eight patients without 
previous SARS-CoV-2 infection who underwent screening gastroscopy for dyspepsia 
composed our control population (Controls).  All Long-COVID-19 and Controls patients 
had received 3 doses of BNT162b2/COMIRNATY vaccine. 
The exclusion criteria were as follows: permanent atrial fibrillation (PAF), ventricular 
ectopic beats >600/24 h, the presence of a pacemaker or implantable cardioverter 
         
         

7 
 
defibrillator, heart failure, clotting disorders, diabetes, kidney failure, liver failure, 
dysthyroidism, dysendocrinopathy, alcohol abuse, active cancer under chemotherapy, 
peripheral neuropathy or conditions potentially affecting the peripheral nervous system. 
This study was performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the 1975 
Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (22 
March 2021) of the San Francesco Hospital of Telese Terme (BN). All procedures 
complied with the ethical standards of institutional and national research. Informed consent 
was obtained from all participants included in the study. 
Study protocol 
All patients were clinically stable and in sinus rhythm. Patients had been on a stable 
pharmacological regimen for at least 2 weeks before hospital admission. Therefore, all 
patients were on optimal medical therapy at the time of admission. After admission, all 
patients provided their demographic data, medical history, vital signs, clinical and 
anthropometric data, neurologic examination results, blood chemistry test results, and 
HRV assessment via 24-h ECG, as previously described[13].  In short,  patients were 
applied a portable twelve-channel tape recorder, processed by a DM Software Inc. version 
12.5.0078a, (Diagnostic Monitoring Software P.O. Box 3109; 209 Kingsbury Grade, #3; 
Stateline, NV 89449, USA) for 24-h ECG recordings which were uploaded to the PC. After 
scanning, any artifacts were removed and the recordings were analyzed with the 24-h 
ECG software at a sampling frequency of 128 Hz. At admission, all patients underwent 
preparation of the skin, then self-adhesive electrodes were placed in the usual positions 
for twelve-lead ECG monitoring, and recording was started between 9:00 and 9:30 a.m.. 
During recording, patients were allowed to be standing or sitting next to their beds, while 
no other activities were allowed. The 24-h ECG monitoring software documented HRV 
parameters automatically as numerical data. Patients were considered eligible for the 
         
         

8 
 
study if their recordings had at least 12 h of analyzable intervals between consecutive R 
peaks. Furthermore, the recording period was analyzed if it included at least half of the 
night-time (from 00:00 a.m. through to 5:00 a.m.) and half of the daytime (from 7:30 a.m. 
through to 11:30 p.m.) [23]. The labelling of each beat as normal or aberrant was done 
according to recognition by the algorithm for tape analysis and based on an investigator’s 
check. Time-domain measures (SDNN, SDANN, ASDNN, rMSSD, pNN50) and frequency-
domain measures (VLF, LF, HF, LF/HF) were calculated for HRV assessment. Power 
spectral density (PSD) was estimated using the Blackman–Tukey method in all accepted 
segments after linear trend removal. PSD is a highly reproducible tool used to assess the 
functional balance between parasympathetic and sympathetic domains of the autonomic 
nervous system activity and to break down the total variation of a data series into its 
frequency components. The total power (TP) and the power in the very low frequency 
band (VLF, 0.01–0.04 Hz), low frequency band (LF, 0.04–0.15 Hz), and high frequency 
band (HF, 0.15–0.45 Hz) were then computed by numerical integration of the spectral 
density function. The analysis took into consideration only normalized TP, VLF, LF, and 
HF values which were expressed as natural logarithms (ln ms2) to minimize skewness. 
The LF/HF ratio was also calculated. Based on the ACC/AHA/ESC consensus [23], the 
following HRV parameters were defined: HF component, mainly reflecting the efferent 
vagal activity; LF component, which is mediated by efferent vagal and sympathetic activity; 
LF/HF ratio, a measure of sympatho/vagal balance; VLF component, which reflects 
neuroendocrine and thermoregulatory influences mediated mainly by the sympathetic 
system[[24], [25]]. The time-domain parameters, which are most commonly used, were 
derived from normal RR intervals (NN). These include the square root of the mean 
squared differences of successive RR intervals (RMSSD) that is associated with 
respiratory effects on heart rate and is modulated by both parasympathetic and 
sympathetic activity, with the proportion derived by dividing the number of interval 
         
         

9 
 
differences of successive NN intervals greater than 50 ms (NN50) by the total number of 
NN intervals (pNN50) reflects rapid adjustments, and the standard deviation of NN (SDNN) 
expresses overall HRV regulation. 
Upper endoscopy was performed under conscious sedation following overnight fasting. 
Biopsies from the gastric mucosa were performed via gastrointestinal endoscopy from the 
proximal (fundus) and distal (antrum) parts of the stomach (Supplementary Figure 1). 
The same endoscopist performed all the biopsies for controls and patients in a blinded 
fashion. 
An additional skin biopsy from the leg was performed to assess intraepidermal nerve fiber 
density (IENF) for comparison with mucosal innervation. The density of IENF was 
calculated according to international guidelines [26]. 
The leg biopsies were collected by a 3-mm disposable punch. All biopsies were fixed in 
Zamboni solution for 5 hours and then cryopretected with a solution of 
polyvinylpyrrolidone. All the biopsies were immunostained and analyzed at the Skin Biopsy 
Lab of the ICS Maugeri IRCCS of Telese Terme. Tissue samples were cut with a sliding 
microtome at 100 µm for the gastric mucosa and 50 µm for the leg. Specimens were 
processed via indirect immunofluorescence using a panel of primary antibodies including 
pan-neuronal marker protein gene product 9.5 (PGP 9.5) for “total” nerve fibers, 
vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) for autonomic cholinergic fibers, collagen type IV (COL-
IV) for glandular structures, blood vessels and thin muscle layers separating mucosa from 
sub-mucosa and ulex europaeus agglutinin I (ULEX) an agglutinin that binds to epithelia 
and endothelia. To characterize the immunohistochemical phenotype of mucosa nerve 
fibers we assessed, in a sub-group of subjects, the colocalization of VIP with adrenergic 
(DBH), sensory (Sub-P) and pan-neuronal (PGP 9.5) markers. Primary antibodies were 
visualized using species-specific secondary antibodies coupled with the fluorophores Cy2, 
Cy5 and Cy3 (Supplementary Table 1) [27]. 
         
         

10 
 
We acquired a total of 248 digital images from the fundus and antrum using a non-laser 
confocal microscope (Axioimager M.2 with Apotome2, Zeiss, Jena, Germany). The density 
of nerve fibers in the fundus and antrum was quantified with Neurolucida 360 software 
(MBF Bioscience, Inc., Williston, VT, USA) that performs accurate reconstruction of 
neuronal structures. Briefly, the software, based on a 3D tracing method, uses algorithms 
to trace axons point by point. The software only takes in account fluorescence 
morphologically compatible with axon structure in terms of thickness, continuity, curvature, 
direction and brightness and creates a 3D map of the nerves included within the region of 
interest. Finally, the software calculates the length of PGP 9.5 and VIP nerves per volume 
of tissue (nm/µm3) (Supplementary Figure 1) [27].  For the quantitative evaluation, sample 
size analysis was based on the mean and standard deviation of pan-neuronal fundus 
nerve density observed in the control group considering a minimum difference of 1 nm/µm³ 
of nerve length. The calculated sample size was of 10 patients to achieve a statistical 
significance. 
All the procedures for quantifying nerve fibers were executed by an operator blinded to the 
diagnosis. 
Statistical analysis 
To test whether variables were normally distributed, we used the Shapiro-Wilk test. 
Differences between the Long-COVID-19 patients and the Control group were evaluated 
by the Mann–Whitney test or t test, as appropriate. Pearson or Spearman tests were used, 
as appropriate, to assess possible correlations between clinical, morphological, and 
demographic data. We performed power and sample-size analysis using the “onemean” 
command of Stata software and assuming an error alpha equal to 5% and a study power 
of 80%.The data are presented as the mean ± SD or median and interquartile range (IQR) 
based on the distribution of the values.

Results 
The demographic and clinical data, neurological examination findings and laboratory data 
of the patients in the study groups are summarized in Table1 and Supplementary Table 2. 
Among the Long-COVID-19patients, 66% (8/12) reported orthostatic intolerance, 66% 
(8/12) reported vasomotor symptoms, 25% (3/12) reported hyperhidrosis, and 42% (5/12) 
reported gastrointestinal symptoms, while in the Control group 25% (2/8) reported 
vasomotor symptoms, 25% (2/8) reported gastrointestinal symptoms and none reported 
orthostatic intolerance or hyperhidrosis. No subjects from the two groups reported urinary 
or pupillomotor dysfunction. 
Samples of gastric biopsies from Long-COVID-19 patients were obtained 21.0±10.2 (range 
12-52) weeks after nasopharyngeal swab negativity. Endoscopy revealed that the stomach 
mucosa appeared normal in Control and Long-COVID-19 patients. Hematoxylin-eosin 
(H&E) staining of histological sections of the gastric mucosa for Helicobacter pylori was 
negative in Controls and Long-COVID-19 patients. 
In controls the gastric mucosa, as observed by fluorescence microscopy, appeared to be 
richly innervated with both PGP 9.5 and VIP staining. Fibers from a rich submucosal 
neural plexus reached the apex of the papillae. In patient samples, the innervation was 
poorer and irregular. The submucosal plexus was less rich, fibers in the mucosal papillae 
were sparse, and most of the fibers did not reach the apex of the papillae (Figure 1). 
In both antrum and fundus, sensory (Sub-P-ir) and noradrenergic (DBH-ir) nerve fibers 
were scarcely represented with no difference between patients and controls. They were 
located in muscularis mucosa and did not colocalize with VIP that, as it happens in the 
skin, also in the gastric mucosa colocalize with cholinergic fibers as demonstrated by its 
         
         

12 
 
coexpressionwith ChAT [28]. In our samples most of the VIP-ir fibers showed 
colocalization with PGP 9.5 (Supplementary Figure 3). 
Quantitative assessment of mucosal innervation demonstrated that the density of PGP-ir 
nerves in Long-COVID-19 patients was lower than that in Controls (p<0.01) in the fundus 
and in the antrum (p<0.05). The loss of VIP-ir (cholinergic) nerves was even more evident 
in the fundus (p<0.01). This loss, although to a lesser extent, was also observed in the 
antrum (p<0.01) (Figure 1 and 2A). 
Intraepidermal nerve fiber density was not different between the 2 groups of patients 
(p>0.05), and no correlation was found between IENF and mucosal nerves. Data on 
mucosal and cutaneous innervation are detailed in Table 2. 
Compared to Controls, Long-COVID-19 patients had impaired cardiovascular autonomic 
control, as assessed by HRV (LF/HF ratio = 1.7±0.2 vs 1.1±0.1, p<0.01); higher D-dimer 
levels, 796.6±167.7 vs 348.7±78.1, p<0.01 (suggesting a higher risk of thromboembolism); 
and higher NT-proBNP levels, 646.4±307.9 vs 258.6±172.1, p<0.01, a marker of heart 
failure. Details on recorded parameters of HRV are reported in Table 3. 
The gastric mucosa nerve density correlated with the HRV (LF/HF R=0.50, p<0.05, Coef. -
2.40),  NT-proBNP level (R=0.52, p<0.01,) and D-dimer level (R=0.61, p<0.01,) (Figure 2B 
to 2D).  
Discussion 
The present study provides, to our knowledge, the first in vivo morphological evidence of 
selective cholinergic denervation within the gastric mucosa of patients with Long-COVID-
19. By integrating histological findings with functional autonomic assessment, our results 
support the existence of a structural–functional impairment of the autonomic nervous 
system, with predominant involvement of the parasympathetic (vagal) pathway. 
         
         

13 
 
A central finding of this study is the marked reduction in mucosal nerve fiber density, 
particularly affecting cholinergic (VIP-positive) fibers. This observation is of particular 
relevance, as it suggests that autonomic dysfunction in Long-COVID-19 is not merely a 
functional disturbance but may reflect an underlying structural lesion. The preferential 
involvement of cholinergic fibers further points to a selective vulnerability of the 
parasympathetic system, consistent with the clinical phenotype characterized by reduced 
vagal tone and sympathovagal imbalance. 
Importantly, the observed structural alterations were significantly associated with functional 
indices of autonomic regulation, including HRV parameters, NT-proBNP levels, and D-
dimer concentrations. This convergence of morphological and physiological data 
strengthens the hypothesis that vagal impairment represents a central mechanism linking 
local neural damage to systemic manifestations. In particular, the correlation with HRV 
indices reinforces the interpretation of reduced parasympathetic activity, while the 
association with biomarkers of cardiovascular stress and coagulation suggests a broader 
systemic impact of autonomic disruption. 
The absence of significant differences in intraepidermal nerve fiber density between 
patients and controls provides an additional layer of insight. Unlike conditions such as 
diabetic neuropathy, in which somatic and autonomic fibers are concomitantly affected 
[21], our findings suggest a more selective involvement of visceral autonomic pathways. 
This selective vulnerability may reflect specific mechanisms of SARS-CoV-2 neurotropism 
or immune-mediated injury targeting the vagus nerve and its peripheral 
projections.Interestingly, recent evidence has suggested that molecular mimicry may 
trigger autoimmune responses against proteins expressed in central vagal nuclei, including 
the nucleus ambiguus and the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus, potentially contributing 
to autonomic dysfunction in Long-COVID. Although our findings do not directly address 
         
         

14 
 
central nervous system involvement, they are consistent with the hypothesis that 
dysfunction of the vagal axis may occur at multiple anatomical levels. [29] 
From a pathophysiological perspective, these findings support a model in which SARS-
CoV-2-related injury to vagal fibers leads to a disruption of the cholinergic anti-
inflammatory pathway. Such disruption may result in a failure to adequately regulate 
immune responses, thereby sustaining a pro-inflammatory state and contributing to the 
persistence of symptoms. In this framework, the gastrointestinal tract given its extensive 
vagal innervation and its role as a major immunological interface may represent a critical 
site of pathological interaction. 
Our findings support an overall parasympathetic impairment in Long-COVID-19 patients. 
In our study, the loss of nerve fibers in the gastric mucosa of patients with Long- COVID-
19 was not associated with cutaneous loss of somatic fibers, suggesting that in these 
patients, gastric mucosa denervation might be the effect of a more selective pathogenic 
mechanism affecting primarily the autonomic nervous system. The selectivity of a 
parasympathetic involvement is also suggested by the evidence of an impairment of 
gastric motility and a normal sudomotor function in post-COVID-19 patients [30]. 
A clear understanding of the pathogenetic mechanisms may be helpful in the management 
of Long-COVID-19, which remains a medical challenge and a public health problem since 
it affects 5-10% of individuals with acute SARS-CoV-2 infection. Among the different 
pathogenetic hypotheses, some evidence points to early and persistent involvement of the 
VN that may underlie several symptoms. The VN innervates the larynx, pharynx, lungs, 
heart, and gastrointestinal tract. The GI tract is the second-largest interface between the 
external environment and the body and is continuously vulnerable to potentially damaging 
agents and pathogens. In particular, in the stomach, the VN acts as a sensory afferent and 
efferent pathway to sense injury and to apply protective strategies controlling involuntary 
         
         

15 
 
visceral functions as part of the autonomic nervous system and modulating systemic 
inflammation. In particular, the role of gastric vagal innervation is essentially protective. It 
signals potential threats to the central nervous system at the gastrointestinal level, eliciting 
autonomic, emotional-affective and neuroendocrine reactions[31]. So, a disruption of vagal 
innervation may underlie persistent symptoms after SARS-CoV-2 infection. 
Histological evidence of VN involvement has recently been provided through the 
demonstration of inflammatory cell infiltration during SARS-CoV-2 infection [30].  
Although we are not able to discriminate between intrinsic and extrinsic parasympathetic 
gastric innervation, our data support the hypothesis that the axonal damage to vagal fibers 
induced by SARS-CoV-2 trafficking along nerve fibers leads to autonomic dysfunction and 
a lack of VN-mediated inhibition of proinflammatory mechanisms in Long-COVID-19 
patients. 
This model is in agreement with recent morphological and functional findings of VN 
impairment based on neck ultrasound observations of increased echogenicity in the 
epineurium, focal thickening of the nerve and reduced gastrointestinal motility [31]. 
The clinical implications of these findings are substantial. The identification of a structural 
autonomic substrate opens new avenues for targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at 
restoring vagal function or enhancing cholinergic signaling. Interventions such as vagus 
nerve stimulation or pharmacological modulation of cholinergic pathways may therefore 
represent promising approaches for the management of Long-COVID-19, although further 
studies are needed to evaluate their efficacy. 
 
Limitations 
This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center proof-of-concept study with a 
relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. So further 
         
         

16 
 
studies based on larger population of patients and controls are warranted. However, the 
strict inclusion criteria and the invasive nature of the procedures required for tissue 
acquisition make the recruitment of larger cohorts challenging. 
Second, the control group consisted of patients undergoing endoscopy for dyspeptic 
symptoms rather than completely healthy individuals, which may introduce potential bias. 
Nevertheless, no macroscopic or histological abnormalities were observed in the gastric 
mucosa, supporting their suitability as controls. 
Third, we did not include patients who recovered from SARS-CoV-2 infection without 
persistent symptoms, which would have provided additional insight into the specificity of 
the observed alterations. 
Finally, our methodological approach does not allow discrimination between intrinsic 
enteric and extrinsic vagal cholinergic innervation or between cholinergic, and non 
adrenergic non cholinergic (NANC) VIP-positive fibers. Therefore, although our findings 
strongly suggest vagal involvement, the precise anatomical origin of the observed 
denervation cannot be definitively established. 
Conclusions 
In conclusion, this study provides the first in vivo morphological evidence of gastric 
mucosal cholinergic denervation in patients with Long-COVID-19. The observed 
association between structural denervation and alterations in heart rate variability supports 
the presence of a structural–functional autonomic impairment, likely involving vagal 
pathways. 
These findings identify a potential anatomical substrate for dysautonomia in Long-COVID-
19and offer a novel perspective on the pathophysiology of persistent symptoms. 
Further studies are warranted to confirm these results and to explore whether targeted 
interventions aimed at restoring vagal function may improve clinical outcomes in this 
population.
