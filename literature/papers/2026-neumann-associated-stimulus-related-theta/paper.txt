Abstract
Background
Fatigue is the main driver of impaired quality of life in post COVID-19. Yet, fatigue overlaps with
depression, anxiety, poor sleep, and cognitive complaints. Task-related neurophysiological correlates
have been largely unexplored and subjective symptoms often diverge from objective cognitive
performance. We investigated whether fatigue (i) is independently associated with task-related cognitive
performance, (ii) has identifiable EEG correlates, and (iii) mediates the relationship between subjective
cognitive symptoms and objective performance.
Methods
In 98 individuals with prior PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection from the COVIDOM/NAPKON study in
Kiel, we recorded 128-channel EEG during a psychomotor vigilance task. Fatigue (FACIT-Fatigue
subscale), depression and anxiety (HADS), and sleep quality (PSQI) were assessed. Partial correlations,
group comparisons of pre- versus post-stimulus theta and alpha peak power, and a bootstrap mediation
analysis were performed. Key correlations were replicated in the population-based COVIDOM sample
from Kiel (n = 2,549). We investigated the oscillatory activity by removing aperiodic components from the
total signal.
Results
Stronger fatigue was independently associated with slower reaction times, whereas depression, anxiety,
and sleep were not. This finding was replicated in the larger COVIDOM sample from Kiel. The increase in
theta and alpha peak power from the pre- to the post-stimulus period was significantly smaller in
participants with clinically significant fatigue. Descriptively, these participants showed slightly higher
pre-stimulus theta and alpha power and lower post-stimulus peak power than participants without
clinically significant fatigue. The presence of subjective cognitive symptoms (disorientation, slowed
thinking, difficulties concentrating, or forgetfulness) was associated with slower reaction times. This
association was substantially mediated by fatigue (39% of the effect), acknowledging potential measure
overlap.
Conclusions
Fatigue is a construct with distinct behavioral and electrophysiological signatures and a central link
between subjective cognitive complaints and objective deficits, suggesting fatigue as an essential target
for clinical assessment and intervention.

Introduction
Fatigue
Fatigue is defined as the sensation of depleted energy reserves accompanied by an increased need for
rest that is disproportionate to the preceding exertion [1]. It is a major symptom of various conditions,
including post COVID-19 condition (PCC) [2], chronic fatigue syndrome [3], multiple sclerosis [4], and
cancer [5]. Although healthy individuals can also experience fatigue, ‘clinically significant fatigue’ (CSF) is
characterized by high severity, significant impairment of daily activities, and persistence over time (often
without improvement through rest), features that distinguish CSF from other forms of everyday fatigue
[3, 6, 7].
Given the consensus that fatigue is a subjective sensation, it has consequently been measured to date
only by questionnaires or clinical interviews [8, 9]. In contrast, the objectively measurable, temporary
decline in motor or cognitive performance induced by activity during a task is referred to as ‘fatigability’
[8, 10]. Fatigue and fatigability are considered distinct constructs, as the correlations between them are
generally quite weak [8, 11]. Nevertheless, individuals who report higher levels of fatigue also tend to
exhibit greater fatigability [12].
Fatigue has been identified as the key factor impairing quality of life after infection with severe acute
respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) [13]. It was thus found to occur significantly more
frequently in individuals following an infection than in a randomized, Germany-wide control cohort
assessed prior to the pandemic [14]. Structural alterations in subcortical brain regions have been
proposed as a cause of the severity of PCC-associated fatigue [15]. It is noteworthy that fatigue
frequently co-occurs with depressive symptoms, poor sleep quality, cognitive symptoms, and anxiety [16,
17]. However, since these conditions are strongly correlated [18, 19], it often remains unclear whether
fatigue is a primary symptom of a specific health issue or merely a consequence of other symptoms.
Therefore, the assessment of fatigue always requires careful consideration of, and differentiation from,
potentially overlapping affective and cognitive symptoms. A simple reaction-time task that is widely
used in research on sleep and fatigue is the PVT [20–22].
Electrophysiological correlates
Electroencephalography (EEG) signals reflect the summed postsynaptic activity of (sub)cortical neurons,
comprising both periodic (oscillatory) and aperiodic activity [23]. Thus, the power spectral density not
only encompasses periodic oscillations such as in the alpha and theta frequency band, but also has a
distinct aperiodic component characterized by a 1/f-like distribution, reflecting background neuronal
activity [24, 25]. Since distinct physiological correlates of aperiodic activity have been identified, periodic
and aperiodic components should clearly be parameterized and analyzed separately [26]. This
separation is important in practice because changes in the aperiodic component can influence the
measurement of oscillatory activity and can thus lead to misinterpretation of the signal [24, 27].

Previous research has aimed to identify electrophysiological signatures of fatigue mainly in resting-state
EEG data, with increased theta power and decreased alpha power identified as the most promising
candidates [28, 29]. In addition to resting-state EEG, task-related EEG data show that both
frontal/frontomedial theta power and parietal alpha power increase over the course of a task,
accompanied by increasing error rates and prolonged reaction times [30–34]. These changes have been
interpreted as a compensatory effort in response to growing mental fatigue and declining attentional
performance [30, 33, 34]. An increase in task-related theta power is associated with early preparatory
processes involved in attention and memory encoding, and can therefore occur during or prior to
stimulus presentation [35]. Similarly, higher pre-stimulus alpha power has been found to be associated
with reduced sustained attention and, hence, to predict subsequent errors [36]. However, although they
claimed to investigate mental fatigue, task-related EEG studies have merely examined fatigability, while
the task-related neurophysiological correlates of fatigue have remained largely unexplored. An especially
understudied area is the relationship between fatigue and brain activity after the presentation of a task-
relevant stimulus (‘post-stimulus’).
Other studies investigating cognitive function independently of fatigue found that higher task-related
theta power during cognitive processing and lower alpha power during task performance are both
associated with better cognitive performance and less cognitive impairment [37, 38]. Furthermore,
activity in the theta frequency band is a key component of the P3 wave [39], the amplitude of which is
consistently reduced in individuals with lower cognitive functioning and greater fatigue [40–46].
Likewise, post-stimulus alpha power has been shown to be associated with cognitive performance and
P3 amplitude [47].
Brain fog and cognitive symptoms
Patients with various medical conditions frequently self-report a sensation known as ‘brain fog’,
characterized by deficits in cognitive performance, fatigue, and affective symptoms [48]. However,
subjective cognitive symptoms and objective cognitive performance are not strongly correlated with
each other and are therefore typically regarded as distinct concepts [48–50]. For instance, most
individuals with persistent symptoms following SARS-CoV-2 infection report cognitive symptoms and
fatigue [51, 52], but although their performance on neuropsychological test is poorer on average, their
subjectively perceived cognitive symptoms often do not align their actual cognitive performance [49, 53,
54]. In fact, identifying the reasons for subjective cognitive symptoms associated with brain fog is
challenging because these symptoms can stem from a variety of causes, including fatigue, depression,
sleep disturbances, or structural brain changes [15, 55–57]. Anyhow, there are good reasons to assume
that subjective cognitive symptoms are primarily linked to fatigue [49], as the latter appears to be the key
mediator between subjective cognitive symptoms and cognitive performance [58].
Research question
The aim of the present study was to investigate in detail the interrelationships between fatigue and task-
related cognitive performance as well as their underlying neurophysiological correlates. We
Page 6/28

hypothesized that fatigue, but not depressive symptoms, anxiety or sleep quality, was independently
associated with task-related cognitive performance. To examine the role of neurophysiological activity in
fatigue, we compared the task-related EEG profiles of individuals with and without CSF, defined by a
validated questionnaire cutoff. In doing so, we focused upon theta- and alpha power before and after
stimulus onset, as changes in both frequency bands have consistently been linked to fatigue before.
Finally, we hypothesized that individuals who reported cognitive symptoms perform worse on a
psychomotor vigilance task than those without such symptoms, and that this difference could be
explained by fatigue. The study was conducted on a cohort of individuals with a prior SARS-CoV-2
infection and, consequently, a high prevalence of persistent fatigue.
Methods
Study cohort
The participants of the present study (N = 98) were randomly recruited as a sub-cohort of COVIDOM in
Kiel (also referred to as ‘NAPKON-POP’). COVIDOM is a large, longitudinal multicenter study embedded
within the National Pandemic Cohort Network (NAPKON), with study sites in Berlin, Kiel, and Würzburg
[59]. Every COVIDOM participant who took part in the follow-up examination at the Kiel study site
between June 2023 and July 2025 was eligible for the present study. These individuals were ≥ 18 years
old, resided in Schleswig-Holstein and, like all other COVIDOM participants, had a prior SARS-CoV-2
infection diagnosed by Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) test [59]. Both in COVIDOM and in the present
study, written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation, in
accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The present study was approved by the ethics committee of
Kiel University (reference number: Kiel D446/23) and registered in the German Clinical Studies Registry
(ID: DRKS00032132; Registration Date: 26 June 2023).
Study procedures
The COVIDOM anamnesis interview, among other things, also asked for neurological symptoms that had
developed either during the acute phase of COVID-19 or afterwards. One response option addressed the
presence of cognitive symptoms, including disorientation, slowed thinking, difficulties concentrating, or
forgetfulness, which hereafter will be referred to as ‘subjective cognitive symptoms’. In addition,
COVIDOM participants underwent cognitive screening using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment
(MoCA), where total scores < 26 are considered indicative of cognitive impairment [60].
All participants of the present study paid a one-time study visit to the Department of Neurology,
University Hospital Schleswig-Holstein, Campus Kiel, where they first performed the paper-and-pencil
Trail Making Test [61]. Subsequently, an EEG was performed comprising recordings during four
computerized cognitive tasks as well as a resting-state measurement. Finally, participants were asked to
complete three questionnaires to assess fatigue, depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and sleep
quality.

Cognitive performance and fatigue assessment
All four computerized cognitive tasks performed during EEG recording had been programmed in
PsychoPy (version 2022.2.4) and presented to the participants on a 27-inch computer screen placed at a
distance of 1 meter. For the purpose of the present study, we confined the analysis to the Psychomotor
Vigilance Task (PVT) [62] because it efficiently captures the cognitive domains of sustained attention
and alertness in the reaction time.
Each trial started with the presentation of a white fixation cross for 2–10 seconds, followed by the
display of a red number, counting the milliseconds elapsed since its onset (Fig. 1). Participants were
instructed to press the spacebar as quickly as possible as soon as the red number appeared. Following
this response, the counter stopped and displayed the individual reaction time for 500 ms. Then, the
fixation cross reappeared to initiate the next trial.
Fatigue was assessed using the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy – Fatigue Subscale
(FACIT-Fatigue subscale) questionnaire [63], which was generally employed throughout the COVIDOM
study [16]. The questionnaire addresses the presence of self-reported fatigue, its severity, and its impact
on daily activities and functioning over the preceding 7 days [64]. A resulting score ≤ 30 is usually
considered indicative of CSF [65]. Anxiety and depression were assessed with the Hospital Anxiety and
Depression Scale (HADS) [66], a self-report instrument developed for use in somatic settings that
deliberately excluded symptoms that also occur in physical disorders (e.g., fatigue, headaches). The
German translation of the HADS was used in the present study [67]. Notably, the HADS score can be split
into an anxiety sub-score (HADS-A) and a depression subscore (HADS-D). Sleep quality was assessed
with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [68]. The PSQI refers to the sleep behavior of the last four
weeks.
EEG recording
EEG was recorded using a 128-channel cap with equidistant electrode layout (128Ch Standard Brain Cap
for actiCHamp Plus, Easycap GmbH, Wörthsee, Germany) and an actiCHamp Plus Amplifier (Brain
Products GmbH, Gilching, Germany). The sampling rate was 1000 Hz with an amplitude resolution of 0.1
µV. Electrode impedances were kept below 20 kΩ. The reference electrode was placed on the nose, and
two electrooculogram electrodes were positioned diagonally beneath each eye.
EEG preprocessing
EEG data were analyzed using Matlab R2024b (version: 24.2.0.2806996) [69] with custom-written scripts
based upon the Fieldtrip (version: 20250106) [70] and EEGLAB (version: 2024.2) [71] toolboxes.
Preprocessing was carried out in two streams:
1. For artifact identification, the continuous data underwent DC correction and band-pass filtering (1 to
40 Hz; onepass-zerophase, Hamming-windowed sinc Finite Impulse Response), followed by the
removal of noisy channels based upon variance criteria (sample median: 39 removed channels,
Page 8/28

range: 0–48), and of 1-second segments with |z| > 2 (sample median: 23 removed segments, range:
0–36). Subsequently, independent component analysis (runica, EEGLAB) was performed, with
components automatically classified as muscle, eye, heart, line noise, channel noise, or other
artifact using ICLabel [72] and pop_icflag (sample median: 58 removed components, range: 0–86).
2. For spectral power analysis, the raw data were band-pass filtered (1 to 90 Hz) and a 50 Hz band-
stop was applied. After noisy channels and artifactual components identified in stream 1 had been
removed, the data were segmented into 2.4-second epochs spanning ± 1.2 seconds around each
PVT stimulus onset. Trials with extreme joint probability were excluded (sample median: 4 removed
trials, range: 1–11), removed noisy channels were interpolated using spherical splines, and the
signal was re-referenced to common average. For complete filter specifications and rejection
criteria, see Supplementary Methods.
EEG spectral power analysis
EEG spectral power was computed separately for the 1-second pre- and post-stimulus intervals in order
to distinguish event-related and non-event-related activity [73]. Trial-averaged power spectra (1 to 40 Hz)
were computed by way of fast Fourier transformation using the mtmfft method in Fieldtrip with 4
seconds padding and a dpss taper providing 4 Hz smoothing. The aperiodic component was computed
using the FOOOF algorithm in knee mode [74] and was then subtracted from the total spectrum to obtain
the oscillatory component, averaged over channels. Alpha peak power was defined as the maximum
power within 8 to 12 Hz whereas theta peak power was defined as the maximum power within 4 to 7 Hz.
Statistical analyses
All data preparation, visualization, and statistical analyses were performed with R (version: 4.4.2) [75]
and JASP (version: 0.19.3.0) [76]. EEG-specific data processing and visualizations (topographical plots)
were done with Matlab R2024b [69]. All scripts and data used in the present study are publicly available
at github.com/christianneumannCAU/EPOC-Kiel and openneuro.org/datasets/ds007605, respectively.
The study cohort was divided into two sub-groups according to the CSF cutoff of the FACIT-Fatigue
subscale, and demographic data were summarized separately for each group. Differences in age, years
of education, and MoCA total score were assessed for statistical significance using a two-tailed Mann-
Whitney U test for independent samples. Differences in the distribution of gender and pre-diagnosed
neurological disorders between groups were analyzed with a χ² tests of independence. P values < 0.05
were considered statistically significant throughout.
Association between fatigue and task-related cognitive performance
To assess the level of association between fatigue and task-related cognitive performance after
adjustment for depression, anxiety, and sleep quality, a partial Spearman rank correlation analysis was
carried out between FACIT-Fatigue subscale score and PVT reaction time, controlling for the following
covariates: HADS-D total score, HADS-A total score, PSQI total score, age and years of education. To
Page 9/28

examine whether depression, anxiety, or sleep quality are independently associated with cognitive
performance, a partial correlation analysis was also performed between PVT reaction time and HADS-D
total score, HADS-A total score, and PSQI total score, respectively, controlling for the same psychological
and demographic covariates. P values were Holm-corrected to account for the multiplicity of these four
primary correlation analyses.
The partial correlation analyses were then repeated for the complete COVIDOM cohort from Kiel (n = 
2,549). Here, PSQI and years of education were not included as control variables because the data were
not available. Moreover, depressive symptoms and anxiety were measured in COVIDOM by the Patient
Health Questionnaire − 8 (PHQ-8) [77] and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7) [78].
Neurophysiological correlates of fatigue
Pair-wise differences between the pre- and post-stimulus measurements of theta (4 to 7 Hz) and alpha
(8 to 12 Hz) peak power were tested for statistical significance using a two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank
test. Whether these differences were significantly smaller in individuals with CSF than without CSF was
assessed using an one-tailed Mann-Whitney U test. P values were Holm-corrected for the two primary
tests.
Role of subjective cognitive symptoms
Participants in the present study were also stratified according to their answer (“yes” or “no”) to the
COVIDOM neurological anamnesis question about symptoms developed in connection with COVID-19
(see above). Resulting group differences in PVT reaction time were assessed for statistical significance
using a one-tailed Mann-Whitney U test, and the effect size was quantified by the corresponding rank-
biserial correlation. In addition, the effect of fatigue on the association between subjective cognitive
symptoms and PVT reaction time, was assessed by regression-based mediation analysis, using the
mediation package [79] of R. Indirect and direct effects were estimated by nonparametric bootstrap
resampling (10,000 simulations), controlling for HADS-D total score, HADS-A total score, and PSQI total
score, age and years of education. Holm correction was applied to p values of the two primary mediation
tests.
Results
Study characteristics
Instances of missing data were noted, with some overlap, for PVT (n = 1), MoCA (n = 1), FACIT-Fatigue
subscale (n = 2), HADS-D total score (n = 3), HADS-A total score (n = 1), PSQI total score (n = 6) and EEG
recordings (n = 3). Reasons included time and health-related constraints, failure to record button
responses, missing key questionnaire items, and corruption of data files. All behavioral data fell within
expected ranges, and no outliers were removed. Upon visual inspection, EEG peak power values

exceeding the mean by at least 5 SD were classified as outliers, resulting in the exclusion of one

Association between fatigue and task-related cognitive performance
Stronger fatigue (i.e., a lower FACIT-Fatigue subscale score) was significantly associated with slower
reaction times in the PVT (ρ = -0.29, p = 0.03) after adjustment for HADS-D total score, HADS-A total
score, PSQI total score, age and years of education. No significant correlation with PVT reaction time
was observed for HADS-D total score (ρ = -0.13, p = 0.77), HADS-A total score (ρ = 0.09, p = 0.88), or PSQI
total score (ρ = -0.07, p = 0.88).
Similar results were obtained in the complete COVIDOM cohort from Kiel. Slower PVT reaction times
were associated with stronger fatigue (ρ = -0.11, p < 0.001) after adjustment for PHQ-8, GAD-7 and age,
but not with PHQ-8 (ρ = 0.03, p = 0.18) or GAD-7 (ρ = -0.03, p = 0.24) itself.
Neurophysiological correlates of fatigue
Both alpha (W = 120, p < 0.001) and theta (W = 39, p < 0.001) peak power increased significantly from pre-
stimulus to post-stimulus during the PVT (Fig. 2). The increase was significantly smaller in participants
with CSF than in those without CSF, both for theta (U = 647, p = 0.002) and alpha (U = 703, p = 0.004) peak
power (Fig. 2). Moreover, participants with CSF exhibited slightly higher pre-stimulus theta and alpha
power, on average, but lower post-stimulus peak power than participants without CSF. Topographical
plots support these findings (Fig. 3–4): Across all participants, theta power predominantly increased
over frontal albeit widely distributed regions while alpha power primarily increased over parietal regions.
In addition, participants with CSF showed slightly higher pre-stimulus theta and alpha power in some
regions. Consequently, the stimulus-related increase in power was more pronounced in participants
without CSF than in those with CSF (Fig. 2–4).
Task-related cognitive performance and subjective cognitive symptoms
Page 11/28

Participants reporting subjective cognitive symptoms had significantly slower PVT reaction times than
those without such symptoms (U = 1601, p < 0.001), with a rank-biserial correlation of 0.44. Mediation
analysis revealed a significant indirect effect (p = 0.04), but no significant direct effect (p = 0.21). The
proportion mediated was 0.39, indicating that fatigue accounted for 39% of the association between
subjective cognitive symptoms and PVT reaction time. After adjusting for fatigue, the direct association
between subjective cognitive symptoms and PVT reaction time was no longer statistically significant.
Overall, these findings were consistent with a substantial mediation of PVT reaction time by fatigue.
Discussion
In an observational cross-sectional study, we investigated the relationship between fatigue and task-
related cognitive performance, as well as their electrophysiological correlates, among individuals with
prior SARS-CoV-2 infection. We made three key findings:
First, stronger fatigue was associated with slower PVT-assessed reaction time, independently of
potential mediators such as depression, anxiety, sleep quality, or age and education. This result was
qualitatively replicated in the larger COVIDOM cohort from Kiel. Second, study participants with clinically
significant fatigue (CSF) exhibited a smaller task-related increase in both alpha and theta peak power
from pre-stimulus to post-stimulus period during the PVT than participants without CSF. This is also
reflected descriptively by their higher average pre-stimulus and lower average post-stimulus theta and
alpha peak power. Third, subjective cognitive symptoms, as defined by the COVIDOM anamnesis
interview, were associated with slower PVT reaction times, and the association was substantially
mediated by fatigue, considering possible content overlap of the self-report measures of fatigue and
subjective cognitive symptoms.
Taken together, the above findings support fatigue as a distinct neuropsychological concept with
measurable behavioral and electrophysiological qualities, and as a central common correlate of
subjective cognitive symptoms and objective performance reduction after SARS-CoV-2 infection.
1. Fatigue is independently associated with task-related performance
Stronger fatigue was found to be correlated with slower PVT reaction times even after adjustment for
depressive symptoms, anxiety, sleep quality, age and years of education. Notably, depressive symptoms,
anxiety, and sleep quality themselves showed no significant association with PVT reaction time. Even
though all of these symptoms are not easy to distinguish [17–19], our findings empirically confirm that
fatigue, as a neuropsychological concept, is clearly distinct from overlapping affective and sleep-related
symptoms. Our results thus suggest that fatigue has its own effect on sustained attention and alertness
that cannot be reduced to comorbid affective disturbance. This is consistent with previous research
showing that individuals with post-viral fatigue often experience cognitive impairments [55]. Replication
of the observed relationship between fatigue and cognitive performance in a larger population-based
sample confirms its robustness beyond ‘laboratory’ cohorts. This conclusion appears valid despite the
use of different depression and anxiety assessment tools (PHQ-8, GAD-7) and a lack of PSQI data in the
Page 12/28

larger sample. The smaller correlation coefficients noted in the latter were not unexpected, given the
greater heterogeneity of COVIDOM. Importantly, however both the direction and the specificity of the
effect were consistent.
The PVT is primarily used to study fatigue in the context of sleep deprivation and time-on-task effects,
often measured by lapses [20, 22]. Our study takes this approach even further by demonstrating that the
mean reaction time over a five-minute task is associated with fatigue in a way that is not sleep-related.
Moreover, the association relates to subjective fatigue, i.e., a perception-level construct that is distinct
from acute task-induced fatigability, consistently shown to be only weakly correlated with the former [8,
11, 12].
2. Neurophysiological correlates: reduced stimulus-related modulation
All participants showed an increase of both theta and alpha peak power from the pre- to the post-
stimulus interval, with a frontal distribution observed for theta power and a parietal distribution for alpha
power. This pattern is consistent with the established roles of the two neurophysiological characteristics
in cognitive control and stimulus encoding (theta) [35, 80], and attention modulation (alpha) [81],
respectively. Notably, participants with CSF exhibited a smaller pre- to post-stimulus increase in both
bands due to a combination of higher pre-stimulus and lower post-stimulus peak power.
The observed association between CSF and higher pre-stimulus theta and alpha power is consistent with
earlier reports of (i) an increased resting-state theta power in fatigued individuals [28, 29], (ii) increases
in theta and alpha power over the course of a task accompanied by decreases in cognitive performance
[30, 33, 34], and (iii) a link between higher pre-stimulus alpha power and reduced sustained attention
[36]. The reported activities were observed in regions similar to those identified in the present study.
These results suggest that individuals with fatigue are less well prepared for stimuli due to decreased
attentional functioning. The descriptively higher average pre-stimulus theta and alpha power could also
be interpreted as a compensatory mechanism to counteract increased effort.
While the reduced post-stimulus peak among study participants with CSF agrees with a previous
observation of individuals with reduced cognitive function also exhibiting reduced task-related theta
engagement [38], it contrasts with another report of higher alpha power during task performance in
individuals with lower cognitive functioning [37]. However, both of these studies did not investigate the
role of fatigue, rendering our study one of the first to reveal an attenuated increase in post-stimulus theta
and alpha peak power in individuals with fatigue. The reduced theta and alpha peak increase in
participants with CSF may reflect diminished stimulus processing due to fatigue, resulting in slower
reactions.
Increased theta activity was observed between 200 and 400 ms post-stimulus (Supplementary Figure
S1), a time window that corresponds to the N2 - P3 complex [73]. Frontocentral theta activity is
considered a key component of P3 [39], the amplitude of which has been shown to decrease with
prolonged reaction times and in individuals with post COVID-19 [40, 41, 44, 82].
Page 13/28

In participants without CSF, theta activity appeared to be more widely distributed across the scalp and
not confined to the frontal regions. This is consistent with the hypothesized role of theta oscillations as a
communicator and coordinator between functionally relevant networks [80, 83, 84].
3. Subjective cognitive symptoms are explained by fatigue
Participants with self-reported cognitive symptoms showed slower PVT reaction times, with a moderate-
to-large effect size (rank-biserial correlation = 0.44). However, mediation analysis indicated that this
association was substantially mediated by fatigue. Indeed, 39% of the total effect was explicable by the
influence of fatigue, and after adjusting for fatigue, the direct effect of subjective cognitive symptoms on
reaction time was no longer statistically significant. Since depressive symptoms, anxiety, sleep quality,
age, and years of education were held constant in the model, these factors could be excluded as
possible alternative explanations.
Our result adds to a growing body of evidence that subjective cognitive complaints and formally
measured cognition are only loosely linked [48–50, 53, 85], and that fatigue is a primary mediator
between them [58]. Practically it suggests that, when a patient with prior SARS-CoV-2 infection reports
“brain fog”, the most likely tractable clinical target and determinant of objective attentional performance
is fatigue rather than cognitive symptomatology per se. Moreover, if fatigue is a primary mediator of self-
reported cognitive symptoms, this would explain why such reports often do not align with task-based
measures of cognitive performance in post COVID-19 [49], as impairments in sustained attention are
expected to fluctuate over time [86].
The above distinction is crucial for clinical diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making in fatigue-related
conditions such as post COVID-19. Cognitive training may initially appear to be a plausible intervention
for patients reporting cognitive symptoms, as it directly targets cognitive function [87]. However, if
fatigue rather than cognitive impairment per se is the main driver of these symptoms, cognitive training
should be carefully reconsidered. An appropriate balance may be required in such cases to avoid overly
demanding interventions that may exacerbate symptoms while offering limited benefit.
Limitations and future research
As the present study is cross-sectional in design, causal inferences about the direction of the
relationship between fatigue and task-related cognitive performance cannot be drawn, although the
result of the mediation analysis clearly suggests that fatigue is an upstream driver. Second, subjective
cognitive symptoms were captured by a single interview item aggregating disorientation, slowed
thinking, concentration difficulties, and forgetfulness. A more granular symptom inventory would allow
finer-grained mediation modeling. In addition, some FACIT-Fatigue items describing the difficulty of
starting or finishing usual activities may share experiential content with the aggregated cognitive
symptom item, and the present study did not perform item-level discriminant validity checks. Third, all
participants had a prior PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, and no non-infected control group was
included. The results characterize the structure of fatigue and its correlates within this population but
Page 14/28

cannot address whether the observed relationships are specific to post COVID-19 or can be generalized
to fatigue in the context of other conditions. Fourth, the PVT captures sustained attention and alertness
with high sensitivity but does not probe higher-order executive or memory processes. Whether the
observed neural signature extends to other cognitive domains remains to be determined. Finally, the PVT
was administered last in the study procedure. This could have exacerbated the association between
fatigue and task-related cognitive performance due to higher fatigability.
In future research, alpha power could instead be analyzed using regions-of-interest approaches rather
than whole-scalp averages, or be further decomposed to assess potential motor-related contributions.
Given the strong association observed with fatigue over the preceding week, future studies should also
consider longer temporal assessment windows.
Conclusions
In individuals with prior SARS-CoV-2 infection, fatigue is associated with slower reaction times during a
psychomotor vigilance task independently of depressive symptoms, anxiety, and sleep quality. Clinically
significant fatigue is accompanied by a reduced stimulus-related modulation of theta and alpha peak
power, reflecting the combination of elevated pre-stimulus power and diminished post-stimulus peak.
Subjective cognitive symptoms are associated with objective performance decrements, but this link is
substantially mediated by fatigue and cannot be explained by the cognitive complaints themselves.
Taken together, our findings establish fatigue as a distinct construct with reproducible behavioral and
electrophysiological correlates and as a key factor connecting subjective cognitive complaints to
measurable attentional deficits after SARS-CoV-2 infection. Clinically, the results argue for assessing and
prioritizing fatigue, rather than subjective cognitive symptoms alone, when patients report brain fog, and
for weighing this distinction carefully when selecting interventions. Patients should be encouraged to
consider how far fatigue drives their cognitive symptoms, and clinicians should take these complaints
seriously.
