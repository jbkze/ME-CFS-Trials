Abstract
Background
Pathological fatigue is a debilitating symptom in a range of chronic illnesses, including myalgic
encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS). However, drug therapies that are effective at
treating pathological fatigue have proved difficult to identify through conventional drug discovery
pipelines. We sought to identify an ME/CFS gene expression signature in order to map disease pathways
and identify potentially novel therapeutic approaches.
Methods
We analysed ME/CFS patient gene expression profiles obtained from two independent studies
(GSE14577 and GSE227375). The identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subjected to
CMap L1000 analysis for the identification of functional gene networks as well as compounds with the
potential to restore the disrupted gene expression in ME/CFS patients.
Results
We identified 21 up-regulated and 25 down-regulated DEGs common to both gene expression datasets.
Subjecting the 46-gene signature to CMap L1000 analysis identified networks involved in
cytokine/growth factor signalling, infection and immunity, metabolism and cell growth, lipid metabolism,
ubiquitination, nucleotide metabolism, and redox regulation. Four compounds, pioglitazone, rosiglitazone
(PPAR agonists), tideglusib (GSK3 inhibitor), and MHY1485 (mTORC1 agonist), were identified and
tested for their ability to regulate the metabolism and growth of C2C12 muscle cells. Pioglitazone,
tideglusib, and MHY1485, but not rosiglitazone, induced a modest but significant increase in
mitochondrial membrane potential in differentiated C2C12 myoblasts over 24–48 hours. These changes
occurred in the absence of increased mitochondrial mass and ROS production, suggesting enhanced
mitochondrial metabolism rather than mitochondrial biogenesis. While pioglitazone and rosiglitazone
increased the growth of C2C12 myoblasts, no significant effects were observed for tideglusib and
MHY1485.
Conclusions
Our findings, based on reciprocal disease:drug signature-matching, provide a framework for exploring
the therapeutic potential of drugs to target impaired metabolic pathways in conditions involving
pathological fatigue, such as ME/CFS.
Background
Page 2/34

Approximately 25% of primary care patients report fatigue, indicating that it is an extremely common
symptom that is associated with a wide range of pathologies (1, 2). Physiological fatigue after physical
exertion is transient and can be relieved by rest and lifestyle management. In contrast, pathological
fatigue, which is commonly associated with long-term chronic inflammatory conditions, persists despite
adequate rest and lifestyle interventions (3). In prevalent illnesses such as myalgic
encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), multiple sclerosis (MS), cancer-related fatigue
(CRF), post-stroke fatigue (PSF), and more recently long-COVID-19, pathological and persistent fatigue is
associated with exercise intolerance, a decline in physical function and poor health-related quality-of-life
(4, 5). In part because pathological fatigue associated with illness can be clinically mistaken for
physiological tiredness or sleepiness, clinical recognition of its major impacts on daily functioning and
longer-term quality of life is often overlooked (6). These limitations may also extend to research where
the pathophysiology of fatigue and potential therapeutic targets remain largely unclear for many chronic
illnesses (7).
There are a few pharmacological treatments for pathological fatigue, particularly in relation to muscle
dysfunction and exercise intolerance. To date, most clinical trials have investigated immunological or
psychotropic drugs. For example, in ME/CFS, where fatigue is a primary symptom, two systematic
analyses of over 45 trials did not identify any effective drug treatments apart from the possible
exceptions of the immunomodulatory drug rintatolimod and the psychostimulant methylphenidate,
where modest improvements in a subset of patients were observed (8, 9). In at least some MS patients,
the psychostimulant, modafinil, and the dopaminergic agent, amantadine, appear to be the only drugs
that provide significant, albeit modest, improvements in fatigue (10, 11). In a systematic analysis of 32
CRF trials, none of the drugs examined, including dexamphetamine, modafinil, methylphenidate or
paroxetine, were effective for fatigue (12). Similarly, systematic analysis of 22 PSF trials examining a
range of drugs, including modafinil, fluoxetine, and the monoamine stabilizer, OSU6162, reported that
none provided significant improvements in fatigue (13). Additionally, a meta-analysis of 45 studies
examining palliative care in patients with multiple sclerosis, post-polio syndrome, Parkinson's disease,
cancer, HIV/AIDS, end-stage renal disease, multi-type advanced disease in hospice patients, amyotrophic
lateral sclerosis, and end-stage chronic lung disease indicated there was insufficient evidence that any of
the 18 drugs evaluated provided significant benefits in fatigue (14).
Drug repurposing, in which existing compounds with known pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and
safety profiles are identified with previously unsuspected novel activities, may provide an accelerated
route to clinical translation. Given the high attrition rates in clinical trials for novel therapeutics identified
and developed via traditional discovery pipelines, drug repurposing can provide significant advantages in
terms of cost savings through the foreshortening of drug evaluation processes (15). Importantly, for
pathologies such as ME/CFS, where the molecular basis has not been clearly established, repurposed
drugs may provide insights into the underlying pathways and targets responsible.
While most repurposed drugs to date are the result of serendipitous discoveries, recent developments in
computational methods allow disease transcriptomic signatures to be screened against extensive
Page 3/34

databases of drug transcriptomic signatures for the identification of either positively or negatively
correlated profiles (16). For example, positively correlated disease-drug transcriptomic profiles would
suggest that the drug may induce a gene expression profile similar to that observed in the disease and
so might enable insights into the mechanistic basis for pathogenesis. In contrast, negatively correlated
disease-drug transcriptomic profiles would suggest that the drug may reverse the disease expression
profile and so might have therapeutic potential in reversing the pathological phenotype. The large-scale
transcriptomic profile database, Library of Integrated Cellular Signatures (LINCS) Connectivity Map
(CMap L1000), contains over one million gene expression profiles regulated by over 40,000 genetic and
small-molecule perturbagens, allowing disease expression signatures to be matched with negatively
correlated drug-induced expression profiles for the identification of compounds with the potential to
revert the pathological phenotype (17). Thus, LINCS CMap L1000 allows rational data-driven studies on
drug repositioning based on signature reversion, for which there is proof-of-concept in a range of
pathologies, including cancer and kidney disease (18, 19).
Given that pathological fatigue is an obligate symptom of ME/CFS for which there is currently no
approved drug treatment, we have examined the gene expression profiles of ME/CFS patients from two
independently published studies (20, 21). Our analysis identified dysregulated gene networks involved in
immunity and metabolism that were common to both studies. Using CMap L1000, we identified
compounds with known immune and metabolic targets with the potential to induce reciprocal profiles to
those identified in ME/CFS patients. Focussing on compounds having known metabolic targets, we
show that a peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) agonist (pioglitazone), a glycogen
synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) inhibitor (tideglusib), and an agonist of mechanistic target of rapamycin
complex 1 (mTORC1) were able to increase mitochondrial membrane potential and growth in C2C12
mouse muscle cells. These findings may provide new avenues for the development of therapies
targeting cellular metabolism in long-term fatiguing conditions such as ME/CFS.
Methods
ME/CFS gene expression analysis
ME/CFS gene expression profiles were obtained from the publicly available GEO datasets GSE14577 and
GSE227375 (20, 21). The GSE14577 dataset was obtained from the PBMCs of eight post-viral male
ME/CFS patients and seven healthy male controls and was generated using Affymetrix Human Genome
U133A and U133B Arrays (20). The GSE227375 dataset was obtained from the PBMCs of eight post-viral
male ME/CFS patients and nine healthy male controls and was generated using Illumina NextSeq 500
RNA-seq technology (21). The GSE14577 dataset was parsed in GEOquery and then analysed using
RStudio (v4.3.2) using the limma package. The GSE227375 dataset was analysed in RStudio using the
DESeq2 package. The Benjamini and Hochberg method was applied for controlling the false discovery
rate (FDR). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analysed using the EnhancedVolcano package
(v1.24.0). DEGs with an FDR ≤ 0.05 and fold change (FC) ≥ 1.25 were considered significant.
Page 4/34

Connectivity map (CMap) L1000 analysis
At the time of publication, the LINCS CMap L1000 database (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/cmap/)
contained over one million gene expression profiles regulated by over 40,000 genetic and small-molecule
perturbagens (17). We performed CMap L1000 analysis to identify novel i) disease-pathway
relationships and ii) disease-drug relationships. The L1000 database was queried using the ME/CFS
disease signature identified from GSE14577 and GSE227375 to identify perturbagens that induce i)
similar gene expression profiles to ME/CFS (i.e., those with positive CMap scores) and ii) inverse gene
expression profiles to ME/CFS (i.e., those with negative CMap scores). The identification of inverse
disease-drug relationships in LINCS CMap L1000 allows the discovery of drugs with the potential to
reverse a disease expression signature, which has been used to identify new therapeutics where a priori
knowledge of disease mechanism, drug targets, and deregulated pathways may not be available (17, 22).
Compounds with connectivity scores ≤ -0.64, and -log10(FDR) ≥ 15 were selected for further analysis.
Pathway and network analysis
The pathway enrichment analysis using KEGG and Reactome databases was performed in the ShinyGO
(v0.81) tool, whereas for network analysis, we utilized STRING (v12.0). Gene networks were developed
using STRING MCL clustering analysis.
Drug treatment of mouse C2C12 myoblasts and myotubes
The mouse C2C12 myoblast cell line (kind gift of Prof. Aaron Russell, Institute for Physical Activity and
Nutrition, Deakin University) were maintained in medium (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium [DMEM],
2mM L-glutamine, 100 units/ml penicillin and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin) containing 10% heat-inactivated
foetal bovine serum (HI-FBS)(all supplied by ThermoFisher Scientific) at 37°C in the presence of 5% CO2
and passaged when < 80% confluent. For differentiation into myotubes, cells at > 80% confluency were
washed in PBS and placed in medium (DMEM, 2mM L-glutamine, 100 units/ml penicillin and 0.1 mg/ml
streptomycin) containing 1% horse serum (Sigma-Aldrich). The differentiation of C2C12 myoblasts was
determined to be > 90% at 48 hours (h) by Hoechst 33342 (ThermoFisher Scientific) staining of
multinucleated myotubes and confirmation of growth arrest using the MTT assay. Myotube cultures
were replenished with fresh medium and 1% horse serum every 48h. Drugs utilized included pioglitazone
(Jomar Life Research), rosiglitazone (Jomar Life Research), tideglusib (Jomar Life Research), and
MHY1485 (Sigma-Aldrich). All drug compounds were dissolved in DMSO (Sigma‒Aldrich) and
subsequently serially diluted using sterile PBS (ThermoFisher Scientific) to the desired concentrations
for treatments. For drug treatments, C2C12 myoblasts or myotubes were washed with PBS and then
placed in either growth medium or differentiation medium, respectively, with drug titrations and time
courses being performed.

Mitochondrial analysis
The mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial mass were quantified using
Tetramethylrhodamine Ethyl Ester (TMRE) (Jomar Life Research) and MitoTracker Green (MTG) (Sigma-
Aldrich), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Triplicate wells containing either
C2C12 myoblasts or myotubes were treated with either vehicle or the indicated concentrations of drugs
(pioglitazone, rosiglitazone, tideglusib, and MHY1485) for up to 48h, after which 100mM TMRE or MTG
was added for 30 minutes (min) at 37°C. TMRE fluorescence was analysed at 544nm (excitation) and
590nm (emission), and MTG fluorescence was analysed at 485nm (excitation) and 520nm (emission)
using a microplate reader (BMG Labtech POLARstar Omega). For the analysis of intracellular ATP
production, triplicate wells containing either C2C12 myoblasts or myotubes were treated with drugs for
up to 48h, after which they were washed twice with PBS, and ATP was quantified by measuring
bioluminescence using an ATP determination kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s
instructions.
Reactive oxygen species (ROS) analysis
The intracellular ROS production in C2C12 myoblasts and myotubes was assessed using the MitoSOX
Red (MSR) (ThermoFisher Scientific). Triplicate wells containing either C2C12 myoblasts or myotubes
were treated with either vehicle or the indicated concentrations of drugs (pioglitazone, rosiglitazone,
tideglusib, and MHY1485) for up to 60 minutes. Hydrogen peroxide (1mM) was included as a control for
inducing mitochondrial ROS production. Cells were then incubated in 1µM MSR for up to 30 min before
fluorescence was examined using a microplate reader (BMG Labtech POLARstar Omega) at 355nm
(excitation) and 620nm (emission).
Cell growth analysis
The MTT (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) assay (ThermoFisher Scientific)
was carried out to measure C2C12 growth. C2C12 myoblasts or myotubes subjected to drug titrations
were grown for up to 72h, following which MTT reagent was added for 3 h at 37°C. The medium was
then removed, and solubilization buffer (1% w/v SDS, 0.01M HCL) was added. Absorbance signals at
550nm were analysed.
Results
Differential gene expression in ME/CFS
To identify dysregulated gene expression in ME/CFS, we analysed two independently published datasets
in which gene expression in ME/CFS patients and healthy controls has been published (Fig. 1). In the
first study, Gow et. al. (GEO Accession GSE14577) examined mRNA expression profiles using a
microarray platform and reported DEGs involved in oxidative stress, apoptosis, and immune modulation
Page 6/34

(20). In the second study, Gamer et. al. (GEO Accession GSE227375) examined gene expression using
RNA-seq and reported DEGs involved in immune cell signalling, positive regulation of response to an
external stimulus, and natural killer (NK) cell function (21).
We first examined both GSE14577 and GSE227375 to identify DEGs common to both datasets. The
identified DEGs were then subjected to CMap L1000 analysis to identify i) functional gene networks and
pathways involved in ME/CFS and ii) compounds with the potential to reverse ME/CFS disease
signatures and so restore gene expression profiles to those observed in healthy control individuals.
Using these CMap L1000 outputs, compounds were mapped to functional gene networks and prioritized
for further examination for their in vitro activity in C2C12 muscle cells.
Volcano plots depicting DEGs are presented in Fig. 2A (GSE14577) and Fig. 2B (GSE227375). As
expected, RNA-seq analysis resulted in larger fold changes (FC) in gene expression (Fig. 2B) compared
to microarray analysis (Fig. 2A). A total of 312 significantly up-regulated and 286 significantly down-
regulated DEGs were identified in the GSE14577 dataset, while 1243 significantly up-regulated and 1625
down-regulated DEGs were identified in the GSE227375 dataset (Fig. 2C, 2D; FDR ≤ 0.05, -0.3 < Log2FC > 
0.3).

Despite the heterogeneity between the studies of Gow et. al. (20) and Gamer et. al.(21) in terms of their
methodologies, gene expression platforms, and patient cohorts, our analysis identified 21 up-regulated
(Fig. 2C) and 25 down-regulated (Fig. 2D) DEGs that were common to both studies (Fig. 2C and 2D,
Tables 1 and 2). This ME/CFS 46-gene signature included up-regulated DEGs known to encode proteins
serving a broad range of cellular functions including calcium signalling, metabolism, G-protein signalling,
ubiquitination and cell cycle regulation (Table 1). Additionally, down-regulated DEGs encode proteins
involved in inositol lipid signalling, cell cycle regulation, RNA processing, and epigenetic regulation
(Table 2).
ME/CFS pathway and network analysis
While KEGG and Reactome analyses of the 46 ME/CFS DEGs in Tables 1 and 2 identified a small number
of generalized pathways that included homeostasis, dopaminergic neurons, and infection, insights into
potential therapeutic targets and pathways from this analysis were limited (data not shown). The ability
Page 11/34

of such tools to identify functional networks in disease is known to be limited when small panels of
DEGs are analysed (e.g., our 46-gene signature), in part, because the results depend on the quality of the
gene annotations and the completeness of the functional groups represented.
We therefore subjected the ME/CFS 46-gene signature to the CMap L1000 database that allows the
identification of transcriptomic response similarities. The CMap L1000 database contains over one
million mRNA signatures from genetic perturbagens (e.g., expression of shRNAs, cDNAs) in 244 cell
lines (17). shRNA and cDNA perturbagens in the L1000 database that induce either similar or reciprocal
gene expression profiles to those identified in a specific disease may provide insights into disease
pathways and therapeutic targets. For example, the ability of specific shRNA and cDNA perturbagens in
the L1000 platform to induce gene signatures similar (i.e., having positive Connectivity Scores) to those
identified in Tables 1 and 2 would suggest that the targeted genes may, at least in part, control pathways
that promote the ME/CFS clinical phenotype. Conversely, shRNA and cDNA perturbagens that induce
reciprocal gene signatures (i.e., having negative Connectivity Scores) to those identified in Tables 1 and
2 would suggest that the targeted genes may have the potential to reverse the ME/CFS clinical
phenotype (17).
Analysis of the ME/CFS 46-gene signature using CMap L1000 identified perturbagens targeting 83 genes
(Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1). It is notable that, of the 83 genes having a -log10FDR > 
15, all had negative connectivity scores less than − 0.64 (Supplementary Fig. 1, inset). While it is not
clear why the CMap L1000 analysis did not identify genes with -log10FDR > 15 with positive connectivity
scores (Supplementary Fig. 1), it could be related to the proposal that ME/CFS patients demonstrate a
hypometabolic state characterized by an overall decreased flux through multiple pathways (23).
We next performed STRING analysis on the 83 genes identified by CMap L1000 to gain insight into
possible functional disease pathways. This analysis identified multiple networks involving i)
cytokine/growth factor signalling, ii) infection and immunity, iii) metabolism and cell growth, iv) lipid
metabolism, v) cytoskeletal regulation, vi) ubiquitination, vii) nucleotide metabolism, and viii) redox
regulation (Fig. 3A). Pathway enrichment analysis using KEGG identified, i) metabolic pathways, ii)
infection (Kaposi sarcoma associated herpes virus infection, Epstein-Barr virus infection, Yersinia
infection), iii) cytokine signalling (TNF signalling), iv) growth factor signalling (ErbB, FoxO signalling), v)
endocrine resistance, vi) reactive oxygen species (ROS) signalling and vii) autophagy pathways being
represented within the gene network (Fig. 3B). Reactome analysis identified pathways involved in, i)
metabolism, ii) immune response, iii) infection, iv) cytokine signalling, v) post-translational modifications
and vi) cellular response to stress (Fig. 3C).
The identification of gene networks controlling cytokine signalling and infection in Fig. 3 is in line with
reports that ME/CFS patients exhibit a range of immunological disruptions (24). In addition, multiple
infectious pathogens identified in Fig. 3, including the SARS-CoV-2 virus responsible for COVID-19, have
been linked with the ability to trigger ME/CFS (24, 25). Also prominent in our analysis was the
identification of metabolic and cell growth pathways (Fig. 3).

CMap analysis of ME/CFS gene expression signatures for the identification of compounds.
The ME/CFS gene signature was then used to query the CMap L1000 database to identify compounds
with the potential to induce reciprocal gene expression profiles (17). This approach allows the
calculation of connectivity (similarity) scores between the ME/CFS DEGs and the gene signatures
induced by 32,979 compounds across 244 cell lines. Compounds with negative connectivity scores are
predicted to induce a gene expression profile that is negatively correlated with disease (17). Thus,
compounds with negative connectivity scores may have the potential to reverse, at least in part, the gene
expression profiles associated with ME/CFS and so may have therapeutic potential.
CMap L1000 analysis of the ME/CFS genes shown in Table 1 and Table 2 resulted in an output of
compounds with connectivity scores ranging from − 0.73 to 0.72 (Fig. 4). From this analysis, a total of 14
compounds (0.01% of the total CMap L1000 database) with negative Connectivity Scores of ≤-0.65 and -
log10(FDR) > 15.0 were identified (Fig. 4B, inset). Consistent with our pathway analysis identifying gene
networks controlling cytokine signalling, infection, and immunity in ME/CFS (Fig. 3), a total of 7 of the 14
(50%) compounds (7/14, 50%) were identified with known anti-inflammatory or analgesic activity (SB-
705498, Mefenamic acid, Skimmianine, Ginkgolide-a, PF-750, PRE-084, and VX-702)(Supplementary
Table 2). Also consistent with our pathway analysis (Fig. 3) identifying metabolic deregulation, 2/14
(14%) compounds (Supplementary Table 2) were identified with the ability to regulate mitochondrial
metabolism or nutrient sensing (Timofibrate and Indoximod). In addition, 2/14 (14%) neurotransmitter
regulators (Supplementary Table 2) used in the treatment of psychiatric illness were identified
(Idalopirdine and Buspirone). Other compounds included a second mitochondrial-derived activator of
caspases (SMAC) mimetic (GDC-0152), a histone deacetylase inhibitor (Pracinostat) used in the
treatment of cancer, and a multi-targeted kinase inhibitor (Arcyriaflavin-a).
Although our analysis identified multiple compounds with anti-inflammatory and analgesic activity
(Supplementary Table 2), clinical trials to date examining drugs targeting immune pathways in ME/CFS
patients have not reported significant and sustained improvements for the majority of patients (8, 26–
28). On the other hand, the identification of compounds using CMap L1000 with the ability to target
energy metabolism was intriguing. ME/CFS patients have been reported to have significant reductions in
aerobic metabolic capacity as measured in cardio-pulmonary exercise tests (CPET) (29, 30). Such
impaired aerobic metabolism has been associated with altered blood metabolite profiles in patients that
suggest impaired mitochondrial metabolism (23, 31). However, there are currently no effective
pharmacological treatments targeting such defects associated with pathological fatigue in ME/CFS. We
therefore focused our investigations on compound classes with the potential to regulate mitochondrial
metabolism and nutrient sensing.
Indoximod decreases the conversion of tryptophan to N-formyl-kynurenine by the indoleamine 2,3-
dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) enzyme. While the precise mechanism of action remains unclear, indoximod
appears to i) reduce tryptophan levels, resulting in the induction of nutrient-sensing signals and the
activation of mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), and ii) induce the ligand-activated
Page 13/34

transcription factor, aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) involved in the expression of genes mediating
immune responses (32). Thus, in addition to its ability to regulate mTORC1 metabolic pathways,
indoximod can also regulate immune responses. In order to investigate the metabolic consequences of
mTOR activation in the absence of immune transcription, we employed the mTORC1 agonist, MHY1485.
In addition, because one of the consequences of GSK3 inhibition is the activation of mTORC1, we also
included a GSK3 inhibitor, tideglusib, to further investigate perturbation of the mTOR pathway (33).
While timofibrate remains uncharacterized, the chemically-related fibrates, clofibrate and fenofibrate, are
prodrugs that are converted to clofibric acid and fenofibric acid, respectively, in the liver and function as
activators of PPARα and PPARg (34). While fibrates such as fenofibrate are used clinically in the
treatment of dyslipidemia associated with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and metabolic syndrome,
interpreting results from in vitro investigations, including C2C12 cells, can be difficult owing to cell-
specific differences in the expression of the esterases necessary for prodrug conversion to active
fenofibric acid (35). We therefore examined the more bioavailable PPAR agonists, pioglitazone and
rosiglitazone (36).
Analysis of mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP
production, and mitochondrial mass
We firstly investigated the impact of pioglitazone, rosiglitazone, tideglusib, and MHY1485 on
mitochondrial metabolism in undifferentiated C2C12 myoblasts. While pioglitazone, rosiglitazone, and
MHY1485 had no significant impact on mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP production, and
mitochondrial mass, there was a modest but significant increase in both mitochondrial membrane
potential (Fig. 5C) and ATP production (Fig. 5G) following treatment with tideglusib.
We further examined the effects of each compound on C2C12 differentiated myotubes. While neither
pioglitazone nor rosiglitazone affected mitochondrial membrane potential or ATP production at 24h, 1nM
pioglitazone significantly increased both mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production at 48h
(Fig. 6). Furthermore, 1nM of both Tideglusib and MHY1485 significantly increased mitochondrial
membrane potential or ATP production at 24h, however, this increase was lost at 48h (Fig. 6). The
increases in mitochondrial membrane potential (TMRE) or ATP production (luciferase) observed for
pioglitazone, tideglusib, and MHY1485 were independent of mitochondrial mass suggesting that the
response did not involve significant changes in mitochondrial biogenesis.
Analysis of ROS
In some cases, increased mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production (as observed in Fig. 5,
6) can be associated with increased ROS production (37). We therefore examined ROS production in
response to each compound. None of the four compounds significantly increased ROS production in
C2C12 myoblasts (Supplementary Fig. 2A). However, there was a significant, albeit modest, transient
increase in ROS production following 30 min of rosiglitazone and tideglusib in C2C12 myotubes
(Supplementary Fig. 2B).
Page 14/34

Analysis of C2C12 myoblast growth
We further examined the effects of the selected compounds on cell growth. While both 10nM
pioglitazone and 10nM rosiglitazone increased cell growth of C2C12 myoblasts at 48h and 72h (Fig. 7A,
7B), no significant increases were observed for tideglusib or MHY1485 (Fig. 7C, 7D). Furthermore, none
of the selected compounds had a significant effect on the growth of post-mitotic C2C12 myotubes
(Supplementary Fig. 3).
Discussion
Using two independent transcriptomic studies reported by Gow et. al. and Gamer et. al., we have
identified a set of overlapping DEGs in ME/CFS patients from which we have investigated potential
disease pathways and therapeutic avenues to treatment (20, 21). The overlapping gene expression
signature identified genes involved multiple pathways broadly related to immune dysfunction, oxidative
stress, apoptosis, and oxidative phosphorylation. Consistent with the post-infectious aetiology observed
in some patients, immune dysfunction has been widely reported in ME/CFS (24, 25). However, despite
such immune disruptions, the identification of reproducible cytokine or immune cell biomarkers in
ME/CFS patients has so far remained elusive (38).
Interestingly, our analysis identified a number of common overlapping DEGs with proposed roles in
metabolism including amino acid oxidation via the TCA cycle (SLC7a8, CAPS), oxidative stress (ARRDC1-
AS1), mitochondrial ATP production (Panx1), mitochondrial biogenesis (RAP2C), metabolic
reprogramming involving oxidative phosphorylation (UQCC3, CBX5), autophagy (BECN1) and the pentose
phosphate pathway (PGD) suggesting disruptions in cellular metabolism (Tables 1 and 2). Further
examination of the DEGs common to both datasets using the CMap L1000 database for gene-
enrichment signatures (i.e., genetic perturbagens) allowed the identification of an 83-gene ME/CFS
network (Supplementary Table 1). Given the post-infectious aetiology described for ME/CFS, it is
perhaps not surprising that this network included multiple modules related to infection, cytokine
signalling and immunity (Fig. 3). However, the identification of modules involving cellular metabolism
and redox regulation in the 83-gene network suggests that such pathways may also be disrupted in
ME/CFS (Fig. 3).
The molecular analysis of disease pathways has been widely employed to identify potential therapeutic
targets from which lead compounds can be identified and optimized using preclinical models. However,
for a range of chronic conditions such as ME/CFS, the lack of validated therapeutic targets, the unclear
disease aetiology, together with the lack of proven preclinical models pose profound obstacles to drug
discovery and validation. Within such a knowledge gap, therapeutic development of drugs for ME/CFS
has been largely based on empirical clinical trials in which existing drugs have been repurposed based
on clinical rationales rather than preclinical mechanistic evidence. We therefore examined an alternative
approach that does not require a priori knowledge of preclinical mechanism or disease aetiology in
which compounds are identified with the potential to induce gene signatures that are reciprocal (or
Page 15/34

inverse) to those observed in ME/CFS patients using the CMap L1000 platform (17). Such an approach
has been widely employed for the identification of compounds that have the potential to reverse disease
gene expression profiles and so could provide a novel therapeutic route for drug development (17–19,
22).
Consistent with our pathway mapping (Fig. 3), the majority of compounds identified using the CMap
L1000 database have known indications related to immunity, inflammation, and pain (Supplementary
Table 2, SB-705498, Mefenamic acid, Skimmianine, Indoximod, Ginkgolide-a, PRE-084, and VX-702).
However, despite the consistent association between immune dysfunction and ME/CFS, clinical trials
investigating immunotherapies such as intravenous immunoglobulin G, anti-histamines, hydrocortisone
and fludrocortisone, IL-1 antagonism, and B-cell ablation using rituximab have not, so far, demonstrated
sustained responses in the majority of patients (8, 26–28). One possible exception may be
cyclophosphamide, an alkylating agent widely used in cancer treatment, which was found to result in
significant symptom improvement and remissions in some patients (39). In addition, the significant
association of viral infections preceding the onset of ME/CFS has led to a number of clinical trials
investigating antiviral therapies, including rintatolimod and valganciclovir, with some significant
improvements (40, 41). However, the safety profiles and variable therapeutic windows of such drugs
remain an issue in terms of clinical translation.
While ME/CFS is associated with a range of autonomic impairments, our pathway analysis did not
identify neurological networks (Fig. 3). However, our CMap L1000 analysis identified drugs known to
modify neurotransmission in psychiatric conditions. For example, buspirone (Supplementary Table 2)
modifies serotonin and dopamine neurotransmission and is used clinically in the treatment of anxiety
and depression. Idalopirdine (Supplementary Table 2) modifies serotonin, glutamate, and cholinergic
neurotransmission and is used in the treatment of dementia and schizophrenia. While antidepressants,
including buspirone, have been used in the treatment of ME/CFS, their efficacy remains unclear (42). For
example, despite the reported involvement of the dopamine and serotonin pathways in ME/CFS, trials
examining ondansetron, duloxetine, phenelzine, and fluoxetine have not identified significant
improvements in patients (8, 28).
Our analysis identifying disruption of metabolic pathways in ME/CFS together the CMap L1000
identification of compounds known to be metabolic regulators, suggested that the targeting of cellular
metabolism may provide one therapeutic avenue for the treatment of ME/CFS. In examining the
metabolic PPAR agonists, pioglitazone and rosiglitazone, only pioglitazone significantly increased
mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production in C2C12 myotubes (Fig. 6). This increase did not
appear to be associated with the induction of mitochondrial biogenesis as we did not observe any
change in mitochondrial mass (Fig. 6). Furthermore, despite the increased mitochondrial membrane
potential and ATP production in response to pioglitazone, we did not observe increased ROS production
(Supplementary Fig. 2). While pioglitazone and rosiglitazone are PPARg agonists, they are also able to
activate PPARα at higher concentrations (36). It is noteworthy that pioglitazone is nearly 10-times less
potent as an activator for PPARg than rosiglitazone (36). Thus, it is possible that the ability of
Page 16/34

pioglitazone but not rosiglitazone to increase mitochondrial membrane potential in C2C12 myotubes
may be due to the activation of PPARα rather than PPARg. While such a proposal would be consistent
with the reported increase in PPARα mRNA expression following C2C12 myotube differentiation, further
work will be required to determine the mechanistic basis for the difference between pioglitazone and
rosiglitazone observed in our studies (43).
Pioglitazone and rosiglitazone are approved clinically in the treatment of T2DM. While their ability to
activate PPAR-mediated transcriptional regulation of metabolic genes such as Glucose Transporter Type
4, adiponectin and Acyl-CoA Synthetase Long Chain Family Member 1 are thought to underpin
improvements in insulin sensitivity, there is evidence that they can also improve mitochondrial
metabolism (44). For example, pioglitazone has been shown to enhance oxygen consumption and ATP
production in insulin-resistant C2C12 muscle cells (45). In part, the ability of PPAR agonists to increase
mitochondrial metabolism may be responsible for the increased growth capacity of C2C12 myoblasts
observed in Fig. 7. Others have shown the decrease in exercise capacity, ADP-dependent mitochondrial
respiration, complex I and III activities, and citrate synthase activity observed in high-fat diet diabetic
mice were all ameliorated following pioglitazone treatment (46). While it remains unclear whether
pioglitazone and rosiglitazone improve mitochondrial metabolism solely via PPAR-dependent pathways,
the discovery that pioglitazone can directly bind the MitoNEET protein to regulate oxidative capacity
suggests that multiple pathways may be involved (47).
The underlying molecular basis for the pathological fatigue experienced by ME/CFS patients remains
unclear. However, several lines of evidence suggest that impaired mitochondrial function may be a
contributing factor. Primary peripheral blood monocytic cells, primary skeletal muscle cells and
immortalized lymphocytes from ME/CFS patients exhibit a reduced mitochondrial coupling efficiency,
ATP synthesis and oxygen consumption rate compared to cells isolated from healthy controls (48, 49).
Furthermore, Mandarano et al. found a significant reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential in
primary CD8+ T cells isolated from ME/CFS patients (50). The reduced mitochondrial metabolism in
ME/CFS is proposed to result in altered metabolic profiles whereby compensatory anaplerosis in which
specific amino acids and metabolites are used to replenish TCA intermediates thereby bridging the
aerobic deficit (31). Additionally, Naviaux et. al. have shown that ME/CFS patients have abnormal
metabolite profiles in their peripheral blood that are consistent with an overall reduction in metabolic
capacity (23). Thus, given our findings that pioglitazone is able to increase mitochondrial membrane
potential in C2C12 myoblasts, future work will be important for determining whether it can restore
mitochondrial metabolism, including oxygen consumption and ATP production, in primary cells isolated
from ME/CFS patients.
To our knowledge, the ability of pioglitazone to improve pathological fatigue in patients has not been
reported. However, there is evidence that pioglitazone can improve skeletal muscle metabolism in vivo.
For example, pioglitazone significantly improved oxygen consumption, anaerobic threshold and skeletal
muscle fatty acid oxidation in a small cohort of metabolic syndrome patients subjected to bicycle
ergometer exercise testing (51). Furthermore, while oxygen consumption in skeletal muscle explants
Page 17/34

taken from T2DM patients was reduced compared to age- and BMI-matched control subjects, explants
taken from T2DM patients that had been treated with pioglitazone (but not rosiglitazone) demonstrated
partial restoration (52). In line with the observed impairment in mitochondrial metabolism in cells
isolated from ME/CFS patients, several studies have reported reduced oxygen consumption and
anaerobic thresholds in patients when measured by cardio-pulmonary exercise tests (29, 30). Thus,
future work examining the ability of pioglitazone to increase mitochondrial metabolism including
oxidative phosphorylation in primary cells obtained from ME/CFS patients may pave the way for future
clinical trials in which the re-purposing of pioglitazone for the treatment of ME/CFS could be considered.
Our results further showed that tideglusib induced a transient increase of mitochondrial membrane
potential and ATP production in both C2C12 myoblasts and myotubes (Fig. 5, 6). The increased
mitochondrial membrane potential in response to tideglusib did not appear to be due to mitochondrial
biogenesis, as mitochondrial mass remained unchanged (Fig. 5, 6). Tideglusib is a GSK-3β inhibitor that
is currently in clinical development for degenerative pathologies, including Alzheimer’s disease and
myotonic dystrophy (53, 54). While such conditions are known to be associated with protein aggregation
and proteasomal dysfunction, they are also characterized by impaired mitochondrial metabolism. For
example, in a study by Martin et. al., GSK3β inhibition both in cell lines and in mice increased
mitochondrial membrane potential and respiration (55). Importantly, these studies found that GSK3β
inhibition increased the stability of PPAR coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) that is a key regulator of the
PPARs (55). In part, such a pathway may facilitate the ability of PGC1α to increase aspartate and
glutamate levels, leading to improved energy utilization and mitochondrial respiration (56). Also
consistent with our findings, Theeuwes et. al. found that siRNA inhibition of GSK3β in C2C12 cells
increased mitochondrial respiration (57). Tideglusib has been shown to improve oxidative metabolism
and reduce muscle fatigue in a C57BL/10-mdx mouse model of muscular dystrophy (58). Similarly,
tideglusib demonstrated improvements in neuromuscular symptoms, including fatigue, in a Phase II
study of myotonic dystrophy type 1, although whether the improvement was mediated via mitochondrial
metabolism was not assessed (54). Given its favourable pharmacokinetic and toxicity profiles, further
investigation into the potential repurposing of tideglusib for the treatment of ME/CFS is warranted (54).
Crosstalk has been reported for GSK3 and mTORC1 in a number of cell types whereby GSK3 inhibition
can lead to mTORC1 activation and, reciprocally, mTORC1 activation can lead to GSK3 inactivation (59).
Given our findings with the GSK3 inhibitor, tideglusib, we also analysed the mTORC1 activator, MHY1485.
Similar to tideglusib, MHY1485 was also shown to transiently increase mitochondrial membrane
potential and ATP production in C2C12 myotubes in a manner that was independent of both
mitochondrial mass and ROS production (Fig. 6, Supplementary Fig. 2). Although both mTORC1 and
GSK3 are known to control cell growth and metabolism, neither MHY1485 nor tideglusib significantly
affected the growth of C2C12 myoblasts (Fig. 7). While our studies did not investigate the mechanisms
by which MHY1485 may influence mitochondrial membrane potential, Bantug et. al. reported that
mTORC signalling via GSK3 at mitochondria-ER contact sites promotes the TCA cycle flux that is
important for the effector functions of memory CD8+ T cells suggesting functional roles in mitochondrial
respiration (60). In addition, while the over-expression of the IRX5 homeobox transcription factor
Page 18/34

disrupted mitochondrial structure and impeded mitochondrial oxygen consumption in human bone
marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells, these effects could be partly overcome by MHY1485,
implicating a role for mTORC1 activation in mitochondrial function (61). It could be that the impaired
mitochondrial metabolism in ME/CFS is due to the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria as a
consequence of aberrant autophagy. BECN1 (Beclin-1), a key regulator of autophagy that integrates
signals from mTORC1, was identified as part of our ME/CFS disease signature (Table 1). Others have
reported that the autophagy-related protein, ATG13, is upregulated in the serum of ME/CFS patients,
suggesting impaired or abortive autophagy (62). While approaches that promote mTORC1 activity could
conceivably restore defective autophagic pathways, it is noteworthy that a single-arm clinical trial in
which ME/CFS patients were treated with a low dose of the mTORC1 inhibitor, rapamycin, reported
improvements in multiple symptoms, including fatigue and PEM, although the improvements did not
always correlate with autophagic markers such as BECN1 (63). Further work will be important in
determining whether the targeting of mTORC1 may be therapeutically beneficial in ME/CFS.
Our study has several limitations. Most notably, our study was performed in a single C2C12 muscle cell
line, and so there will be important differences in terms of the in vivo physiology in ME/CFS patients. In
addition, while the application of CMap has had some success in identifying candidate drugs for
repurposing in a number of pathologies, the drug-induced gene expression profiles within CMap were not
generated from cells derived from ME-CFS patients. Thus, the ability of a drug to revert an ME/CFS
disease signature in CMap cell lines may not translate to reversion in ME/CFS patients. Furthermore,
while we have examined short-term drug effects on mitochondrial metabolism and growth, we have not
examined the effects of each compound on the expression of ME/CFS disease signature genes. Future
work will be important in examining primary cells derived from ME/CFS patients and the ability of each
compound to revert the disease signature. Lastly, determinants of therapeutic efficacy are complex and
involve a range of factors beyond matching inverse compound-disease expression profiles. For example,
therapeutic responses can be determined by the bioavailability of the drug to target tissues and its
therapeutic window. Thus, further preclinical investigations will be important before randomized
prospective ME/CFS clinical trials could be considered.
Despite over four decades of research, ME/CFS remains a disease for which there is no clearly
understood mechanistic basis, no reproducible biomarkers, no clear therapeutic targets and no validated
pre-clinical models. Developing new drug discovery pipelines within such a ‘knowledge vacuum’ presents
substantial barriers to clinical translation. These issues are compounded by the escalating costs and
length of time required to deliver new therapies following conventional drug development pipelines that
involve target identification, lead development, in vitro evaluation, pre-clinical testing and clinical trials.
Even if such conventional pipelines identify a new drug with therapeutic potential, the current risk-
adverse economic climate and high failure rates can hinder development. For ME/CFS, an off-the-shelf
approach has predominated in which the majority of controlled drug trials have examined existing
immunotherapies that were selected on the basis that ME/CFS is an immune disease (64). Although
such quasi ad hoc approaches are important for orphan diseases such as ME/CFS, alternative
approaches in which drug repurposing based on the unbiased matching of reciprocal disease:drug gene
Page 19/34

expression signatures may offer important alternative routes to clinical trials. To date, reciprocal
matching of disease:drug expression signatures has been applied to a number of diseases with some
success (16, 18, 19). Thus, with a detailed understanding of ME/CFS aetiology, biomarkers, therapeutic
targets, and pre-clinical models still lacking, rational approaches to drug repurposing, such as described
herein, may be an attractive alternative to conventional drug development pathways.
To our knowledge, there are no approved drug therapies targeting pathological fatigue associated with
mitochondrial dysfunction. In the case of ME/CFS, while some dietary and nutritional supplements
aimed at improving mitochondrial function have been investigated, objective improvements have not
been observed (65). With pathological fatigue being a prevalent symptom associated with diverse
diseases, ‘metabolic’ drugs may have wide application provided that both efficacy and safety can be
demonstrated. In particular, with the finding that long-COVID demonstrates many of the clinical
hallmarks of ME/CFS including pathological fatigue, the approaches outlined herein may have wider
application, particularly for diseases for which traditional drug development pathways may not be
available and for which there is urgent unmet need (25).

Figure 1
Workflow. Drug repurposing scheme for diseases with unclear aetiology and no approved therapies. The
differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using two independent datasets (GSE14577 and
GSE227375). DEGs common to both datasets were subjected to Connectivity Map (CMAP) L1000 for the
identification of functional gene networks and potential therapeutic compounds for ME/CFS. The
compounds were further prioritized based on their targets and tested in vitro for their ability to improve
mitochondrial bioenergetics.
