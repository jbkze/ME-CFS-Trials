## Summary

A German team compared people with lasting fatigue and other symptoms after a COVID-19 infection to people with similar lasting symptoms that began after a COVID-19 vaccination rather than an infection. Both groups carried similar autoantibodies (antibodies that mistakenly attack the body's own receptors) against stress-hormone and nerve receptors, and many still had leftover spike protein — a leftover piece of the virus or vaccine — circulating in their blood, especially those whose symptoms began after vaccination.

## Why this matters

It suggests that chronic-fatigue-type illness after an infection and after a vaccination share the same underlying immune problem, supporting the idea that leftover viral/vaccine protein plus self-attacking antibodies — not the specific trigger — may drive the illness, regardless of whether it started with COVID-19 or the vaccine against it.

## Notes

Confirmed via Crossref (DOI resolves to medRxiv, posted 2026-09-23) and medRxiv lookup. Institute for Clinical Immunology, Marien Hospital Herne / University Hospital of the Ruhr-University Bochum — not Wirth/Scheibenbogen. Cross-sectional study: 28 PACS (post-acute COVID-19 syndrome) patients, 29 PACVS (post-acute COVID-19 vaccination syndrome) patients, 16 controls; found substantial clinical and immunological overlap, including GPCR (G-protein coupled receptor) autoantibodies and altered T-cell responses in both groups, with circulating spike protein detected in both (more often in the vaccinated group).
