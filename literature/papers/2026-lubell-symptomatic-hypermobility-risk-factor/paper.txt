Abstract 
45 
Background 
46 
In a retrospective online survey, we assessed the extent to which people with symptomatic 
47 
hypermobility are at risk of Long COVID with a high degree of post-exertional symptom 
48 
exacerbation, a form of Long COVID similar to myalgic encephalomyelitis.  
49 
Methods 
50 
Participants were 1,816 adults with prior COVID-19 infection; 19.4% reported Long COVID, 
51 
defined as symptoms persisting ≥3 months. Survey measures identified Long COVID with high 
52 
post-exertional symptom exacerbation, generalized joint hypermobility (GJH), extreme 
53 
hypermobility, and a pre-COVID orthostatic/neurocognitive symptom burden (ONS profile). 
54 
Logistic regression assessed whether ONS profile and hypermobility, together defined as 
55 
symptomatic hypermobility, were associated with increased risk of Long COVID with post-
56 
exertional symptom exacerbation. 
57 
Results 
58 
In the full sample, both extreme hypermobility (OR 3.15, 95 % CI 2.00-4.95) and an ONS profile 
59 
pre-COVID (OR 3.29, 95% CI 2.34-4.61) were strongly predictive of Long COVID with high post-
60 
exertional symptom exacerbation. These effects were cumulative, leading to an OR of 9.46 
61 
(95% CI 4.93-18.17) for people with both conditions. People who both had an ONS profile pre-
62 
COVID and had generalized joint hypermobility also had a higher risk of Long COVID with high 
63 
post-exertional symptom exacerbation (OR 5.54, 95% CI 3.51-8.75).  
64 
Conclusions 
65 
In this dataset, people with symptomatic hypermobility were at high risk of Long COVID with 
66 
high levels of post-exertional symptom exacerbation. Further research is needed to understand 
67 
the biological mechanisms of viral-onset illness to promote more effective and targeted 
68 
treatments tailored to the disease pathways shared by groups of individuals with common 
69 
vulnerabilities.

In an earlier analysis of data from an online survey of 1,816 adults in the United States and 
72 
United Kingdom (352 reporting Long COVID and 1464 reporting no Long COVID), we showed 
73 
that both generalized joint hypermobility (GJH) and extreme hypermobility were significant risk 
74 
factors for developing Long COVID.1 Other research has similarly found an association between 
75 
GJH and Long COVID.2,3 In this paper, we refine our earlier analysis by assessing the extent to 
76 
which people with symptomatic GJH and symptomatic extreme hypermobility are at risk of a 
77 
Long COVID presentation that approximates myalgic encephalomyelitis (ME; sometimes 
78 
referred to as myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome; ME/CFS): Long COVID with 
79 
a high degree of post-exertional symptom exacerbation (PESE). 
80 
People with GJH have joints that move beyond the normal range, often due to lax ligaments 
81 
from variant connective tissue in the body. GJH is estimated to affect approximately 10 to 20% 
82 
of the general population,4 with rates up to 78.8% in people with complex chronic conditions, 
83 
including ME.5  
84 
Not everyone with GJH develops symptoms related to their hypermobility. Symptomatic GJH6 is 
85 
characterised by musculoskeletal manifestations such as chronic or widespread pain,7-9 
86 
recurrent subluxations, joint instability, and musculoskeletal overuse injuries (including 
87 
tendinitis, bursitis).10 Approximately two-thirds6 of people with symptomatic GJH11 also report 
88 
multisystemic symptoms,12 including physical and cognitive fatigue,7,8,13,14orthostatic 
89 
intolerance15,16 and/or postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS),16,17 along with 
90 
gastrointestinal symptoms18 and features consistent with mast cell activation syndrome.19 
91 
Many people with symptomatic hypermobility are diagnosed with hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos 
92 
Syndrome (hEDS) or another less common form of Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome, or one of several 
93 
hypermobility spectrum disorders (HSD). The estimated combined diagnosed prevalence of 
94 
hEDS/HSD is between 1 in 500 to 1 in 900 individuals,20,21 though some argue it is inconsistently 
95 
diagnosed.11  
96 
A number of studies have found high rates of hEDS, HSD and GJH among people with ME,22-25 
97 
suggesting that people with symptomatic hypermobility may be at higher risk of developing this 
98 
severe chronic condition. Although the extent to which ME and Long COVID overlap remains 
99 
debated, growing evidence suggests that many people with long COVID have a condition 
100 
closely resembling, or effectively identical to, ME.1,26,27 One of the signature characteristics of 
101 
ME is post-exertional neuroimmune exhaustion,28 often referred to as post-exertional malaise. 
102 
While our dataset is not large or comprehensive enough to assess whether symptomatic 
103 
hypermobility is a risk factor for the multifaceted post-exertional neuroimmune exhaustion, we 
104 
are able to assess whether people with hypermobility who were burdened before getting COVID 
105 
by some of the systemic symptoms often experienced by people with hEDS are at greater risk of 
106 
Long COVID with PESE. PESE is a worsening of symptoms after exertion and is one of the 
107 
constituent components of post-exertional neuroimmune exhaustion.  
108 
In this paper, we examine whether having a high burden from orthostatic or neurocognitive 
109 
symptoms pre-COVID plus either GJH or extreme hypermobility predicts Long COVID and Long 
110 
COVID with high PESE. In addition to considering the results for the full sample, we examine the 
111 
results for two sub-groups defined by the severity of initial symptoms during the acute phase of

115 
Methods 
116 
Setting 
117 
This analysis is based on data collected through a survey conducted online via the platform 
118 
Qualtrics in early 2024. The study was approved by the Research Ethics and Governance 
119 
Committee of the Brighton and Sussex Medical School (ER/BSMS9B02/6/4). 
120 
Public and Patient Involvement 
121 
The authorship team includes individuals with hypermobility and Long COVID, as well as a 
122 
patient caregiver. The questionnaire was refined using the suggestions of additional individuals 
123 
with Long COVID. 
124 
Participants 
125 
The dataset includes survey responses from 1,816 adults in the United States and United 
126 
Kingdom who reported at least one COVID-19 infection at least three months before completing 
127 
the survey. 352 respondents reported Long COVID and 1464 reported no Long COVID. We 
128 
recruited respondents from representative online panels maintained by the data firm Dynata. 
129 
For more details on the survey and survey recruitment, see Torok et al. 2025.1 
130 
Outcomes 
131 
Long COVID 
132 
We identified respondents as having Long COVID based on their self-identification in response 
133 
to the survey question “Have you been diagnosed with Long COVID, or do you have symptoms 
134 
that you attribute to Long COVID? For the purposes of this survey, people have Long COVID 
135 
when they experience symptoms related to their COVID-19 infection at least three months after 
136 
their initial infection.”  
137 
Long COVID with high PESE  
138 
We identified Long COVID with high PESE based on responses to the following question asked 
139 
of people who self-identified as having Long COVID: “Do you CURRENTLY experience a 
140 
worsening of your symptoms up to 48 hours after you engage in even a small amount of exertion 
141 
(e.g., doing basic housework)?” Responses were indicated by a slider from 0 (“not a problem) to 
142 
100 (“an extremely severe problem.”) We categorized respondents who had Long COVID and 
143 
provided a response to this question at or above the median response (for those with Long 
144 
COVID) of 20.5 as having Long COVID with high PESE.

Subgroups 
146 
GJH and Extreme Hypermobility 
147 
We identified respondents as having GJH using the Hakim-Grahame self-report questionnaire 
148 
(5PQ), where a score of 2 or greater indicates presence of GJH. We assessed extreme 
149 
hypermobility by summing the number of affirmative responses to 9 questions – the 5PQ plus 4 
150 
additional questions. We identified respondents in the top 10th percentile for their age and 
151 
gender category as having extreme hypermobility. See Torok et. al. 2025 for more details.29 All of 
152 
the individuals with extreme hypermobility also have GJH. 
153 
ONS Profile Pre-COVID and Symptomatic Hypermobility 
154 
We assessed the extent to which respondents report being burdened before getting COVID-19 
155 
by one of the orthostatic or neurocognitive symptoms commonly experienced by people with 
156 
hEDS (ONS profile). We identify respondents as having an ONS profile pre-COVID if their self-
157 
reported pre-COVID-19 levels of any of four specific symptoms – fatigue, dizziness, cognitive 
158 
impairment or inability to remain upright – were in the top tenth percentile of survey responses. 
159 
These questions used a slider and were phrased similarly to the question about PESE 
160 
referenced above. We identified respondents as having symptomatic GJH if they had both an 
161 
ONS profile pre-COVID and GJH. We identified respondents who had an ONS profile pre-COVID 
162 
and had extreme hypermobility as having symptomatic extreme hypermobility. We use the 
163 
phrase symptomatic hypermobility to refer to respondents with either symptomatic GJH or 
164 
symptomatic extreme hypermobility.

COVID-19 Symptoms During Active Infection(s) 
170 
In addition to examining whether and to what extent respondents reported having an ONS

Statistical Analysis 
178 
 
179 
We used Jamovi version 2.3.28 and IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 to analyze unweighted data.

181

Logistic Regressions 
188 
We used a series of separate binomial logistic regressions to test our hypothesis that 
189 
hypermobility and symptomatic hypermobility are predictive of Long COVID with high PESE. 
190 
Each of these regressions controlled for age, sex at birth, number of infections (up to 3), and 
191 
number of vaccine doses, factors previously shown to affect the risk of Long COVID.30 
192 
We conducted four separate regressions of the full sample; each regression had Long COVID 
193 
with high PESE as the dependent variable and included the common covariates noted above 
194 
plus additional covariates noted below: 
195 
The first regression included these predictors: GJH and ONS profile pre-COVID. We also 
196 
included the interaction term of these two predictors because it was significant (before 
197 
Bonferroni correction). 
198 
The second regression included these covariates: symptomatic GJH, ONS profile pre-COVID 
199 
but without GJH, and GJH without ONS profile pre-COVID. This allowed for an estimation of the 
200 
impact of symptomatic GJH. 
201 
The third regression included these covariates: extreme hypermobility and ONS profile pre-
202 
COVID. We excluded the interaction term of these two covariates because it was not 
203 
statistically significant. 
204 
The fourth regression included these covariates: symptomatic extreme hypermobility, ONS 
205 
profile pre-COVID but without extreme hypermobility, and extreme hypermobility without ONS 
206 
profile pre-COVID. This allowed for an estimation of the impact of symptomatic extreme 
207 
hypermobility. 
208 
We repeated these four regressions for each of two subgroups: respondents reporting mild or 
209 
no initial symptoms during (all of) their COVID-19 infection(s) and respondents reporting severe 
210 
initial symptoms during (any of) their COVID-19 infection(s). This yielded a total of 12 separate 
211 
binomial regressions with Long COVID with high PESE as the dependent variable. Because the 
212 
interaction term in the first regression was significant for the full sample (before Bonferonni 
213 
correction), we included it in the first regression for each of the subgroups as well. 
214 
We also conducted the same 12 binomial regressions using Long COVID as a dependent 
215 
variable, rather than Long COVID with high PESE. We assessed the interaction terms but 
216 
omitted them because they were non-significant in all regressions.  
217 
We corrected for multiple comparisons using a Bonferroni correction (p=0.05/12 equals the 
218 
corrected significance of p=0.004). 
219 
We also converted the odds ratios associated with developing Long COVID with high PESE to 
220 
risk ratios using standard formulas. We computed the estimated incidence of Long COVID with 
221 
high PESE for people with symptomatic GJH and symptomatic extreme hypermobility by 
222 
multiplying the risk ratios by the percentages for the comparison populations. These incidences 
223 
thus reflect the regression-adjusted incidence of Long COVID with high PESE, rather than the

Discussion 
312 
In this retrospective survey study, symptomatic hypermobility emerged as an unambiguous and 
313 
clinically crucial predictor of Long COVID with post-exertional symptom exacerbation, a 
314 
phenotype closely overlapping with ME. Extreme hypermobility and an ONS profile pre-COVID 
315 
were each independently associated with increased risk, with a marked cumulative effect. In 
316 
contrast, generalized joint hypermobility alone was not predictive of Long COVID with post-
317 
exertional symptom exacerbation, underscoring that it is the combination of hypermobility with 
318 
multisystem symptom burden that generates the greatest vulnerability. 
319 
Strikingly, these associations were especially strong among individuals with mild or no 
320 
symptoms during the acute phase of COVID-19 infection. This underscores the importance of 
321 
looking beyond initial disease severity in identifying predictors of long-term outcomes. In 
322 
people with mild or no initial symptoms who had symptomatic hypermobility, risk increased up 
323 
to 13-fold, confirming the important role that host vulnerability plays in influencing Long COVID 
324 
risk. This has direct implications for triage and early management: patients who appear “low 
325 
risk” at point of infection may, in fact, require closer follow-up and screening for symptomatic 
326 
hypermobility. 
327 
The pathogenesis of Long COVID in people with no or mild initial symptoms may well differ from 
328 
that of people with severe initial symptoms, with the two groups representing separate 
329 
phenotypes. Progress toward effective pharmacological treatments for the various forms of 
330 
Long COVID will likely require stratification of the patient population according to Long COVID 
331 
phenotype.31 
332 
Mechanistically, these findings are consistent with models in which baseline dysautonomia, 
333 
vascular laxity, immune dysregulation, and impaired tissue resilience amplify the downstream 
334 
impact of viral infection (as summarized in our earlier publication).1 These pathways have been 
335 
implicated in both hypermobility syndromes and ME,27 and offer a plausible shared substrate 
336 
for post-exertional symptom exacerbation. While causal inference is not possible here, the 
337 
convergence of epidemiology and pathophysiology strengthens the case for targeted 
338 
investigation.  
339 
 
340 
More broadly, these results underscore the importance of considering the interaction between 
341 
infection and pre-existing biological vulnerability. Symptomatic hypermobility is one such 
342 
vulnerability: common, under-recognised, and clinically identifiable.

348 
Strengths and Limitations 
349 
As discussed in our previous publication,1 strengths of our survey include recruitment from a 
350 
representative sample, use of a validated questionnaire (the 5PQ) that enables comparisons 
351 
with other datasets and the inclusion of additional questions about hypermobility in other body 
352 
parts; limitations include the use of self-reported retrospective data and the lack of controls for 
353 
co-morbid conditions (which will be addressed in a subsequent analysis), and socioeconomic 
354 
factors. 
355 
In addition to these points, it is important to emphasize the constraints of sample size for 
356 
subgroup analysis. By definition, based on our methodology, no more than 10% of the sample 
357 
has extreme hypermobility and only about 20% have an ONS profile pre-COVID-19; as a result, 
358 
only 48 respondents have symptomatic extreme hypermobility. While this group is more likely 
359 
to have Long COVID than other respondents, there are nevertheless only 22 who have Long 
360 
COVID and 18 who have Long COVID with high PESE. The relatively small numbers in these 
361 
subgroups help explain why there are wide confidence intervals around our estimates. It is 
362 
important to replicate this analysis with a larger dataset that can enable more precise 
363 
estimates. Conducting similar analyses with other datasets that include observations before 
364 
individuals developed COVID-19 will help overcome the limitations imposed by reliance on 
365 
retrospective data. 
366 
Another limitation specific to this analysis is that our proxy for an ME-type presentation of Long 
367 
COVID only focuses on PESE and not the many other factors that go into a diagnosis of ME. This 
368 
is driven largely by sample size, which prevented further refinement of this definition to include 
369 
other factors that might have further constrained who meets the definition, and by the absence 
370 
of direct observations or medical records to supplement the data we gathered through a 
371 
retrospective survey. 
372 
One final limitation relates to the term “symptomatic hypermobility.” Many and perhaps most 
373 
people with symptomatic hypermobility have one of the four orthostatic or neurocognitive 
374 
symptoms used here to identify people with an ONS profile pre-COVID. But the term 
375 
“symptomatic hypermobility” is also used to describe people with more localized symptoms, 
376 
such as frequent joint subluxations. Our analysis of symptomatic hypermobility is limited to 
377 
people in our sample with hypermobility and an ONS profile pre-COVID. 
378 
Conclusion 
379 
The adverse continuing consequences of viral illnesses after the infectious period, as 
380 
exemplified by Long COVID, remain a significant public health issue and a considerable burden 
381 
to the individual and society. Unfortunately, with general interest in COVID-19 waning, millions 
382 
are living with its long-term effects with few targeted treatment programs. This research adds to 
383 
the growing literature underlining biological mechanisms of Long COVID, which increasingly
