Abstract
Objectives
Post-exertional malaise (PEM) is increasingly understood not as a singular symptom but as a
multidimensional exertion-response pattern involving diverse triggers, variable onset, and prolonged
recovery. This study aimed to develop and content-validate the Vienna Post-Exertional Malaise (VPEM)
Questionnaire, a multidimensional patient-reported outcome measure for PEM in myalgic
encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), designed to assess the consequences of PEM,
including functional decline, prolonged recovery, new crash-associated symptoms, and pacing-related
adaptations.
Methods
An iterative mixed-methods instrument-development study was conducted, combining quantitative
ratings and qualitative feedback across sequential evaluation cycles involving individuals with lived
experience and an interdisciplinary expert panel. An initial 13-item draft, derived from PEM, fatigue, and
functional-capacity instruments, as well as consensus literature, was evaluated by individuals with lived
experience of ME/CFS or Long COVID using Likert ratings, open-ended feedback, and semi-structured
interviews. Quantitative data were summarised descriptively; qualitative data were analysed using
deductive–inductive structuring content analysis. Revisions were agreed upon by a core development
team and documented in revision matrices. The revised instrument version was evaluated by an
interdisciplinary expert panel using domain-level ratings and item-level ratings of relevance and clarity.
Results
Twelve individuals with ME/CFS and one individual with Long COVID who also fulfilled ME/CFS criteria
evaluated the questionnaire. Ratings showed high approval for questionnaire length (92.3%) and
terminology (76.9%), while ratings for completeness and clarity were more heterogeneous. Qualitative
analysis revealed conceptual ambiguities concerning the distinction between PEM and non-specific
fatigue, the absence of trigger domains such as sensory, orthostatic and positively valenced emotional
triggers, and inadequate representation of intra-individual variability. Following revision, the expert panel
(n = 11) rated PEM coverage as either complete or largely complete (100%), and the multidimensional
structure as either suitable or very suitable (90.9%). Item-level ratings revealed high content validity in
terms of relevance (S-CVI/Ave = 0.96) and acceptable clarity (S-CVI/Ave = 0.82). The complete revised
questionnaire was subsequently pretested online with five participants.
Conclusions
Page 3/28

The VPEM Questionnaire operationalises PEM as a multidimensional pattern of post-exertional
deterioration. Findings support initial content and face validity and provide the basis for psychometric
validation.
Key Points for Decision-Makers
This study developed and initially content-validated the Vienna Post-Exertional Malaise Questionnaire, a
patient-centred patient-reported outcome measure for assessing post-exertional deterioration in adults
with ME/CFS.
Direct input from individuals with lived experience substantially shaped the instrument, including its
terminology, trigger domains, construct demarcation, and pacing context items.
The VPEM Questionnaire provides a transparent basis for subsequent psychometric validation and may
support more standardised assessment of PEM in ME/CFS and Long COVID populations with PEM.
Introduction
Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) and Long COVID are complex and often
disabling conditions characterised by post-exertional malaise (PEM), a pathological response to often
trivial levels of exertion that involves an abnormal multisystem and multisymptomatic exacerbation.[1]
This response can lead to substantial functional impairment and, in many cases, severe disability.[1, 2]
PEM can be described as a complex, multidimensional and context-dependent phenomenon. It is
characterised by a variety of triggers, including physical, cognitive, sensory, emotional or social, and
orthostatic demands, as well as variable onset latencies, prolonged and often incomplete recovery, and a
broad multisystem symptom profile.[3, 4].
PEM has been described as a multidimensional construct, involving variability in triggers, symptom
exacerbation, delayed onset, and recovery trajectories. PEM is the worsening of general symptoms, or
the development of new symptoms, typically within 48 hr after physical, cognitive or mental exertion,
above a patient- and time-specific threshold, that can last for days to weeks or even months. Patients
experience PEM in a variety of symptoms and severities, but a standard way to capture this in research
and clinics is missing. A clearer understanding of whether post-exertional malaise reflects a primary
pathophysiological process or a downstream, system-level manifestation of the primary
pathophysiological mechanisms remains an important objective for future research, as its underlying
biology is not fully established.[5, 6] To fully understand the pathology of post-exertional malaise
requires robust, standardised measurement approaches that can accommodate the heterogeneity and
complexity of PEM [7, 8], but also to link patients symptoms to underlying cellular and molecular
pathways, and to better guide future therapeutic interventions. At the same time, validated instruments
are needed to systematically assess the clinically observable consequences of PEM, including functional
decline, delayed or prolonged recovery, and crash-associated symptom changes. Several instruments
have been developed to assess these features.[8, 9] The DePaul Symptom Questionnaire PEM subscale
Page 4/28

(DSQ-PEM) has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity in identifying and diagnosing PEM-
related symptoms; however, it primarily assesses symptom frequency and severity while providing
limited coverage of aspects such as trigger diversity, delayed onset, and recovery dynamics.[10] The
newer DSQ-PEM-2 is a shorter alternative addressing some of these limitations, but it remains in an early
stage of validation, and the use of initial screening items may influence case identification depending on
symptom attribution.[11] The Functional Capacity Scale (FunCap-27) assesses activity-related fatigue
and performance decrements across multiple domains. It can indirectly reflect aspects of PEM-related
functional impairment, yet it does not explicitly operationalise or quantify the core features of PEM itself.
[12] Furthermore, qualitative research suggests that terminology commonly used in existing instruments,
such as “fatigue” or “exhaustion”, does not always reflect patients’ lived experience, which is often
described using terms such as a “crash” or flu-like deterioration. This mismatch may affect both
comprehensibility and content validity.[13]
As such, unidimensional or purely symptom-focused assessments do not fully meet current clinical and
research needs. Adequate capture of PEM requires a patient-centred, multidimensional instrument that
systematically addresses trigger profiles, temporal characteristics, functional impact, associated
symptoms, and behavioural adaptations such as pacing.[13] Here, we developed and initially content-
validated a multidimensional questionnaire assessing the clinically observable consequences of PEM in
adults with ME/CFS, including functional decline, recovery dynamics, crash-associated symptoms, and
pacing-related adaptations. We integrated the perspectives of individuals with lived experience of PEM
and interdisciplinary experts across two iterative triangulation cycles, focusing particularly on content
and face validity.
Methods
This study used an iterative mixed-methods approach to initially validate the content of a
multidimensional questionnaire designed to assess the phenotypic manifestations and systemic
consequences of PEM in people with ME/CFS. The development process was informed by established
guidance for PROM development, including the COSMIN methodology for content validity and
methodological recommendations for scale development.[14] This phase primarily focused on
evaluating content validity, operationalised as relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility, as
well as face validity from the perspectives of people with lived experience of ME/CFS or PCS and expert
stakeholders. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Medical University of
Vienna (EK No. 1192/2025). All participants provided written informed consent before participation.
Participants and recruitment
The questionnaire was evaluated by adults with ME/CFS and one participant with Long COVID, as well as
by an interdisciplinary expert panel. Participants were aged 18 or over and reported a physician
diagnosis of ME/CFS or Long COVID based on established consensus criteria.[15] Participants with
Long COVID were included only if they reported PEM and fulfilled ME/CFS criteria. Recruitment was
Page 5/28

conducted in collaboration with patient organisations to maximise heterogeneity regarding disease
duration, sociodemographic characteristics, and functional severity. The degree of functional impairment
among the ME/CFS participants was assessed using the Bell Disability Score. To ensure the inclusion of
individuals with severe functional impairment, flexible participation formats were offered. These
included written or audio-based feedback, assistance from relatives or caregivers, and the option to split
task completion over multiple shorter sessions. The expert panel included professionals from neurology,
internal medicine, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, rehabilitation, and exercise science, as well as
researchers with expertise in ME/CFS. The panel was selected to cover clinical and rehabilitative
perspectives relevant to the assessment of PEM.
Phase 1: Item pool generation
As a first step, the authors generated an initial item pool based on a structured literature review and
existing validated instruments assessing PEM, fatigue, and functional limitations. Specifically, items and
domains from the DSQ-PEM[11], the CDC Symptom Inventory[16], the Chalder Fatigue Questionnaire
(CFQ)[17], and FUNCAP-27[12] were reviewed and adapted where appropriate. Furthermore, conceptual
considerations derived from consensus documents (including the EUROMENE and D-A-CH frameworks)
[15] and the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF)[18] were incorporated.
This ensured an adequate representation of real-life functional demands and contextual factors. Based
on these sources, a preliminary 13-item questionnaire was developed. The initial instrument was
designed to capture four key dimensions of PEM: (1) triggers and contextual factors, (2) symptom
manifestation, (3) functional limitations in daily life, and (4) temporal dynamics, including onset and
recovery patterns. This version served as the foundation for subsequent evaluation.
Phase 2: Participatory evaluation with affected individuals
Data collection
The initial questionnaire version was evaluated through a participatory process involving individuals with
lived experience of ME/CFS and PCS. Data collection employed a multimethod approach combining
quantitative and qualitative components to comprehensively assess content validity, face validity, and
usability from the lived experience perspective. Participants first completed a structured feedback form
capturing sociodemographic characteristics, functional severity (assessed via the Bell Disability Score),
and systematic evaluations of the questionnaire. Quantitative evaluation comprised eight statements
(F1–F8) rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), assessing four
quality dimensions: content relevance (F1, F2), comprehensiveness (F3, F8), comprehensibility/clarity
(F4, F5), and practicability (F6, F7). Qualitative feedback was gathered through six open-ended questions
addressing: (1) missing content relevant to PEM, (2) unclear or ambiguous formulations, (3)
appropriateness and differentiation of response options, (4) perceived burden during completion
(cognitive, emotional, physical), (5) concrete suggestions for improvement, and (6) additional
Page 6/28

comments. In addition, semi-structured interviews were conducted with four participants to explore item
interpretation, cognitive response processes, and subjective experiences of PEM more in-depth. These
interviews, lasting 45–60 minutes, were conducted via Microsoft Teams in June 2025, audio-recorded,
and transcribed verbatim.
Data analysis
Qualitative data were analysed using structuring qualitative content analysis, following the approach
outlined by Kuckartz and Rädiker.[19] A combined deductive–inductive approach was applied. Initial
categories were based on the evaluation domains of content and face validity, the feedback questions,
and the interview guide. These categories were expanded during analysis through inductively developed
subcategories. Coding and data management were supported by MAXQDA 24 Analytics Pro (VERBI
Software, Berlin, Germany). Quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated through methodological
triangulation after each evaluation cycle. Revision decisions were made by a three-member core
development team in regular consensus meetings. In these meetings, quantitative rating patterns,
qualitative themes, individual comments, and feasibility considerations were jointly reviewed. Proposed
changes were classified as adopted, partially adopted, not adopted, or noted. Each decision was
documented in a revision matrix with a corresponding rationale. Decisions were guided by construct
fidelity, clinical interpretability, content validity, and questionnaire economy. This procedure was applied
to both the evaluation by individuals with lived experience and the expert panel evaluation.
Instrument revision: Cycle 1
After completing the evaluation by individuals with lived experience, the three-member core development
team (AK, AAS, and TP) reviewed the quantitative ratings and qualitative findings during consensus
meetings. Revisions focused on conceptual, linguistic, and structural aspects. The revision matrix
documented how each feedback category was translated into item modifications, retained as contextual
information or excluded from the core instrument. The resulting revised version served as the basis for
the subsequent expert panel evaluation.
Phase 3: Expert-based validation
The interdisciplinary expert panel subsequently evaluated the revised questionnaire. Data were collected
using a structured bilingual feedback form. The quantitative evaluation comprised 16 items rated on five-
point scales covering content validity, comprehensibility, practicability, methodological appropriateness,
target-group specificity, and overall assessment. In addition, experts rated each item for relevance and
clarity. Qualitative feedback was collected through open-ended response fields and systematically
documented. Quantitative data were analysed descriptively. Qualitative comments were analysed using
structuring qualitative content analysis, following Kuckartz and Rädiker.[19] Following the expert panel
evaluation, the core development team again reviewed quantitative ratings and qualitative comments
during consensus meetings. Revisions were documented in a second revision matrix and focused on
conceptual consolidation, linguistic clarification, terminology standardisation, and resolution of
Page 7/28

inconsistencies. Changes were implemented only when they improved construct clarity, feasibility, or
standardisation without expanding the instrument beyond the core PEM construct.
Phase 4: Linguistic review, translation and pretest
After the expert-panel revisions, the final German version of the questionnaire was reviewed by a German
linguist. The questionnaire was then translated into English by a second linguist, who performed a
bilingual equivalence check to ensure conceptual consistency between the German and English
instrument versions. To assess the feasibility of the technical implementation, the revised questionnaire
was pre-tested with five participants using the SoSci Survey online platform
(https://www.soscisurvey.de/). The aim of this phase was to identify potential issues with display,
navigation and usability in an online format. Feedback from this pretest led to minor adjustments to the
layout and user guidance. The resulting version is the final, revised VPEM Questionnaire, ready for
subsequent psychometric validation.
Statistical analysis
Quantitative data were analysed descriptively. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and
percentages. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations or medians and
interquartile ranges, depending on distribution and interpretability. Likert-scale responses from the
target-group evaluation were summarised as frequencies and percentages and, for descriptive
interpretation, grouped into disagreement, neutral, and agreement categories. Expert ratings were
summarised across all response categories; where appropriate, the two highest response categories
were combined to indicate a favourable evaluation.
For item-level expert ratings, content validity was additionally quantified using the item-level Content
Validity Index (I-CVI). Ratings were provided on a 5-point scale, with 1 indicating the lowest and 5 the
highest rating. For relevance and clarity ratings, scores of 4 or 5 were classified as favourable. The I-CVI
was calculated as the proportion of experts who provided a favourable rating for each item, with the full
expert panel size (N = 11) as the denominator. The scale-level Content Validity Index using the average
method (S-CVI/Ave) was calculated as the mean of the item-level I-CVI values across all evaluated items.

Quantitative ratings across the eight evaluation items (F1–F8) from the patient cohort (N = 13) are
presented in Fig. 1. Participants reported the highest levels of agreement on the appropriateness of the
overall questionnaire length (F6: 92.3%, n = 12) and the comprehensibility of the terminology (F5: 76.9%,
Page 9/28

n = 10). Substantial agreement was also observed for the logical ordering of items (F7: 61.5%, n = 8) and
relevance to personal experience with ME/CFS or PCS (F2: 53.8%, n = 7). Conversely, ratings were more
heterogeneous for clarity of wording (F4: 46.2% agreement, n = 6; 30.8% disagreement, n = 4),
correspondence with personal PEM experience (F1: 46.2% agreement, n = 6; 38.5% disagreement, n = 5),
and coverage of relevant PEM aspects (F3: 46.2% agreement, n = 6; 38.5% disagreement, n = 5). The
most critical feedback pertained to whether the questionnaire allowed for the full expression of
individual PEM experiences (F8). The majority of participants disagreed with this item (53.8%, n = 7),
while only 38.5% (n = 5) endorsed it.
Qualitative findings from participants with ME/CFS or PCS
Qualitative feedback was obtained from feedback questionnaires, document annotations, semi-
structured interviews, and supplementary written submissions from 13 participants with ME/CFS or
PCS. Structuring content analysis yielded six main categories. Conceptual criticism was most frequent
(130 segments; 11 contributors), followed by missing content (83 segments; 12 contributors), linguistic
criticism (76 segments; 12 contributors), structural criticism (24 segments; 10 contributors), positive
feedback (23 segments; 10 contributors), and burden of completion (9 segments; 6 contributors). Full
subcategory-level results and illustrative quotations from the target population evaluation are provided in
Supplementary File S1. Conceptual criticism mainly concerned the definition and representation of PEM.
Participants described PEM as variable within and between individuals, including differences in crash
thresholds, symptom patterns, latency, intensity, duration, and recovery. They also criticised wording that
could be interpreted as equating PEM with fatigue, exhaustion, or psychological symptoms. Missing
content mainly concerned trigger domains and contextual factors that participants considered
insufficiently represented in the initial draft. Sensory stimuli, orthostatic load, and positively valenced
emotional arousal were repeatedly identified as missing or underrepresented triggers. Additional
comments referred to pacing or coping strategies, medication effects, aggravating factors and the
possibility of incomplete or lasting deterioration after PEM episodes. Linguistic criticism focused on
terminology and precision. Terms such as “fatigue”, “exhaustion”, “tiredness”, and “weakness” were
perceived by several participants as insufficient to describe PEM. Participants also criticised vague
references to exertion levels, duration or frequency and noted that some symptom descriptors did not
match their experience. Structural criticism was less frequent and mainly concerned with response
formats, layout, and readability. Positive feedback referred to the overall coherence, structure, and
feasibility of the questionnaire. Burden of completion was reported less often and was mainly cognitive,
particularly due to recalling and reflecting on recent symptom patterns.
Phase 3: Expert panel quantitative ratings
The interdisciplinary expert panel consisted of 11 professionals with substantive experience in ME/CFS
clinical care and research. The medical field was represented by five clinicians, including three
neurologists (27.3%) and two cardiologists (18.2%). Allied health and rehabilitation professions were
Page 10/28

represented by six experts, comprising four exercise scientists or rehabilitation therapists (36.4%), one
physiotherapist (9.1%), and one occupational therapist (9.1%).
The quantitative expert ratings are presented in Tables 2 and 3. Overall, the revised questionnaire was
rated favourably across all evaluated domains. For content validity, all experts rated PEM coverage as
largely complete (n = 5, 45.5%) or complete (n = 6, 54.5%). The five core dimensions, PEM frequency,
exertion tolerance, functional decline, recovery and new symptoms, were rated as suitable or very
suitable by 10 experts (90.9%). The eight-week recall period showed more variation: four experts (36.4%)
rated it as suitable or very suitable, six (54.5%) as acceptable and one (9.1%) as rather unsuitable. The
comprehensibility ratings were high. Ten experts (90.9%) rated the PEM definition, examples and
wording in the two highest categories, and all experts (100%) did so for the introductory texts and tables.
Questionnaire length was rated as appropriate by eight experts (72.7%), the risk of triggering PEM
through completion was rated as low by seven (63.6%), and response categories were rated as well
differentiated by 10 (90.9%).

Methodological aspects received uniformly positive ratings. All experts rated the non-linear scoring, the
separation of crash severity and recovery duration, and the plausibility checks in the two highest
response categories. Target-group specificity was similarly supported: 10 experts (90.9%) rated the
questionnaire as suitable or very suitable for different ME/CFS severity levels, and all experts rated its
consideration of cultural and socioeconomic differences as appropriate or very appropriate. Overall
appraisal was high. All experts rated the questionnaire as good or very good for clinical and scientific
use; six (54.5%) supported use in practice or research without reservation and five (45.5%) would use it
after minor adaptations.
The results of the content validation at the item level (I-CVI) and scale level (S-CVI/Ave), as well as the
modified kappa values (k*) for relevance and clarity, are presented in Tables 4 and 5. Favourable ratings
were defined a priori as scores of 4 or 5 on the 5-point Likert scale. All items achieved acceptable
content validity for the relevance dimension, exceeding the defined threshold of I-CVI ≥ 0.78. Questions 1
(Post-exertional deterioration), 2 (Exertion tolerance), 3 (Number of crashes), and 6 (Pacing) were rated
as relevant or highly relevant by all 11 experts (I-CVI = 1.00). Question 4 (Recovery capacity) received a
favourable rating from 10 experts (I-CVI = 0.91), and Question 5 (New symptoms) from nine experts (I-
CVI = 0.82). The S-CVI/Ave for relevance was 0.96, indicating excellent validity at the instrument level.
The modified kappa values confirmed these results (k* ranging from 0.81 to 1.00), pointing to excellent
agreement beyond chance. Clarity ratings showed greater variability (I-CVI range: 0.55–0.91). While

Iterative instrument refinement
The questionnaire was revised in two cycles. In both cycles, quantitative ratings and qualitative feedback
were considered jointly and discussed by the three-member core development team. For each major
feedback point, the team decided whether it should be adopted, partially adopted, documented, or not
implemented. The complete revision matrices for triangulation cycles 1 and 2 are provided in
Supplementary File S2.
Page 17/28

Cycle 1: revisions following feedback from individuals with
ME/CFS or PCS
Feedback from participants with lived experience led to a substantial revision of the initial 13-item draft.
Ambivalent ratings of content fit and completeness were explained by qualitative feedback indicating
that the initial draft was perceived as overly aligned with general fatigue, exhaustion, or psychological
symptoms. PEM was therefore described throughout the questionnaire not as a collection of individual
symptoms, but as a disproportionate deterioration after exertion. Psychological symptoms were
removed from the core construct, potentially stigmatising terms were deleted, and wording was aligned
more closely with participants’ lived experience. Missing trigger domains were also added. Sensory or
environmental stimuli, orthostatic load, and positively valenced emotional triggers were incorporated
into the exertion matrix. Pacing was added as a contextual item because low crash frequency may also
reflect strict activity limitation. A simple yes/no PEM screening item was not adopted to avoid false-
negative self-classification among individuals with uncertain symptom attribution. Feedback on
medication effects, comorbidities, social security, and PEM in children or adolescents was documented
but not included in the core questionnaire because these aspects extend beyond the PEM construct.
Instrument structure was also revised. The questionnaire was reduced from 13 items to five core
questions, response options were clarified, and the layout was simplified. Structured count-based
formats were introduced to capture different crash patterns, allowing crash severity and recovery
duration to be assessed in a differentiated but standardised way. After the first revision, the
questionnaire assessed five dimensions: frequency, exertion tolerance, functional decline, recovery
capacity, and new symptoms, supplemented by a non-scored pacing item.
Cycle 2: revisions following expert-panel evaluation
After the first revision, the expert panel supported the overall structure of the instrument, particularly the
multidimensional assessment of PEM, the non-linear weighting and the separate assessment of crash
severity and recovery duration. Thus, it was not required to conduct a fundamental redesign. The second
revision focused on targeted refinements, terminology standardisation and resolution of specific
inconsistencies. Delayed onset was no longer formulated as a mandatory criterion, but as a frequent
feature of PEM. At the same time, the disproportionate response to exertion was emphasised. Social
exertion was integrated into the emotional-social trigger category. The term “crash” was used
consistently, and technical or potentially ambiguous wording was replaced or supplemented with plain-
language formulations. An inconsistency between the definition’s fixed minimum duration of PEM and
the shorter recovery-duration response options was resolved by removing the fixed minimum-duration
criterion. A free-text response option was not implemented because it could have reduced the level of
standardisation and increased completion burden. Also, a two-part version for different severity levels
was not developed, as this would have required a separate development and validation phase. After the
second revision, the instrument was designated the VPEM Questionnaire. The final version retained the
five core PEM dimensions and added two non-scored pacing context items assessing pacing use and
perceived effectiveness.
Page 18/28

Phase 4: Pretest results
The pre-test was completed by five participants on the online SoSci Survey platform. No major
comprehension problems were identified. Two technical and presentational issues emerged. First, the
online layout of the exertion matrix in item 2 was rated as difficult to navigate in its original single-page
format; the matrix was therefore distributed across five separate pages, one per trigger domain, to
improve readability and reduce the burden of scrolling. Second, participants indicated that it was unclear
which fields in item 3 required completion; an instruction was added specifying that only the fields
corresponding to experienced crash intensities should be filled in, with all remaining fields left blank.
Following these adjustments, the instrument was finalised as the VPEM Questionnaire, and the English
and German version is provided in Supplementary File S3.
Discussion
This study used an iterative, exploratory sequential mixed-methods approach to conduct the initial
content validation of the Vienna Post-Exertional Malaise Questionnaire (VPEM Questionnaire), a
multidimensional questionnaire designed to assess the clinically observable consequences of PEM in
individuals with ME/CFS or Long COVID with PEM. The instrument captures five core PEM dimensions -
crash frequency, exertion thresholds across five trigger classes, functional impact, recovery dynamics,
and new crash-associated symptoms - supplemented by non-scored pacing context items. Patient and
expert-panel data converged on the need to move beyond frequency- and severity-centred approaches to
PEM assessment. The evaluation by the ME/CFS and PCS cohort identified conceptual ambiguities in
the initial draft, particularly regarding the distinction between PEM, nonspecific fatigue and
psychological symptoms, as well as insufficient coverage of trigger diversity and intra-individual
variability. Expert ratings of the revised version were generally favourable, particularly regarding content
coverage, intelligibility, methodological design, and overall suitability, thereby supporting the instrument’s
content and face validity at this stage of development. A key finding from the patient evaluation was the
identification of conceptual ambiguities in the primarily literature-based initial draft. In particular,
insufficient differentiation from nonspecific fatigue and psychological symptoms, missing trigger
domains, and inadequate representation of intra-individual variability indicated that the patient
perspective is not merely complementary but methodologically central to the development of a content-
valid PROM.[22]
Multidimensionality of PEM
Our findings highlight that PEM cannot be adequately represented along a single dimension.
Differentiated assessment of trigger types, functional impact, recovery dynamics and associated new
symptoms is required to reflect the clinical reality of individual patients. This aligns with recent work
describing PEM as a multi-layered phenomenon characterised by diverse trigger categories, frequently
delayed onset, variable recovery trajectories, multisystem symptomatology, marked functional
impairment and prolonged or incomplete recovery.[23–25] A purely symptom-focused assessment,
Page 19/28

therefore, risks missing core characteristics of PEM.[9] At the same time, this broader conceptual
coverage exposes a tension between content breadth and feasibility. ME/CFS participants described
experiencing cognitive, emotional and occasionally physical strain when completing the instrument, with
retrospective recall proving particularly challenging. As completing a PEM instrument may exacerbate
symptoms in some individuals, the development of such instruments must balance conceptual
differentiation with practical tolerability.
Conceptual positioning of PEM
Although PEM is often described in clinical criteria as a cardinal symptom of ME/CFS,[9] the feedback
obtained in this study suggested that a multidimensional questionnaire approach would be more
appropriate than relying on a single symptom item. Rather, PEM represents a dynamic post-exertional
deterioration pattern characterised by disproportionate exacerbation of symptoms, functional decline,
delayed onset in many cases and impaired or incomplete recovery.[25, 26] Therefore, its clinical meaning
lies not only in the occurrence of symptoms after exertion, but also in the interaction among exertional
triggers, multisystemic deterioration, loss of function, and the inability to return to the pre-exertional
baseline promptly.[5, 26] In terms of instrument development, this implies that PEM should be assessed
as a multidimensional illness process rather than a unidimensional symptom.[5]
The VPEM Questionnaire was developed to assess the clinically observable consequences of post-
exertional deterioration rather than PEM as a single symptom item. It covers several interrelated aspects
of this deterioration, including crash frequency, exertion thresholds across trigger domains, functional
impact, recovery dynamics, new crash-associated symptoms, and pacing context.
Construct demarcation: fatigue and psychological
symptoms
Inadequate construct demarcation emerged as a central methodological issue for content validity in the
first cycle. This was particularly evident for the term “fatigue”. Target-group feedback and existing
literature indicate that fatigue is a non-specific symptom occurring across many chronic conditions and
does not adequately capture the specific phenomenon of exertion intolerance in ME/CFS.[7, 27] The
distinction between PEM and psychological symptoms also proved highly relevant. Wording that places
PEM close to affective instability, burnout or primarily psychological exhaustion may contribute to
misclassification and reinforce existing experiences of stigma.[28] The VPEM Questionnaire addresses
this risk through patient-near language, particularly the use of the term “crash”, everyday examples, and
the avoidance of psychologising formulations. These linguistic choices are methodological rather than
merely stylistic: many terms may be formally understood but still fail to reflect the lived experience of
PEM. In this context, linguistic precision is a prerequisite for content validity, acceptability, and valid
application of the instrument.[29, 30] Patient-near, non-stigmatising language may also be clinically
relevant, as people with ME/CFS frequently report lack of recognition of their symptoms, with
implications for trust and the therapeutic relationship.[28, 31]
Page 20/28

These findings also require a critical appraisal of initial screening items used in some shorter
instruments. A single filter question that terminates the questionnaire when answered negatively
assumes that respondents can already identify their experience as PEM. This may be problematic,
particularly for newly affected or diagnostically uncertain individuals, and may increase the risk of false-
negative self-classification.[11]
Intra-individual variability and recall window
In developing the VPEM Questionnaire, we captured how post-exertional deterioration manifests across
different types of everyday exertion. We therefore did not conceptualise PEM as a single symptom item
or rely solely on a global severity rating. Instead, we aimed to operationalise PEM through the response
to exertion: which types of exertion typically trigger a crash, at what exertion threshold this occurs, how
strongly daily functioning is impaired, and whether additional symptoms emerge during a crash. This
approach was based on the understanding that PEM is clinically characterised by an abnormal and
disproportionate response to exertion, often involving delayed symptom exacerbation and prolonged
recovery.[13] The same deterioration pattern may become visible after different forms of exertion,
including physical, cognitive, sensory, environmental, emotional-social, and orthostatic demands.[9, 11]
Therefore, we categorised these trigger domains and provided everyday examples to help respondents
identify the level of exertion at which a crash is likely to occur. Crashes often result from cumulative
rather than single exposures. Nevertheless, the questionnaire was designed to capture the typical
exertion thresholds at which specific types of everyday activity are likely to contribute to post-exertional
deterioration. Orthostatic load was treated as a distinct domain because target-group feedback and prior
evidence suggest that upright posture or positional change may trigger post-exertional deterioration
independently of physical activity.[32]
We assessed pacing separately because it can modify the observable expression of PEM. A low crash
frequency may indicate milder disease, but it may also reflect strict activity restriction and successful
avoidance of exertion thresholds.[11] The selected eight-week recall window represented a pragmatic
compromise. It was shorter than the six-month recall period used in some existing instruments and may
therefore reduce recall bias, while remaining long enough to capture recurrent or episodic PEM patterns.
[11, 33] For clinical and research applications, digital symptom diaries or high-frequency monitoring
approaches may complement retrospective instruments by capturing short-term fluctuations and
individual exertion–symptom relationships in greater detail.[34]
Positioning in relation to existing instruments
The VPEM Questionnaire is complementary to, and does not replace existing instruments. Existing PEM
instruments, such as the DSQ-PEM and DSQ-PEM-2, provide established approaches to assessing PEM-
related symptoms and selected PEM characteristics, including frequency, severity, duration, triggers and
pacing-related aspects.[7, 11] Its distinction lies in its focus on the clinically observable consequences of
post-exertional deterioration rather than on fatigue severity or functional capacity alone. Specifically, it
Page 21/28

links exertion thresholds across trigger domains with crash occurrence, functional decline, recovery
dynamics, new crash-associated symptoms, and pacing context within a single assessment framework.
Limitations
Several limitations should be considered. First, the initial item pool was primarily developed based on
existing literature, consensus documents, and established instruments. Individuals with lived experience
of the disease were systematically involved in the evaluation and revision of the first draft, but not in a
formal co-generation phase before the creation of the initial item pool. Earlier patient involvement might
have expanded the initial item pool and pre-empted some of the conceptual revisions required later.
Second, the target-group sample was small and predominantly recruited via patient organisations. While
this approach was suitable for obtaining detailed feedback from individuals highly experienced with
PEM, it may have introduced a selection bias towards individuals with high health literacy, profound
symptom reflection, and strong motivation to participate in questionnaire development. Even though
there were flexible participation formats, the perspectives of individuals who are newly ill, patients with
lower health literacy or from different socioeconomic backgrounds, and those with milder or very severe
disease may not have been fully captured. Third, the expert panel did not include all initially targeted
professional perspectives. Specifically, experts in psychometrics, questionnaire development, and
nursing science were absent. Consequently, psychometric design issues, nursing perspectives, and
questions regarding implementation in various care contexts could only be considered to a limited
extent. Fourth, the revision of the instrument necessarily relied on interpretative decisions by the core
development team. Although all major decisions were discussed and documented in revision matrices,
decisions about whether feedback was integrated into the core construct, retained as contextual
information, or excluded may have influenced the instrument’s final structure. Fifth, the quantitative
ratings, including I-CVI and modified kappa values, should be interpreted as descriptive indicators of
expert agreement rather than formal psychometric validation. While these metrics are valuable for
content validation, they depend on the panel’s composition and size, as well as the chosen rating
thresholds. Finally, different versions of the instrument were evaluated in Cycle 1 and Cycle 2. While this
is expected in an iterative development process, it precludes a direct numerical comparison of the
ratings between the two cycles.
Future Research
The next step is to validate the VPEM Questionnaire psychometrically in a larger cohort of individuals
with ME/CFS. This will entail assessing reliability, construct validity and score interpretation, as well as
comparing the questionnaire with established instruments such as the DSQ-PEM and FUNCAP. Further
work should also examine the questionnaire's discriminant validity in relation to other conditions
characterised by fatigue, such as multiple sclerosis, in order to test its ability to distinguish PEM-related
post-exertional deterioration from fatigue.
Conclusion
Page 22/28

A systematic, iterative, and multi-perspective development process resulted in the VPEM Questionnaire,
a multidimensional instrument assessing PEM across crash frequency, exertion thresholds in five trigger
domains, functional impairment, recovery dynamics, new crash-associated symptoms, and non-scored
pacing context items. Feedback from patients and experts jointly supports the instrument’s initial
content and face validity and provides a conceptually grounded basis for subsequent psychometric
validation in ME/CFS and Long COVID populations with PEM.
