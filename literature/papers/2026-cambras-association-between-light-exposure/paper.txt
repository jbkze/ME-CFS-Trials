Abstract
Background  Light is a major environmental factor regulating circadian rhythms, sleep- wake cycles, and mood-
related behaviors. Patients with Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) often experience 
circadian disruption and poor sleep quality, which severely compromise their quality of life; however, the relationship 
between light exposure and illness severity remains largely unknown.
Methods  An observational cross-sectional cohort secondary study used collected data from 100 ME/CFS patients 
and 56 healthy controls to explore the impact of spontaneous light exposure on multidimensional health status 
and circulating biochemical parameters. Demographic and clinical features were assessed using validated patient-
reported outcome measures. Light intensity, wrist temperature, and physical activity were continuously monitored at 
home over one week using wrist-worn actigraphy. Light intensity during predefined intervals and rhythmic variables 
of light cycle were calculated. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to reduce dimensionality of light 
variables. Multivariable analysis was performed adjusting for age, sex, body mass index, and physical activity.
Results  Following PCA of the light patterns, two components emerged across groups with high consistency: PC1 
(explaining 61.7% of the total variance) reflected higher daytime light and rhythm stability, and PC2 (explaining 16.1%) 
represented nocturnal/early-morning light and rhythm instability. In ME/CFS patients, light variables were more 
extensively associated with clinical outcomes measures (FIS-40, PSQI and SF-36) than in healthy controls (all p < 0.05).

healthy controls. More stable daytime light appears to relate to better symptom profiles, whereas irregular exposure

improving the quality of life in ME/CFS, representing a promising emerging for future translational research.

Conversely, disruptions in light exposure patterns, such 
as insufficient daytime light or excessive exposure to arti­
ficial light at night (LAN) have been associated with sleep 
disturbances [2–7], cognitive impairments and mood 
disorders [4–6, 8] and metabolic dysregulation [9–11]. 
Therefore, understanding how different light exposure 
profiles affect human health is essential for developing 
interventions aimed at improving health-related quality 
of life, particularly in vulnerable populations.
Myalgic encephalomyelitis, also referred to as chronic 
fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), is a complex and debili­
tating multifaceted disorder characterized by persis­
tent or relapsing fatigue lasting at least six months that 
is not alleviate by rest. It is frequently accompanied by 
post-exertional malaise, cognitive impairments, non-
restorative sleep, autonomic dysfunction, and a broad 
spectrum of somatic symptoms that substantially impair 
daily functioning by more than 50% among sufferers [12]. 
Despite increasing recognition of the clinical and societal 
burden of ME/CFS, its underlying pathophysiological 
mechanisms remain poorly understood, and reliable bio­
markers or environmental indicators of disease severity 
are still lacking [13].
Through the visual and non-visual pathways, light may 
influence both fatigue and quality of life directly via neu­
ral circuits regulating mood and alertness, and indirectly 
through the modulation of circadian rhythms. Disruption 
of these rhythms, often referred to as chronodisruption, 
has been increasingly recognized as a key mechanism 
linking abnormal light exposure to adverse health out­
comes [14, 15]. Individuals with ME/CFS frequently 
experience reduced exposure to natural daylight, often 
as a consequence of mobility limitations or prolonged 
periods spent indoors or bedbound [16]. Insufficient light 
exposure may further exacerbate these health and well­
being challenges by contributing to fatigue, sleep distur­
bances, cognitive dysfunction (“brain fog”), alterations in 
neurotransmitter homeostasis, and reduced vitamin D 
biosynthesis [16–19]. However, the extent to which light 
exposure patterns are associated with symptom severity 
in ME/CFS remains largely unexplored.
Actigraphy, a non-invasive method for continuously 
monitoring rest–activity cycles, represents a valuable tool 
for assessing sleep and circadian rhythms in real-world 
settings [20–23]. Modern wrist-worn actigraphy can also 
capture ambient light intensity, enabling the simultane­
ous assessment of behavioral and environmental circa­
dian inputs [24]. Thus, by integrating actigraphy-derived 
light exposure data with clinical outcomes, it is possible 
to investigate potential associations between objective 
light exposure patterns and subjective experiences of 
health status and quality of life in ME/CFS.
In previous studies, we demonstrated that overall light 
intensity was lower in ME/CFS patients than in healthy 
controls, likely reflecting reduced outdoor exposure and 
increased time spent indoors. Given the tight coupling 
between light exposure and physical activity, this obser­
vation is consistent with the lower activity levels typi­
cally reported in this population [25–27]. Building upon 
these findings, the current study constitutes a second­
ary analysis utilizing actigraphy-derived light exposure 
data, health outcomes measures, and circulating blood 
parameters from previous investigations to explore their 
potential association with light exposure patterns in indi­
viduals with ME/CFS [26–28].
This study aimed to extend our prior findings by exam­
ining whether real-world light exposure may differentially 
relate to clinical and physiological outcomes measures in 
ME/CFS patients, independent of differences in over­
all light intensity. We hypothesize that more stable and 
well-defined light exposure patterns are associated with a 
lower symptom burden and better health-related quality 
of life in ME/CFS.
Methods
Study design, setting and participant recruitment
A unicenter, observational cross-sectional cohort sec­
ondary study included 100 ME/CFS patients (mean age: 
49.5 ± 0.99 years; 80 females) and 56 matched healthy 
controls (mean age: 42.9 ± 1.78 years; 45 females) consec­
utively by a specialist clinic from the largest outpatient 
tertiary referral center (ME/CFS Unit, Vall d’Hebron 
University Hospital, Barcelona, Spain) between October 
2020 and March 2022.
Individual with ME/CFS were eligible if they were aged 
≥ 18 years and had a confirmed diagnosis of ME/CFS 
established according to the 2011 Canadian Consensus 
Criteria (also commonly referred to as ICC) [29]. Patient 
selection was based on consecutive enrollment, and 
exclusion criteria were applied as previously reported 
[27].
Healthy controls were eligible if they were aged ≥ 18 
years and reported no history of autonomic and neuro­
logical symptoms, autoimmunity, cancer, cardiovascu­
lar, endocrine, or neuroimmune disorders, as well as no 
alcohol or drug dependence or regular use of medica­
tion, as previously reported [25]. Healthy volunteers were 
recruited through word-of-mouth from the same local 
community and did not meet case criteria for ME/CFS 
at the time of enrollment. All study participants were of 
Caucasian origin and resided in the same geographical 
area.
Ethics statement and consent to participate
After receiving verbal and written information on the 
study protocol, all participants gave signed informed 
consent prior to enrolment. The study was reviewed and 
approved by the local Vall d’Hebron University Hospital

Actigraphy-derived circadian rhythm analysis
Data collection recorded during the summer months 
(June to September) and December was not used, to 
minimize potential bias related to seasonal variations in 
environmental conditions, particularly temperature and 
daylight exposure [25].
Rhythmic parameters for physical activity, skin temper­
ature and light intensity were derived using cosine curve 
fitting (24-hour model), including mesor, amplitude, 
and acrophase. Non-parametric measures of circadian 
organization were also computed, including intradaily 
variability (IV) and interdaily stability (IS). Additional 
derived variables included M10 (10 consecutive hours of 
highest activity/light exposure), L5 (5 consecutive hours 
of lowest activity and light exposure), M5 and L10 for 
skin temperature (inverse rhythm) [26, 33].
Moreover, ambient light intensity was stratified into 
five predefined time intervals across the 24-hour day. 
Mean light exposure was calculated for 02:00–06:00  h 
(L_2– 6), 08:00–12:00 h (L_8–12), 12:00–16:00 h (L_12–
16), 16:00–20:00 h (L_16–20), and 20:00–24:00 h (L_20–
24). These intervals were selected to capture key phases 
of the light–dark cycle, including nocturnal exposure, 
morning light as a primary circadian entraining signal, 
and afternoon–evening exposure. This stratification 
allowed characterization of temporal patterns of light 
exposure relevant to circadian regulation.
Measures
After a week, participants attended a follow-up visit, dur­
ing which they returned the actigraphy device and com­
pleted a set of validated self-reported outcome measures 
assessing their current health status one week after the 
initial clinical assessment. Changes in fatigue perception, 
sleep disturbances, anxiety and depression, autonomic 
dysfunction, and health-related quality of life were rated 
by each participant under the supervision of two trained 
investigators (J.A.-M and J.C.-M.) who oversaw compli­
ance as already described [27].
Fatigue perception
Fatigue was evaluated using the Fatigue Impact Scale 
(FIS-40), a 40-item questionnaire with three subscales 
measuring the perceived impact of fatigue: cognitive (10 
items), physical (10 items), and psychosocial functioning 
(20 items). Each item is scored from 0 (no fatigue) to 4 
(severe fatigue). The total FIS-40 score is calculated by 
summing all 40 items (range 0–160), with higher scores 
reflecting greater functional limitations due to fatigue 
[34].
Sleep quality
Sleep disturbances were assessed using the self-adminis­
tered 19-item Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). The 
Institutional Ethical Committee on Human Research (ref­
erence number: PR/AG-201/2016). The study was carried 
out in accordance with the 2024 Declaration of Helsinki 
and with all the International Conference on Harmo­
nization and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines. After 
providing consent, all participants underwent a clinical 
examination and experimental research protocols.
Study procedures
Data collection
The data collection took place both in the office and at 
home. Demographics and clinical characteristics, out­
come measures, actigraphy-derived data, and laboratory 
biochemical variables used in the present study were 
obtained from an existing dataset. The current analysis 
included participants with complete available data across 
all relevant items.
Detailed methodological procedures for partici­
pant recruitment, clinical evaluation, data acquisition, 
and laboratory blood processing have been previously 
reported. Nevertheless, a brief overview of the methodol­
ogy is provided below for clarity.
Actigraphy-derived measures
Objective behavioral and environmental data were col­
lected using wrist-worn actigraphy.
Participants were equipped with a wrist-worn ambula­
tory actigraphy device (ActTrust®, Condor Instruments, 
São Paulo, Brazil) factory-calibrated by the manufac­
turer, which continuously recorded data on motor activ­
ity (accelerometry), skin temperature (°C), and ambient 
light intensity (lux) at one-minute intervals at home 
over a seven-day period. A week continuous monitoring 
period was selected and deemed sufficient based on sev­
eral well-established protocols in chronobiology [30], and 
the specific clinical constraints of the target population.
Participants were instructed to wear the wearable 
device continuously, except during activities involving 
water exposure (e.g., showering or swimming). Com­
pliance with the wrist-worn actigraphy protocol was 
evaluated through visual inspection of double-plotted 
actigraphy graphs of activity (accelerometry), skin tem­
perature (°C), and ambient light intensity (lux) for all 
datasets. Although the actigraphy records light inten­
sity (lux), values were log-transformed prior to analysis 
using log10 (lux + 0.01). This transformation is commonly 
applied in light exposure research, as the response of the 
circadian system to light follows a non-linear, approxi­
mately semi-logarithmic relationship [31, 32]. The device 
recorded and stored data in its memory for subsequent 
analysis.

questionnaire evaluates seven components of sleep qual­
ity: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, 
habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep 
medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component 
is scored from 0 (no sleep problems) to 3 (severe sleep 
problems). The global PSQI score ranges from 0 to 21, 
with scores above 5 indicating poorer sleep quality [35].
Anxiety and depression
Symptoms of anxiety and depression were measured 
using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale 
(HADS), a validated 14-item self-report instrument com­
prising seven items for anxiety and seven for depression. 
Each item is scored from 0 to 3, resulting in subscale 
scores ranging from 0 to 21. Scores are interpreted as 
follows: 0–7 = normal, 8– 10 = mild, 11–14 = moderate, 
and 15–21 = severe. The overall HADS score ranges from 
0 (no symptoms) to 42 (severe anxiety and depression) 
[36].
Autonomic dysfunction
Participants completed the Composite Autonomic 
Symptom Scale (COMPASS-31), a 31item question­
naire assessing the frequency and severity of autonomic 
symptoms across six domains: orthostatic intolerance, 
vasomotor, secretomotor, gastrointestinal, bladder, and 
pupillomotor systems. Summing the domain scores pro­
duces a total COMPASS-31 score ranging from 0 to 100, 
with higher scores indicating more severe autonomic 
dysfunction [37].
Health-related quality of life
Health-related quality of life was measured using the 
36-item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36), a self-report 
questionnaire evaluating both physical and mental func­
tioning. The SF-36 includes eight subscales: physical 
functioning, physical role functioning, bodily pain, gen­
eral health, vitality, social functioning, emotional role 
functioning, and mental health. Lower scores indicate a 
greater negative impact of health on an individual’s daily 
functioning. The overall SF-36 scores range from 0 to 
100, with higher scores indicating better health outcomes 
[38].
Blood collection and processing
Biological data included biomarkers of endothelial dys­
function and inflammatory and laboratory biochemical 
variables. Detailed methodological procedures for partic­
ipant recruitment, clinical assessment, data acquisition, 
and laboratory sample processing have been previously 
reported [27, 28].
Briefly, fasting venous blood samples from each par­
ticipant were originally collected under standardized 
conditions between 8:00 a.m. and 10:00 a.m., processed 
and stored in aliquots at − 80 °C until further assays. The 
present study used previously generated and stored bio­
marker data. Blood samples were available from a subset 
of study participants, including 64 ME/CFS patients and 
41 matched healthy controls.
Measurement of circulating biochemical parameters
For the current analysis, previously quantified circulating 
endothelial biomarkers were retrieved, including soluble 
endothelin-1 (ET-1), thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1), vascu­
lar cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), and intercellular 
adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), as previously described 
[27, 28]. In addition, laboratory biochemical variables 
were also included (such as levels of cortisol, serotonin, 
triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TChol), HDL-cho­
lesterol (cHDL), LDL-cholesterol (cLDL), and non-HDL 
cholesterol (TChol−cHDL). Derived clinical lipid indices 
were also calculated, including the ratios TChol/cHDL, 
cLDL/cHDL, and TG/cHDL, as previously reported [26].
Statistical analysis
Continuous variables are displayed as the mean ± stan­
dard error (SD), while categorical variables are expressed 
as number of participants (percentages), unless other­
wise indicated. Normality and homogeneity of variance 
were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, 
respectively. For group comparisons involving continu­
ous variables, either Student’s t-test or one-way analysis 
of variance (ANOVA) followed by post-hoc tests were 
used as appropriate.
For this study, participants were subsequently cat­
egorized into two seasonal groups based on expected 
environmental light exposure: a longer-daylight season 
(March to May), which included 56% of ME/CFS patients 
and 40% of healthy controls, and a shorter-daylight sea­
son (October, November, January, and February), com­
prising the remaining participants. A one-way analysis of 
variance (ANOVA) of maximum recorded light intensity 
indicated a trend toward a seasonal effect on light expo­
sure (p = 0.09). Accordingly, all subsequent statistical 
analyses included season as a mandatory covariate.
To reduce dimensionality and identify key patterns of 
light exposure, a principal component analysis (PCA) 
with varimax rotation was applied to nine light-related 
variables (mean, amplitude, IV, IS, L_2–6, L_8–12, L_12–
16, L_16–20, and L_20–24).
Two components with eigenvalues greater than 1 were 
retained and standardized factor scores were calculated 
for use in subsequent analyses. These PCA-derived com­
ponents were included in regression models for each out­
come variable. Each model included group (ME/CFS vs. 
healthy controls) as a between-subjects factor, the two 
light components as covariates, and their interaction

Results
Participant characteristics
Most of the participants completed datasets. However, 
actigraphy-derived data was missing in 6 ME/CFS par­
ticipants and in 3 healthy controls, and health outcomes 
measures were also missing in 1 ME/CFS participant and 
in 3 healthy controls.
ME/CFS differs from healthy controls in most of the 
variables, reflecting well-established clinical and bio­
logical distinctions among study participants. Though, 
these results are not the primary focus of the present 
study, they are therefore displayed in Table 1 and sum­
marized herewith. The healthy controls had a mean age 
of 42.9 ± 1.8 years, whereas the ME/CFS patients had 
a mean age of 49.5 ± 1.0 years. Body mass index (BMI) 
was also significantly higher in the ME/CFS patients 
(mean = 27.0 ± 0.5  kg/m2) than in healthy controls 
(mean = 24.4 ± 0.6 kg/m2), while the proportion of females 
was similar across groups (80% in both).
Consistent with previous reports, subjective assess­
ment scores differed significantly between groups. 
Individuals with ME/CFS showed higher scores on 
the FIS40, HADS, COMPASS-31, and PSQI, indicat­
ing greater perceived fatigue, psychological distress, 
autonomic dysfunction, and poorer sleep quality (all 
p < 0.001). In contrast, SF36 scores were lower in the ME/
CFS patients, reflecting reduced health-related qual­
ity of life (p < 0.0001). Similarly, alterations in circulating 
biochemical parameters were shown, including higher 
levels of ET-1, TSP-1, VCAM-1, and blood lipid profiles 
in ME/CFS patients compared with healthy controls (all 
p < 0.001).
Group differences were also found in circadian-
related variables. It can be observed the lower levels in 
both physical activity and light exposure in the ME/CFS 
group, as both variables use to be associated. Healthy 
controls exhibited higher activity rhythm values (mean, 
amplitude, and M10), whereas ME/CFS showed greater 
rhythm fragmentation (intradaily variability, IV). Tem­
perature rhythm variables were largely comparable 
between groups, with the exception of higher IV in the 
ME/CFS patients. Light-related variables also differed 
significantly, with the ME/CFS group showing lower 
average light exposure, reduced amplitude, and greater 
fragmentation of the light–dark cycle. Additionally, light 
exposure during daytime intervals (L_8–12, L_12–16, 
and L_16–20) was significantly lower in ME/CFS than in 
healthy controls (all p ≤ 0.01). Figure 1 shows the rhyth­
mic profile of the three actigraphy-derived variables: 
light, activity and skin temperature, showing clearly that 
physical activity and light intensity during the day are 
significantly different between ME/CFS and healthy con­
trols (all p < 0.001).
Association between light variables and outcome 
measures
This study aimed to investigate the associations between 
objective light exposure patterns and both subjective 
and biological outcomes measures among participants. 
Accordingly, subsequent analyses focus on examining 
correlation- and regression-based relationships between 
light exposure variables and clinical and biochemical 
measures, while accounting for group membership and 
relevant covariates.
A first approach to associating light variables with self-
reported outcome measures (Table S1) was carried out 
separately for ME/CFS patients and healthy controls. 
However, in some cases, associations were found for light 
intensity while in others for variables of the light rhythm. 
Thus, to assess the main effect of light based on both light 
intensity and rhythmic variables, a PCA with varimax 
rotation was performed using nine light-related vari­
ables: mean, amplitude, IS and IV of the light-dark cycle 
and the mean light intensity in the five timing intervals 
(L_2–6, L_8–12, L_12–16, L_16–20, and L_20–24). As 
shown in Fig. 2A, the rotated component matrix identi­
fied two orthogonal dimensions of light exposure, with 
eigenvalues greater than 1. These components accounted 
for 77.91% of the total variance: component 1 (named as 
Light1) explained 61.78%, and component 2 (named as 
Light2) explained 16.13%. Light1 included strong posi­
tive loadings from mean (0.820), amplitude (0.928), IS 
(0.891), L_12–16 (0.887), L_16–20 (0.855), and L_8–12 
(0.749), while L_IV had a negative loading (− 0.768), indi­
cating that brighter days and more stable light exposure 
patterns are associated with Light1. On the other hand, 
Light2 had high loadings from L_2–6 (0.916) and L_20–
24 (0.612), reflecting a nocturnal/evening light exposure 
pattern. Rotation converged in 3 iterations.
These two components were saved as standardized fac­
tor scores and later used to explore differential effects 
on sleep and mood across disease and control groups. 
The consistency of the overall results was confirmed by 
separate PCA analyses in ME/CFS patients and healthy 

Page 9 of 18
Cambras et al. Journal of Translational Medicine         (2026) 24:1092 
controls, which independently reproduced the same two-
component structure.
Linear regression models were conducted to deter­
mine whether the two PCAderived light components 
differentially predicted sleep, psychological stress, and 
physiological outcomes measures across study partici­
pants (Table  2). The dependent variables included total 
and subscale scores from the FIS-40, HADS, PSQI, and 
SF-36 questionnaires. Each model included group (ME/
CFS patients vs. healthy controls) as a between-subjects 
factor, the two light components (Light1 and Light2) as 
covariates, and their respective interaction terms (Light1 
× Group and Light2 × Group) to assess whether the asso­
ciations between light exposure and outcomes measures 
differed by group. Additionally, age, sex, BMI, season and 
activity (assessed by amplitude of the activity rhythm) 
were included as confounding variables.
As expected, group was a significant factor in all the 
regression models. However, the most important of this 
analysis is the association of the outcome measures with 
light exposure considering group interaction. A signifi­
cant interaction between Light2 and group was observed 
in several models indicating that the association between 
light exposure and clinical outcomes measures differs 
between ME/CFS and healthy controls (p < 0.001) This 
is the case of overall FIS-40, HADS, PSQI and COM­
PASS-31, with positive associations and total SF-36 with 
negative associations. Moreover, the interaction group x 
Light2 was also significant for TSP-1, triglycerides and 
TG/cHDL ratio. The interaction between Light1 and the 
group only accounted for some domains of outcomes 
measures. The adjustments, with or without taking into 
account the activity amplitude, as was subsequently veri­
fied, did not substantially alter these results.
To confirm these findings, we also carried out partial 
correlations separately for both groups of participants 
(Table S2), also controlling for age, sex, BMI, season and 
the amplitude of the activity rhythm, showing that Light2 
is associated with outcomes measures in the ME/CFS 
subjects but not in healthy controls. Thus, Light2 was 
positively associated within ME/CFS patients for total 
COMPASS-31 and some of their domains, and also with 
VCAM-1, triglycerides, TG/cHDL ratio, and was nega­
tively associated with serotonin levels in the ME/CFS 
cohort (all p < 0.05).

Classification of actigraphy-derived light exposure 
patterns
As a second approach, we assessed whether distinct light 
exposure profiles could be identified within the whole 
population (Fig.  2B and C). We classified individuals 
based on their PCA-derived component scores. Groups 
were defined by dichotomizing Light1 and Light2 at their 
respective sample medians (above median = 1, below 
median = 0). This approach yielded four groups based on 
the resulting light exposure patterns:
Group A: Light1 = 1 and Light2 = 1 (both components 
above the median). This pattern may reflect higher over­
all light exposure with poorer temporal distribution (e.g., 
nocturnal light exposure or irregular schedules). It was 
designated the nocturnal light pattern.
Group B: Light1 = 1 and Light2 = 0 (higher daytime 
light exposure and lower nocturnal/temporal disruption). 
This pattern reflects more appropriate circadian timing 
of light exposure and was designated the healthy light 
pattern.
Group C: Light1 = 0 and Light2 = 1 (lower daytime 
light exposure and higher nocturnal/temporal disrup­
tion). This pattern may reflect reduced daytime exposure, 
increased nocturnal exposure, and disrupted rhythms. It 
was designated the adverse light pattern.
Group D: Light1 = 0 and Light2 = 0 (both components 
below the median). This group reflects overall low light 
exposure with relatively stable timing and was designated 
the low diurnal light exposure pattern.
Group A (nocturnal light) shows a similar proportion 
in healthy controls than in ME/CFS (18.5 vs. 18.7%). The 
most adaptive light profile (group B, healthy) is the most 
frequent in ME/CFS (31.3%), with a bit lower proportion 
in healthy controls (29.6%). Group C (adverse) is more 
common in healthy controls than in ME/CFS (33.3% vs. 
24%). Finally, Group D (low diurnal light) is more com­
mon in ME/CFS than in healthy controls (26% vs. 18.5%). 
However, a chi-square test of independence did not show 
different distribution of individuals and light exposure 
profiles.
A series of univariate general linear models (GLM) 
were also conducted separately in ME/CFS and healthy 
controls to explore differences in subjective outcomes 
measures across the four light exposure patterns. It is of 
interest that activity (mean or amplitude) did not differ 
among these groups.

Discussion
This study is the first one, to our knowledge, that exam­
ine the role of exposure to environmental light in multi­
dimensional health outcomes and physiological variables 
in ME/CFS. The findings of this study suggest that

environmental light exposure is differentially associated 
with a range of health outcomes measures, with more 
consistent and pronounced associations shown in ME/
CFS patients. These findings extend current knowledge 
by suggesting that environmental light may represent 
an underexplored but potentially relevant modulator of 
symptom expression in ME/CFS.
Circadian disruption and sleep abnormalities are well 
documented in individuals with ME/CFS, with con­
sistent evidence of altered rest-activity patterns and 
impaired sleep quality contributing to symptom burden 
and reduced health-related quality of life [16, 39, 40]. 
However, although light is the primary environmental 
zeitgeber regulating circadian rhythms, its role as a spon­
taneously occurring, real-world exposure factor in ME/
CFS has been largely unexplored. To our knowledge, no 
previous studies have examined naturalistic light expo­
sure measured using wrist-worn actigraphy in relation to 
multidimensional health outcomes and circulating bio­
chemical markers in ME/CFS.
The methodology used in this study is novel in that it 
characterizes environmental light exposure along two 
distinct dimensions derived from principal component 
analysis, integrating both light intensity and temporal 
stability of light exposure patterns. Using this data-driven 
construct, Light2 likely reflects a combination of environ­
mental exposure and altered behavioral or circadian reg­
ulation and may serve as a proxy of circadian disruption. 
This component showed greater clinical relevance in ME/
CFS than in healthy controls, suggesting that increased 
evening light exposure and/or greater circadian rhythm 
instability may be linked to worse clinical status in ME/
CFS patients. This is important because not only mean 
light intensity, but also the stability of the light–dark 
rhythm appears be relevant in ME/CFS.
These findings indicate that the impact of light expo­
sure is not uniform across the population, but may 
depend on underlying physiological or pathological con­
ditions. In particular, light exposure during biologically 
sensitive phases may contribute to symptom exacerba­
tion in more vulnerable individuals, potentially through 
mechanisms related to circadian misalignment.
Nevertheless, although the present study identified 
associations between light exposure patterns and both 
questionnaire-based outcomes and biochemical mark­
ers, these findings do not establish directionality and, 
thus, reverse causation cannot be excluded. Individuals 
with more severe ME/CFS symptoms may adopt behav­
iors that influence light exposure patterns, including 
increased nighttime awakenings and greater use of arti­
ficial light during the biological night. Moreover, it is also 
plausible that higher symptom burden leads to reduced 
daytime activity and increased time spent indoors or 
bedbound, resulting in diminished exposure to natural 
daylight and a consequent alteration of the overall light–
dark exposure profile.
We acknowledge that physical activity is a multidi­
mensional behavior and that actigraphy-derived ampli­
tude may not fully capture short-term fluctuations, 
pacing strategies, and/or post-exertional malaise variabil­
ity. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be entirely 
excluded given the observational and cross-sectional 
design of the study. Within this context, light exposure 
should be interpreted not only as a potential environ­
mental exposure but also as a proxy of behavioral adapta­
tions to illness and circadian disruption associated with 
illness severity, rather than as an independent causal 
determinant.
This study also proposes a new approach to classify 
spontaneous patterns of light exposure in the ME/CFS 
population. A data-driven classification was applied to 
characterize individual light exposure profiles by com­
bining the two principal components into four groups 
based on median splits. This novel approach enabled the 
identification of distinct patterns integrating light inten­
sity, timing, and rhythm stability as potential variables.
The classification of individuals into four groups was 
intended to facilitate visualization and hypothesis gen­
eration for the development of targeted lifestyle or chro­
notherapeutic interventions. Combining two orthogonal 
dimensions into a 2 × 2 framework allowed us to explore 
whether specific combinations of daytime stability 
(Light1) and nocturnal exposure (Light2) were associ­
ated with multidimensional health outcomes in ME/CFS 
patients compared to healthy controls.
Although the distribution of individuals across the four 
profiles was comparable between ME/CFS and healthy 
controls, as indicating different profiles that can be found 
in the real-world, the clinical relevance of these patterns 
differed between groups. The group with healthy light 
profile was consistently associated with more favorable 
clinical outcomes in the ME/CFS patients, including 
lower physical fatigue and anxiety/depression symptoms, 
fewer autonomic symptoms, reduced sleep complaints, 
and better quality of life, especially compared with the 
adverse light profile. It may be that in ME/CFS, altered 
circadian regulation or increased sensitivity to environ­
mental cues may amplify the physiological and behavioral 
consequences of suboptimal light exposure. The identifi­
cation of maladaptive light exposure patterns associated 
with worse fatigue, sleep quality, and health perception in 
ME/CFS provides preliminary evidence that behavioral 
and environmental modifications may represent feasible 
strategies for symptom management in the condition.
The absence of consistent associations between light 
exposure patterns and clinical outcomes in healthy con­
trols may partly reflect the lower variability and gener­
ally favorable range of outcome measures in this group, 

Page 14 of 18
Cambras et al. Journal of Translational Medicine         (2026) 24:1092 
limiting the ability to detect meaningful associations. 
Differences in sample size between groups may also have 
contributed to greater statistical power in the patient 
analyses. In contrast, individuals with ME/CFS showed 
a broader distribution of symptom severity and physi­
ological measures, which likely increased statistical sen­
sitivity to detect associations. However, importantly, the 
significant group × light exposure interaction observed 
in the combined analysis indicates that the relationship 
between light exposure patterns and clinical outcomes 
differs between ME/CFS patients and healthy controls.
It is of interest that L_2–6 was associated with circu­
lating triglycerides and VCAM-1 levels This is consistent 
with previous evidence linking abnormal light exposure 
and circadian disruption to adverse metabolic profiles 
and increased cardiometabolic risk, including dyslip­
idemia [41, 42]. In particular, light exposure during the 
biological night (light at night, LAN) has been associated 
with altered lipid metabolism, including elevated choles­
terol and LDL levels [43], potentially mediated through 
circadian misalignment of metabolic pathways [10, 44, 
45].
From a mechanistic perspective, light–dark cycles rep­
resent the primary environmental zeitgeber for the circa­
dian system, acting via intrinsically photosensitive retinal 
ganglion cells (ipRGCs), which project to the suprachias­
matic nucleus and other brain regions involved in sleep, 
neuroendocrine, and autonomic regulation [31, 46, 47]. 
Exposure to light at night can suppress melatonin and 
shift circadian phase, leading to circadian misalignment, 
which has been implicated in metabolic dysregulation, 
including impaired lipid homeostasis and increased 
cardiometabolic risk [48, 49]. In addition to meta­
bolic effects, circadian disruption has been associated 
with endothelial dysfunction and low-grade inflamma­
tion [50]. VCAM-1 is a marker of endothelial activation 
upregulated by inflammatory and oxidative stress path­
ways, thus, the observed association may reflect down­
stream vascular consequences of circadian misalignment 
[51], although causality cannot be inferred from the pres­
ent data.
The associations found with circulating serotonin levels 
should be interpreted with caution given the limited sam­
ple size. Also, while circadian and sleep-related mecha­
nisms may influence serotonergic regulation, evidence in 
humans remains limited and the underlying pathways are 
not yet fully established [52].
Thus, it is possible that altered light exposure contrib­
utes to symptom severity through mechanisms such as 
circadian misalignment, altered melatonin secretion, and 
autonomic dysfunction in ME/CFS patients.
Anyhow, these findings together support the hypoth­
esis that environmental light exposure may influence 
multiple physiological domains relevant to ME/CFS, 
including autonomic, metabolic, and affective regulation. 
Intervention studies in other clinical populations further 
support the relevance of light timing. In cancer-related 
fatigue, condition frequently associated with sleep dis­
ruption, morning bright light exposure has been shown 
to improve sleep quality and related symptoms [53]. Like­
wise, systematic evidence from meta-analysis data sug­
gests that light exposure and light-based interventions 
are associated with small-to-moderate improvements in 
subjective wellbeing in general populations [54]. In neu­
rological disorders such as traumatic brain injury and 
stroke, dynamic light interventions have demonstrated 
improvements in sleep and psychomotor performance, 
supporting the feasibility of home-based light approaches 
for fatigue-related symptoms [55].
This suggests that personalized chronotherapy inter­
ventions such as structured light therapy, increased 
daytime outdoor exposure, or reduction of evening and 
nighttime light may offer low-cost and non-invasive 
approaches with potential clinical benefit in individu­
als with ME/CFS. However, a randomized controlled 
crossover study involving 36 ME/CFS patients found that 
bright light therapy (+ 10.000 lx) administered 30 min 
each morning at home over two weeks was not signifi­
cantly effective for fatigue (assessed by Chalder Fatigue 
Questionnaire (CFQ), although improvement of fatigue 
scores was observed immediately after two weeks of BLT 
and also light seemed to have beneficial effects on atten­
tion in patients with ME/CFS [56].
Thus, environmental light exposure is a potentially 
modifiable behavioral factor with low cost and non-
invasive characteristics, that may improve sleep qual­
ity [57–59], circadian alignment [60], and mood-related 
outcomes [61], although these findings are not specific 
to ME/CFS and should be interpreted cautiously. The 
present findings suggest that the impact of light extends 
beyond simple measures of intensity or timing, encom­
passing also the stability, amplitude, and fragmentation of 
the circadian light–dark rhythm. This is especially impor­
tant, since vision-related symptoms are frequent in ME/
CFS and sensory hypersensitivity, including increased 
sensitivity to light stimuli, has also been frequently 
reported in ME/CFS [62]. Thus, high light intensity 
should not generally be recommended in ME/CFS, unless 
well tolerated.
Potential interventions in ME/CFS may need to con­
sider not only daytime light exposure, but also the tem­
poral organization of light across the 24-hour cycle, 
including the minimization of irregular exposure pat­
terns and nocturnal light. This is particularly relevant 
given the sensory hypersensitivity and symptom exacer­
bation that may occur in response to environmental stim­
uli in ME/CFS. Accordingly, light-based strategies should 
aim to reinforce stable circadian light–dark patterns 

Page 15 of 18
Cambras et al. Journal of Translational Medicine         (2026) 24:1092 
by promoting consistent daytime light exposure while 
reducing, and ideally avoiding, light exposure during the 
biological night. Such an approach may help mitigate the 
potential adverse effects associated with disrupted light–
dark rhythms and could be considered as a general, low-
risk behavioral recommendation in the management of 
ME/CFS.
Strengths and limitations
Despite several strengths, including the recording of 
actigraphy-derived 
measurements, 
dimensionality 
reduction via principal component analysis, and the 
assessment of interaction effects, this study has several 
limitations that should be acknowledged.
First, the observational cross-sectional cohort design 
precludes 
causal 
inference. 
Although 
associations 
between light exposure patterns and clinical outcomes 
were identified, the directionality of these relationships 
remains unclear. Moreover, reverse causation cannot be 
excluded, as individuals with more severe symptoms may 
adopt behaviors that reduce or shift light exposure, such 
as spending more time indoors or utilizing artificial light 
during nighttime awakenings. In this context, circadian 
misalignment may represent a downstream consequence 
that further contributes to fatigue, sleep disruption, dys­
autonomia, and endothelial dysfunction. Future prospec­
tive longitudinal studies are therefore required to clarify 
precise temporal and causal relationships.
Second, although actigraphy provides objective and 
ecologically valid estimates of environmental light expo­
sure in real-world settings, the recording period may not 
fully reflect habitual long-term exposure patterns. Impor­
tantly, wrist-worn light sensors do not accurately reflect 
retinal light exposure, as factors such as sleeve coverage 
and arm positioning relative to light sources may intro­
duce measurement error. In addition, standard photopic 
illuminance (lux) lacks spectral sensitivity and does not 
capture circadian-effective metrics such as melanopic 
illuminance, which more directly influence the human 
circadian system via non-visual retinal pathways. While 
any resulting measurement error is likely non-differential 
between groups, future studies should incorporate eye-
level wearable sensors or spectral light measurements to 
more precisely quantify biologically relevant light expo­
sure in ME/CFS patients.
Third, while the sample size was sufficient to detect 
moderate effects, the study may have been underpowered 
to identify smaller interaction effects or subtle subgroup 
differences, including those related to chronotype and 
comorbidities. In addition, the tertiary care setting may 
limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader 
ME/CFS population, particularly those with severe or 
bedbound disease.
Fourth, polypharmacy and concomitant use of psycho­
tropic or hypnotic medications in the ME/CFS cohort, 
may affect activity and may potentially introducing 
residual confounding. This highlights the need for more 
detailed stratification in future prospective longitudinal 
studies.
Lastly, although PCA allowed the reduction of com­
plex light exposure variables into a smaller set of inter­
pretable components, these data-driven patterns are 
inherently sample-dependent and require replication 
in independent cohorts to confirm their robustness 
and generalizability. Accordingly, the stratified analysis 
based on median splits of the four light exposure profiles 
should be interpreted with caution as exploratory and 
hypothesis-generating, providing a conceptual frame­
work for future research and complementing our primary 
continuous regression analyses.
Conclusions and recommendations for further 
research
To the best of our knowledge, this is the exploratory 
first study to provide evidence that environmental light 
exposure patterns are associated with illness severity 
and well-being in ME/CFS, particularly for fatigue, anxi­
ety/depression, dysautonomia, sleep disturbances, and 
health-related quality of life. Profiles characterized by 
stable daytime light exposure and reduced evening or 
nocturnal light exposure were generally associated with 
more favorable clinical outcomes measures among the 
participants. This study contributes to our understand­
ing of the role of the light exposure patterns in ME/CFS 
severity.
Overall, these findings extend previous analyses based 
on continuous light exposure metrics by suggesting that 
the temporal organization of light exposure, rather than 
intensity alone, may have clinically relevant associations 
in ME/CFS. This supports the notion that not only the 
quantity but also the timing and stability of light expo­
sure are critical determinants of symptom expression in 
ME/CFS.
From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight 
environmental light exposure as a potentially modifi­
able, non-invasive factor that may contribute to symptom 
management in ME/CFS. Behavioral strategies aimed 
at increasing daytime light exposure, if possible, while 
minimizing evening and nighttime light exposure and 
improving light timing may help support circadian align­
ment and improve sleep-wake cycle regulation in ME/
CFS.
Emerging studies have increasingly used wearable-
based approaches to assess sleep–wake patterns and 
rest-activity rhythms, underscoring the value of continu­
ous, ecologically valid monitoring to capture behavioral 
heterogeneity and circadian disruption in everyday life. 

Page 16 of 18
Cambras et al. Journal of Translational Medicine         (2026) 24:1092 
Within this context, actigraphy remains a well-estab­
lished and widely used method for the longitudinal 
assessment of rest-activity rhythms in clinical popula­
tions [63]. In parallel, advances in biomedical sensing and 
digital health technologies reflect a broader shift toward 
more sensitive, objective, and personalized approaches to 
physiological monitoring [64]. These developments pro­
vide general methodological context for the increasing 
use of wearable and biosensor-based tools in the post-
acute infection syndromes such as ME/CFS and Long 
COVID.
Thus, although promising, more evidence is needed to 
clarify the directionality and clinical relevance of these 
associations, and to determine whether targeted modula­
tion of light exposure may lead to meaningful improve­
ments of health status in ME/CFS. In this context, 
longitudinal study design and light assessment should 
consider not only photopic intensity (lux), but also cir­
cadian-effective metrics such as melanopic illuminance, 
spectral composition, and seasonal variation, all of which 
may critically influence circadian responses. Further 
research should also explore the potential neurobiologi­
cal mechanisms linking light exposure, circadian regula­
tion, and clinical characteristics including comorbidities 
in ME/CFS, and in other post-acute infection syndromes 
such as Long COVID.
