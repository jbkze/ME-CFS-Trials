Complex chronic conditions represent a growing health burden that is poorly 
understood and inadequately managed within current health systems. This 
review focuses on the potential of epigenetic DNA methylation to provide a path-
way for better understanding of four closely related conditions: myalgic encepha-
lomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), long COVID (LC), ﬁbromyalgia 
(FM), and hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (hEDS). These conditions have 
extensive overlapping symptoms, suggesting shared underlying pathophysiologi-
cal mechanisms. Epigenetic regulation, particularly DNA methylation, provides a 
powerful framework for comparative and longitudinal studies across these clini-
cally overlapping conditions. Here, we synthesise evidence from current DNA
methylation studies in ME/CFS, LC, and FM, and consider how an integrated
cross-condition approach could advance mechanistic insight, enable diagnostic
stratiﬁcation, and improve patient outcomes.

Complex chronic conditions
Complex chronic conditions are ongoing, lifelong diseases that are currently poorly managed 
within health systems. The COVID-19 pandem ic has added 400 million more cases to the health
burden of countries worldwide [1]. This has heightened interest among clinicians and scientists in 
the frequently misunderstood related conditions. Much greater attention from the research and 
clinical communities is timely to highlight strategies that promise better cross-condition manage-
ment and meaningful, evidence-based, effective treatments.
Currently, myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) [2], long COVID ( LC)
[3], and ﬁbromyalgia (FM) [4] are stand-alone complex chronic conditions, and yet they share many 
overlapping symptoms, with multilayered molecular and p hysiological dysfunction spanning diverse
biological systems [5]. Hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (hEDS) is less well characterised but 
is a future comparative arm [6], as hypermobility is found in ME/CFS patients as well as in stand-
alone hEDS. Consequential comorbidities, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) [7] 
and mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS) [8], occur at signiﬁcant frequencies in patients with 
these chronic conditions [5]. From the same triggering event, ME/CFS and FM can develop in 
different people [9,10]. A more coordinated, integrated approach to understanding the pathophys-
iology of these chronic conditions may provide cross-beneﬁts t o patients with each condition.

The familial, intergenerational occurrence of ME/CFS suggested genetic linkage to the condition
[18], and possible risk loci were highlighted in early genome-wide association studies (GWAS)
[19,20]. Precision Life Oxford added a combinatorial platform to GWAS, so that clusters of 
single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) could be interrogated. Remarkably, 199 SNP s within
14 clusters, linked to just 15 genes, identiﬁed 91% of the ME/CFS samples in UK Biobank [21]. 
This indicated that a complex pattern of natural genetic variations, rather than distinct mutations, 
was responsible for susceptible individuals developing ME/CFS. In 2025, the DecodeME GWAS
study of >15 000 ME/CFS patients revealed genetic linkage to a further 8 loci [22]. Application of 
the combinatorial platform analysis to the DecodeME dataset expanded the key SNPs to >7500,
now linked to 259 genes [23], highlighting the complexity of genetic susce ptibility risk factors.
Long COVID 
LC emerged rapidly in large numbers as a post-viral syn drome from the COVID-19 pandemic
[5,24,25]. Persistent clinical symptoms were given the name post-acute sequelae of SARS-
CoV-2 (PASC) [3,26]. The World Health Organisation (WHO) proposed the name ‘Post-COVID 
Condition’ for symptoms persisting for 3 months. ii The popularised name LC, encompassing 
both those with an ME/CFS-like syndrome [27] and those with organ damage, is now used almost 
exclusively for the documented 65 million cases iii and, more likely, 400 million cases [1]. Each new 
infection/reinfection with SARS-CoV-2, now an endemic virus, carries a risk of developing pe rsis-
tent, debilitating symptoms characteristic of LC [28]. Those who have recovered from LC within 
2 years carry an increased risk of cardiovascular, neurological, and metabolic disorders [24]. 
With individual testing for COVID no longer occurring, there is an urgent need for new, effective 
diagnostic tools to identify and follow the progression of LC. Better treatment strategies would miti-
gate the long-term consequ ences for the many millions affected by LC and ME/CFS worldwide.
Genetic susceptibility derived from a combinatorial GWAS of LC patients identiﬁed 39 SNPs that 
could be linked to nine genes identiﬁed in the combinatorial analysis of ME/CFS patients [29]. A 
GWAS study with >6000 patients with LC highlighted the gene encoding the FOXP4 transcription
factor, which is involved in lung physiology [30]. 
Fibromyalgia 
FM is a complex chronic disorder affecting up to 5% of the global population. It is classiﬁed as a 
musculoskeletal disorder in which the characteristic feature is widespread body pain [31]. The 
increased sensitivity to pain is likely the result of the central nervous system not processing pain 
signals normally and muscle stiffness [4]. It has extensive symptom overlap with ME/CFS and LC, 
including fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, cognitive difﬁculties with memory loss and ‘brain fog’, as well 
as digestive problems similar to those seen in ME/CFS. Familial l inkage in FM results in an 8- to 14-
fold increase in the risk of developing the condition among ﬁrst-degree relatives [32]. A recent 2026 
GWAS study with 54 000 patients identiﬁed 26 loci linked to FM with strong neural connections [33]. 
Hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos syndrome
hEDS is the major subtype (80–90%) among the 13 connective tissue disorders ari sing from
abnormal collagen formation and function [6]. It affects multiple organ systems and presents 
with clinical symptoms of skin hyperelasticity, joint hypermobility, and blood vessel fragility [34]. 
The genetic linkage of this subtype involves a combination of SNPs, as in ME/CFS and LC [35]. 
Despite hEDS being classiﬁed as a distinct condition, it manifests many of the same symptoms

Shared symptoms and overlapping clinical features
The core overlapping symptoms for ME/CFS, LC, FM, and hEDS are fatigue, cognitive impairment, 
unrefreshing sleep, and generalised and speciﬁc muscle and joint pain. Post-exertional malaise 
(PEM), a response to stress or activity, is a hallmark symptom of ME/CFS but has now been
well-documented in LC [38,39]. While FM patients have relapsing ‘ﬂares’ that have not generally 
been accepted as PEM, recent publications have strongly advo cated that PEM is a core symptom
of FM [40,41]. The signiﬁcant incidence of hypermobility in patients with ME/CFS complicates the 
understanding of whether PEM is a part of hEDS alone , but exercise intolerance has been reported
[42]. Whether the similar FM ﬂares and hEDS exercise intolerance are mediated by the same 
molecular and physiological changes as those in ME/CFS [43] needs to be determined and adds 
weight to the value of a more integrated approach. Dysautonomia is a feature of all conditions in 
which the regulation of involuntary physiological functions is abnormal, affecting blood pressure, 
cardiac rhythms, temperature, blood sugar, and gastrointestinal functions [44].

An epigenetic framework for complex chronic conditions
Various epigenetic characteristics have been investigated in complex chronic co nditions: DNA
methylation [45,46], histone modiﬁcations [47], and noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) [48]. Epigenetic 
changes can occur as a result of external environmental inﬂuences such as stress, diet, exercise, 
and chemical pollutants that affect gene expression by modulating gene regulators [49]. iv Such 
changes can negatively impact human health, leading to disease [49,50]. 
DNA methylation provides a framework for investigating complex chronic conditions because it 
captures the dynamic interaction between genetic suscepti bility, immune dysregulation, and
persistent changes in gene regulation [51]. The DNA methylome proﬁle can vary with t he
biological clock [52] and agein g [53], so it is important in patient studies of chronic diseases to 
use age- and sex-matched healthy controls. The authors have observed a very mild ageing effect 
in controls utilising principal component analysis, but not in the patient proﬁles, where the
disease-related changes were dominant [54]. 
Multi-omics studies of ME/CFS have demonstrated that epigenetic DNA methylome analyses 
yielded stable, consistent changes across patients, compared with analyses of the transcriptome
and proteome of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) [54–58]. The authors found that 
healthy controls had an approximately 0.1% change in DNA methylation month to month,
whereas ME/CFS patients were more variable, at approximately 2% [58]. 
Comparative studies of ME/CFS and LC with standardised study parameters showed common 
methylation changes at cytosine-phosphate guanine (CpG) sites in both conditions [54]. CpG 
sites are regions of DNA where a cytosine nucleotide sits next to a guanine nucleotide along 
the 5' to 3' direction and are considered as the main target sites for DNA methylation. A minority 
of changes at CpG sites differed between the cohorts, although not among patients within the
same cohort [54]. This implied that the selection of targeted regions could be suitable for

Could a DNA methylation susceptibility signature be developed for these chronic conditions? 
A study by Jason and colleagues of 4500 University of Chicago students investigated blood 
samples taken prior to some contracting an Epstein–Barr virus infection. The preinfection blood 
samples of those who developed ME/CFS showed immune irregu larities and lower cytokine
levels, but not behavioural differences, compared with those who did not develop ME/CFS
[63], as well as metabolic differences [64], implying a susceptibility ‘proﬁle’ linked to development
of the condition [65]. Such differences are inferred to be reﬂected in DNA methylation proﬁles.
How can epigenetic analysis close the knowledge gaps?
Major knowledge gaps greatly hinder our understanding and effective clinical management of 
chronic conditions. Epigenetic DNA methylation can be harnessed to address the lack of early 
biomarkers for molecula r diagnostic tests for health management immediately after onset, rather
than waiting several years for a deﬁnitive diagnosis [66]. As yet, possible molecular biomarkers for 
a simple, ﬁrst-step early diagnosis that could be accessible to all clinicians and their patients have 
not been fully validated. ME/CFS, LC, FM, and hEDS all lack a simple molecular diagnostic test 
that would enable early diagnosis and better disease management, and at least a chance for 
partial recovery. Having a diagnostic platform with targeted, common methylation changes that
could initially distinguish these chronic conditions from other diseases exhibiting persistent
chronic fatigue would be a huge advance for patient management.
Tests based on epigenetic molecular signatures unique to each chronic condition are possible, as 
illustrated by methylation data from a study comparing ME/CFS and LC [54]. They could eliminate 
diagnostic ambiguity and enable appropriate best-practice management to begin as soon as 
possible. This ﬁrst comparative study indicates that the DNA methylome has considerable prom-
ise for deriving molecular signatures with high selectivity a nd sensitivity for the development of
deﬁnitive early diagnostic tests, as outlined in the strategy (Figure 2, key ﬁgure).
Advanced service platforms have been developed for assessing clinical epigenetic tests, includ-
ing whole-genome bisulphite sequencing (WGBS) and chip technology (Inﬁnium arrays) for 
measuring DNA methylation. These platforms have facilitated epigenomic diagnostics and the 
scientiﬁc validation of diagnostic tests for clinical use in genetic conditions. Genome-wide epige-
netic signatures (episignatures) have been validated for diagnosis and phenotypic correlation in 
42 Mendelian neurodevelopmental disorders, and recently episignatures have been investigated 
clinical ly using EpiSign METRIC (Methylation-based episignature testing and reference-based
interpretation and classiﬁcation) software [67–70].

DNA methylation studies on ME/CFS
Most DNA methylation studies have used Illumina arrays [71–76], and some have used reduced 
representation bisulphite sequencing (RRBS) [43,54,57,58,99]. Genome-wide studies in ME/ 
CFS have primarily focused on PBMCs and speciﬁc immune cell subtypes (CD4+ and CD3+ T 
cells). Differentially methylated sites are typically deﬁned using thresholds of approximately 
5–15% change relative to healthy controls, although criteria have varied. The full body of
published work from 2014 to 2026 is summarised in Table 1. 
Array-based DNA methylome studies using Illumina 450K/EPIC platforms in PBMC-derived 
samples have identiﬁed widespread DMCs in ME/CFS. Most changes are hypermethylated 
(~72%) and associated with genes involved in cel lular metabolism, immune regulation, and
hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis function [52,53]. A study of 70 female ME/CFS patients iden-
tiﬁed four distinct subtypes based on DNA methylation patterns [54]. Surprisingly, an Illumina 
850K EPIC array–based study reported that approximately 98% of differentially methylated 
sites were hypomethylated, in sharp contrast to previous studies, but still predominantly associ-
ated with immune and cell signalling pathways [75].

More recent studies have used RRBS, a sequence-based approach for proﬁling genome-wide 
DNA methylation, for higher-resolution analysis of changes in ME/CFS (Table 1). By enriching 
for CpG-dense regulatory regions, RRBS enables more precise identiﬁcation of differentially 
methylated regions (DMRs) and provides greater insight into their potential regulatory and func-
tional signiﬁcance [76]. The ﬁrst RRBS-based methylation study in ME/CFS identiﬁed DMRs 
associated with 122 genes involved in neurological, immune, and metabolic pathways [57]. 
Subsequent longitudinal RRBS analysis examining PEM relapse–recovery dynamics in ME/CFS 
demonstrated that symptom exacerbation was accompanied by dynamic methylation changes 
at speciﬁc DMRs linked to inﬂammatory pathways, interleukin-8 signalling, nuclear factor

CFS [43].

DNA methylation studies of ﬁbromyalgia
DNA methylome studies in FM have used a greater diversity of tissue sources, including saliva and 
leukocytes. Both Illumina chip analyses and RRBS have been applied, with marked variation
among the methodologies used.
A whole blood pilot study of women identiﬁed 69 predominantly hypermethylated CpG sites and 
increased chromosomal instability. The affected genes were linked to neu ronal signaling, chro-
matin regulation, muscle function, oxidative stress, and inﬂammatory pathways [87]. 
A genome-wide whole blood study by contrast, identiﬁed 1,610 differentially methylated CpG 
sites, with most showing hypomethylation. These changes were enriched in pathways re lated
to immune function, oxidative stress, DNA repair, and neuronal signalling, and were associated
with altered cortical excitability [88]. The leukocyte studies with a large patient cohort produced 
a modest number of changes with the parameters used [89,90], and a saliva study revealed 
only ﬁve CpG differentially methylated sites, highlighting potential roles for immu ne and cellular
signalling pathways in disease pathology [91]. 
Limitations of published DNA methylation studies
Despite limitations of heterogeneity in cohort size and composition, tissue sources, analytical plat-
forms, and statistical thresholds, the studies reviewed here collectively have provided important 
insights into both the pathophysiology of the individual conditions and shared mechanisms. 
Heterogeneity and lifestyle confounders (smoking, BMI, medications, etc.) among patients with 
chronic conditions are potential limitations in currently published studies using this ap proach,
but precision medicine studies of individual patients have shown that the dominant changes
due to the condition override the inﬂuence of such confounders and do not materially affect the
output data [54,57,58]. However, differences in experimental design and analytical approaches 
currently limit the validity of direct cross-study comparisons and hinder the identiﬁcation of repro-
ducible and more precise disease-associated methylation signatures. Future progress will require

Improving treatment options for complex chronic conditions
Despite the substantial clinical burden, effective evidence-based therapies for these conditions 
remain extremely limited, highlighting the urgent need for biologically informed precision medicine
approaches [62]. Changes in DNA methylation patterns can identify promising targets within 
biochemical pathways for the development of evidence-based, speciﬁc, targeted treatments. 
Since effective treatments are so limited, a new paradigm is needed, tailored to the complexity 
of these chronic conditions, rather than targeting individual s ymptoms. Despite the limitations
due to extensive heterogeneity in the analytical parameters used in the studies in Tables 1– 3
and Table S1 in the Supplemental information online, combined gene enrichment analysis of
the individual studies (Figure 3) reveals key regulatory pathways and physiological processes 
for targeted treatments for ME/CFS, LC, and FM, as well as key overlapping physiological
aspects that could inform common treatments.

cellular stress, supporting the concept that these clinically overlapping conditions may share

Concluding remarks and future perspectives
The global emergence of Long COVID has brought unprecedented visibility to complex chronic 
conditions, transforming what were once ignored or poorly understood conditions into a major 
public health priority. This has exposed critical gaps in healthcare systems, with delayed diagno-
sis, limited therapeutic options, and fragmented models of care. These observations explain the 
longstanding challenges faced by patients and their absence from mainstream clinical response 
pathways, leading them to rely instead on patient support networks with limited resources. The 
scale and urgency of LC now provide a unique opportunity and impetus to evaluate and 
stren gthen clinical, research, and policy frameworks for this broader class of chronic conditions.
For LC, a relatively recent condition existing for 5–6 years, insights from patients with ME/CFS
who have been affected for much longer periods since onset can provide insights into the ongo-
ing LC pathophysiology [98]. 
Addressing these challenges will require new approaches that capture the biological complexity 
underlying these conditions (see Outstanding question s). Converging evidence of multilayered 
dysregulation across immune, metabolic, neurological, and autonomic systems indicates that 
single-symptom/discipline-focused models are inadequate. As described in this article, epige-
netic regulation, particularly DNA methylation, offers a powerful framework for linking environmen-
tal triggers, host susceptibility, and persistent changes in gene regulation. Its relative stability, 
sensitivity, and accessibility in peripheral tissues position it as a leading candidate for biomarker 
development, disease stratiﬁcation, and monitoring of disease trajectories. Future approaches, 
however, should also prioritise the inte gration of multi-omic data and well-characterised clinical
cohorts to disentangle the shared and condition-speciﬁc mechanisms. Rather than representing
entirely distinct disorders, ME/CFS, LC, FM, and EDS may ultimately be shown to exist along an
overlapping epigenetic and physiological spectrum, shaped by shared mechanisms of maladap-
tive chronic stress responses.
Translating the current biological and epigenetic advances into clinical practice will require consis-
tently applied, robust, scalable diagnostic tools that can be implemented in routine healthcare 
settings. The identiﬁcation of shared and condition-speciﬁc epigenetic signatures could enable 
a tiered diagnostic approach to distinguish speciﬁc disease subtypes. Such stratiﬁcation would 
provide a critical foundation, allowing treatments to be tailored to the underlying mol ecular and
physiological proﬁles of individual patients, rather than relying solely on current trial-and-error
approaches.
