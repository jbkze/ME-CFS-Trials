Abstract
Post-exertional malaise (PEM), the defining feature of myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/
CFS), is increasingly recognized in individuals with Long COVID. Although traditionally viewed as symptom 
exacerbation and/or emergence of new symptoms following exertion, PEM may reflect disrupted coordination 
across interconnected physiological systems operating over distinct post-exertional timescales. Such disruption 
may result in altered post-exertional physiological trajectories that contribute to multisystem manifestations and 
potential cardiometabolic consequences. This perspective outlines a conceptual approach for investigating PEM 
through longitudinal assessment of post-exertional physiological trajectories while considering disease severity 
and underlying cardiometabolic health, including obesity and type 2 diabetes. Integrating these dimensions may 
help contextualize post-exertional responses, inform future longitudinal cardiometabolic profiling, facilitate patient 
stratification, and provide a framework for future mechanistic and interventional studies in ME/CFS and Long 
COVID.

​From post-exertional homeostasis to post-
exertional malaise
The physiological response to physical exertion depends 
on coordinated interactions across multiple organs 
and tissues. In healthy individuals, physical activity is 
accompanied by integrated cardiovascular, endothe­
lial, metabolic, endocrine, autonomic, muscular and 
immune responses, together with dynamic regulation of 
energy production, substrate utilization, and fluid bal­
ance, all of which help maintain systemic homeostasis. 
These responses are normally coordinated across physi­
ological systems and timescales, contributing to adaptive 
post-exertional trajectories that ultimately restore physi­
ological homeostasis. Maintaining the integrity of these 
adaptive responses is fundamental to cardiometabolic 
homeostasis [1–4].
Within the spectrum of post-acute infection syn­
dromes (PAIS) [5], post-exertional malaise (PEM) is the 
defining feature of myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic 
fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) [6] and is increasingly rec­
ognized in individuals with Long COVID [7, 8]. These 
two conditions share symptom profiles [9], providing an 
opportunity to investigate post-exertional pathophysiol­
ogy [10]. Traditionally, PEM is defined as the worsening 
of symptoms following physical, cognitive, or emotional 
exertion [6]. Although PEM may be triggered by differ­
ent forms of exertion, this perspective focuses primarily 
on physiological responses to physical exertion and the 
post-exertional trajectories that may contribute to the 
cardiometabolic manifestations of ME/CFS and Long 
COVID. The substantial overlap between the physiologi­
cal systems involved in post-exertional adaptation and 
those implicated across the spectrum of cardiometabolic 
disease suggests that investigating post-exertional physi­
ological trajectories may provide broader insights into 
cardiometabolic dysfunction across conditions such as 
obesity, metabolic syndrome, and type 2 diabetes (T2D).
From this perspective, PEM may reflect a broader dis­
turbance in the coordination of multiple physiological 
systems rather than simply the consequence of exertion. 
While the mechanisms underlying PEM remain incom­
pletely understood [10], one possibility is that physi­
ological processes that normally operate in a coordinated 
manner become transiently or persistently dysregulated, 
contributing to altered post-exertional physiological tra­
jectories across interconnected systems (Fig. 1). This 
interpretation is consistent with evidence of abnormali­
ties involving cardiovascular, endothelial, metabolic, 
endocrine, autonomic, muscular and immune systems 
both in ME/CFS [11] and Long COVID [7], although the 
causality between these abnormalities and PEM remains 
uncertain. Viewed from this perspective, PEM may also 
provide a unique research opportunity to understand 
how cardiometabolic adaptation is achieved under 
conditions of physiological stress and how underlying 
cardiometabolic health shapes post-exertional physiolog­
ical trajectories.
PEM beyond symptom exacerbation
Notwithstanding its defining feature of both ME/CFS and 
Long COVID, PEM remains incompletely assessed and 
characterized in Long COVID cohorts [12]. Relatively 
few studies have incorporated pre- and post-exertional 
approaches to assess PEM directly [10]. As a result, PEM 
is typically viewed from a diagnostic or clinical perspec­
tive rather than as a pathophysiological process requiring 
mechanistic investigation.
Recent recommendations for Long COVID clini­
cal trials further highlight the complexity of PEM as a 
pathophysiological phenomenon [13]. PEM may exhibit 
delayed onset following exertion and is characterized by 
substantial temporal variability, prolonged recovery, and, 
in some cases, incomplete return to pre-exertional base­
line functional capacity. Consequently, the physiological 
state of a participant at the time of assessment may sub­
stantially influence the interpretation of study outcomes. 
This concept is supported by emerging cardiovascular 
and cardiometabolic evidence showing that physiologi­
cal abnormalities in ME/CFS may become more apparent 
under orthostatic challenge than under resting condi­
tions, indicating that the physiological context in which 
patients are evaluated can substantially influence the 
observed phenotype [14]. These recommendations also 
emphasize documenting whether participants are expe­
riencing PEM during data collection, while recognizing 
that study procedures themselves may influence symp­
tom expression during the assessment period [13, 15].
Importantly, the physiological impact of exertion is 
unlikely to be the same across all levels of disease sever­
ity. While individuals with mild or moderate disease may 
tolerate certain exertional assessment protocols, includ­
ing cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET), hand­
grip, or tilt-table testing [16–18], those who are severely 
affected or bed-bound often live with limited physiologi­
cal reserve and may experience symptom worsening in 
response to challenges far less demanding than those 
imposed by conventional exertional testing [19]. In these 
patients, PEM may not simply reflect a more pronounced 
version of the response observed in milder cases, but a 
distinct physiological state characterized by altered post-
exertional trajectories and reduced capacity to restore 
physiological homeostasis following exertion [20, 21]. 
These observations highlight the need to consider dis­
ease severity both when interpreting study findings and 
when selecting the timing and nature of physiological 
assessments.
This distinction becomes particularly relevant as 
the physiological systems involved in post-exertional

adaptation operate across different timescales. Some 
responses, including autonomic, hemodynamic, vascu­
lar, and endothelial adjustments, occur within seconds 
to minutes after exertion. Others unfold over hours to 
days and involve inflammatory signaling, hepatic and 
systemic metabolic regulation, substrate utilization, 
immune-metabolic interactions, and energetic processes. 
Longer-term alterations may further influence broader 
cardiometabolic regulation, potentially contributing 
to distinct cardiometabolic profiles following repeated 
exertional episodes [22, 23]. One possibility is that PEM 
reflects a disruption in the normal temporal coordination 
of physiological responses to physical exertion (Fig. 1). 
Such desynchronization may contribute both to the het­
erogeneity of responses observed across individuals with 
ME/CFS and Long COVID and to inconsistent findings 
when physiological responses are assessed at different 
stages of the post-exertional period.
This temporal complexity highlights the importance 
of longitudinal assessment. As post-exertional responses 
evolve at different rates across physiological domains 
[1–4], understanding PEM depends not only on what is 
measured, but also on when it is measured. Viewed from 
this perspective, the timing of assessment may become 
as important as the nature of the assessment itself [24, 
25]. Together, these observations suggest that character­
ization of PEM depends not only on when physiological 
responses are measured, but also on the physiological 
context in which they are assessed. Integrating these tem­
poral and physiological dimensions may therefore be 
critical for understanding the heterogeneity of PEM and 
identifying distinct post-exertional physiological tra­
jectories across biologically and clinically meaningful 
patient subgroups.

Importantly, immune, inflammatory, neurovascular, 
and viral persistence mechanisms proposed in ME/CFS 
and Long COVID are not mutually exclusive with the 
perspective proposed here. Rather, PEM may represent 
a physiological state in which diverse biological distur­
bances converge and become expressed through altered 
coordination across interconnected systems following 
exertion. If so, a major challenge may not be to identify 
a single mechanism underlying PEM, but to understand 
how multiple mechanisms interact across systems and 
timescales to shape post-exertional physiological tra­
jectories, and whether these interactions define distinct 
cardiometabolic profiles with different clinical and bio­
logical manifestations.
Future clinical and therapeutic considerations
The potential influence of cardiometabolic health, includ­
ing pre-existing or acquired obesity, metabolic syndrome, 
and T2D, remains underexplored in ME/CFS and Long 
COVID research. These observations raise the possibil­
ity that underlying cardiometabolic health contributes to 
distinct post-exertional physiological trajectories rather 
than simply representing a clinical comorbidity. Greater 
inclusion of individuals across different cardiometabolic 
health profiles may provide important insights into how 
pre-existing cardiometabolic conditions influence post-
exertional trajectories and contribute to the heteroge­
neity of PEM. Indeed, the relationship between Long 
COVID and T2D has emerged as an area of increas­
ing research interest, with ongoing nationwide studies 
specifically designed to evaluate its epidemiology and 
long-term health consequences [28]. However, cardio­
metabolic health is unlikely to be the only source of vari­
ability influencing post-exertional responses.
Beyond cardiometabolic health, factors such as age, 
biological sex, disease duration, and disease severity are 
also likely to influence both post-exertional physiological 
responses and the trajectories of post-exertional symp­
toms. Rather than simply modifying the magnitude of 
PEM, these characteristics may shape distinct post-exer­
tional physiological trajectories across systems and tim­
escales. Future studies should therefore consider these 
dimensions in both study design and data interpretation 
to improve patient stratification and identify biologically 
and clinically meaningful patient subgroups.
A better understanding of these sources of variation 
may also help inform the evaluation of cardiometabolic 
interventions in PAIS [29]. Moreover, the growing inter­
est in repurposing established cardiometabolic thera­
pies, including metformin, reflects an evolving view 
that cardiometabolic pathways may represent potential 
therapeutic targets in ME/CFS and Long COVID [30].

A cardiometabolic interpretation of recurrent post-
exertional dysregulation
Over the course of these chronic conditions, individuals 
with ME/CFS and Long COVID experience recurrent 
cycles of physical exertion, PEM, and prolonged physi­
ological disturbance [6, 7, 10, 15]. The cumulative patho­
physiological consequences of these repeated episodes 
remain largely unexplored. One possibility is that recur­
rent episodes of PEM are accompanied by disruption 
of the coordinated physiological responses required for 
post-exertional adaptation. Although individual episodes 
are often transient, repeated disruption of physiological 
system coordination could contribute to alterations in 
post-exertional physiological trajectories and cumulative 
disturbances in cardiometabolic regulation over time, 
potentially leading to distinct cardiometabolic profiles 
across individuals (Fig. 1).
A growing body of evidence implicates abnormali­
ties involving endothelial function, oxygen extraction, 
autonomic regulation, lactate metabolism, coagulation 
pathways, metabolic flexibility, glucose homeostasis, and 
lipid metabolism both in ME/CFS [11] and Long COVID 
[7]. Reduced stroke volume, cardiac output, and cere­
bral blood flow, particularly during orthostatic challenge, 
have been consistently reported in ME/CFS, providing 
objective evidence of impaired cardiovascular adapta­
tion [26]. Moreover, impaired capillary blood flow asso­
ciated with endothelial dysfunction and vascular wall 
alterations has also been proposed as a contributor to 
impaired cardiovascular adaptation in ME/CFS and Long 
COVID [27]. Although these findings are often investi­
gated separately, their frequent co-occurrence raises the 
possibility that they represent interconnected manifesta­
tions of a broader systems-level disturbance, rather than 
isolated abnormalities affecting individual physiological 
pathways.
If PEM reflects a disruption in the coordinated recov­
ery of physiological systems following exertion, repeated 
episodes of post-exertional dysregulation could have 
consequences that extend beyond transient symptom 
worsening. Over time, recurrent disturbances across car­
diovascular, autonomic, endothelial, metabolic, immune, 
and energetic pathways could contribute to a cumulative 
cardiometabolic burden, the magnitude of which may 
depend on the frequency, duration, and persistence of 
PEM episodes. Longitudinal cardiometabolic profiling 
may therefore help characterize post-exertional physi­
ological trajectories and determine whether recurrent 
disturbances are associated with cumulative cardiometa­
bolic burden, particularly across individuals with differ­
ent cardiometabolic health profiles. Whether recurrent 
PEM contributes directly to such burden or instead 
reflects underlying pathophysiological processes that also 
influence disease severity, remains uncertain.

Whether such interventions influence PEM remains 
unknown. However, interventions capable of modulat­
ing multiple physiological systems simultaneously and 
improving cardiometabolic function may not only offer 
therapeutic potential but also provide an opportunity to 
determine whether improving cardiometabolic function 
modifies post-exertional physiological trajectories and 
ultimately influences the manifestation and heterogeneity 
of PEM.
Concluding remarks
PEM has traditionally been viewed as symptom exac­
erbation and/or emergence of new symptoms follow­
ing exertion. This perspective proposes that PEM may 
instead reflect disrupted coordination across intercon­
nected physiological systems operating over distinct 
post-exertional timescales. Viewed from this perspective, 
recurrent post-exertional disturbances may contribute 
not only to symptom burden, but also to altered cardio­
metabolic adaptation and broader cardiometabolic con­
sequences in ME/CFS and Long COVID. Rather than 
viewing PEM solely through a clinical lens, this perspec­
tive proposes that PEM provides an opportunity to inves­
tigate how physiological systems coordinate and adapt 
over time following physiological stress. Integrating 
longitudinal physiological assessment with longitudinal 
cardiometabolic profiling may therefore help identify dis­
tinct post-exertional physiological trajectories, improve 
patient stratification, and generate new hypotheses 
regarding the mechanisms linking post-exertional dys­
regulation to cardiometabolic manifestations in ME/CFS 
and Long COVID.
Key questions
 	•
Does PEM reflect disrupted coordination across 
physiological systems and timescales?
 	•
Can distinct post-exertional physiological trajectories 
define patient subgroups?
 	•
Does recurrent post-exertional physiological 
dysregulation contribute to cumulative 
cardiometabolic burden?
 	•
How do the timing of assessment and disease 
heterogeneity influence the identification of post-
exertional physiological trajectories?
 	•
Can longitudinal cardiometabolic profiling identify 
biomarkers associated with distinct post-exertional 
trajectories?
Together, addressing these questions may help determine 
whether PEM represents disruption of the coordinated 
physiological responses that shape post-exertional physi­
ological trajectories, and how these alterations contribute 
to the multisystem and cardiometabolic manifestations 
of ME/CFS and Long COVID. Ultimately, advancing 
our understanding of PEM may require moving beyond 
symptom characterization toward the longitudinal inves­
tigation of post-exertional physiological trajectories 
across interconnected physiological systems and tim­
escales, while integrating cardiometabolic health as an 
important source of biological heterogeneity, particularly 
in individuals with obesity and T2D, in future mechanis­
tic and interventional studies.
