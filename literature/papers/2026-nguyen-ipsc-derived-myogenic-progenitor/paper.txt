Abstract
Background
Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a debilitating multisystem disorder characterised by
profound fatigue, post-exertional malaise, muscle pain and weakness. Although skeletal muscle abnormalities and
mitochondrial alterations have been reported in people with ME/CFS, the underlying biological processes remain poorly
understood, and no validated disease models, biomarkers or effective treatments are currently available. This study used
patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) myogenic progenitor (MP) cells to investigate intrinsic transcriptomic
abnormalities in ME/CFS and identify potential drug repurposing candidates.
Methods
MP cells were generated from iPSCs derived from people with ME/CFS and healthy controls, and successfully
characterised. Global mRNA sequencing was performed to compare transcriptomic profiles between ME/CFS and control
MP cells (n = 6 ME/CFS; n = 7 controls). Differentially expressed genes and pathways were identified, followed by drug
repurposing analysis using the Library of Integrated Network-based Cellular Signatures drug database (LINCS2) and
supporting literature review.
Results
RNA sequencing identified seven differentially expressed genes at adjusted p < 0.05, including MIR205HG, FEZF1, HLA-DMB,
SLC1A2, SYT13, GALNT4, and SLC2A14. Pathway analysis identified 73 differentially expressed pathways, of which 96%
were downregulated in ME/CFS MP cells. Downregulated pathways included those involved in cell cycle regulation, DNA
replication, mismatch repair, immune response and muscle cytoskeleton regulation-related pathways. In contrast,
upregulated pathways were associated with metabolic reprogramming, including increased reliance on branched-chain
amino acids for energy production. These findings suggest intrinsic abnormalities in ME/CFS-derived MP cells, particularly
involving metabolic regulation, mitochondrial function and muscle-related cellular processes. Based on the differentially
expressed genes, LINCS2-based drug repurposing analysis and literature review identified 22 candidate drugs with
potential relevance to ME/CFS. These included safe and widely available agents such as leflunomide, melatonin and
midodrine, which target pathways related to antiviral defence, immunomodulation, neurotransmitter modulation, autonomic
regulation and vascular function.
Conclusions
Patient-derived iPSC-MP cells provide a useful model for investigating intrinsic skeletal muscle-related abnormalities in
ME/CFS. Transcriptomic profiling revealed altered pathways related to cell cycle regulation, immune function, cytoskeletal
organisation and energy metabolism, supporting the involvement of mitochondrial and metabolic dysfunction in ME/CFS
pathobiology. Drug repurposing analysis identified several clinically relevant candidates that may warrant further functional
validation as potential therapeutic options for ME/CFS.
Introduction
Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a chronic disease known to affect around 67 million
people worldwide [1]. ME/CFS affects the quality of life of both patients and their caregivers, and imposes a significant
economic burden estimated to cost up to US$362 billion annually in the USA [2] and up to $14 billion in Australia [3].
However, approximately 84–91% of individuals with ME/CFS remain undiagnosed, largely due to the diagnostic complexity
Page 2/27

of the condition, heterogeneity, overlap with other disorders, the absence of validated biomarkers and effective treatments
that incentivise help seeking [4]. Despite decades of research, there are still no approved drug treatments, biomarkers, or
clinical practice guidelines for ME/CFS [5].
Post-exertional malaise (PEM) is recognised as a hallmark of ME/CFS [6]. Muscle pain (myalgia) and weakness, which can
worsen for several days following even minor physical exertion, are common and are often reported to be more disabling
than the fatigue itself [7]. Reduction of hand grip strength and quadricep strength are frequently observed in ME/CFS
patients and correlate with the severity of disease progression [8, 9]. MRI, electron imaging and histology studies have
shown damage to skeletal muscle, mitochondrial dysfunction and an increase in anaerobic metabolism in skeletal muscle
of ME/CFS patients [6, 10, 11]. While identifying specific molecular targets for ME/CFS remains an ideal goal, it has been
largely elusive and target-based drug discovery in ME/CFS has yet to translate into successful clinical treatments [12].
Cells derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) retain the genetic information of the original donor [13], enabling
the identification of genetic signatures and pathway alterations that may distinguish patients and healthy cohorts, and
which can facilitate the discovery of disease biomarkers and accelerate the development of new therapeutic approaches
through drug repurposing and drug discovery. Gene expression signatures (GES) obtained via RNA sequencing from iPSC-
derived cells have been successfully used to identify biomarkers and screen potential drug candidates for
neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and frontotemporal dementia [14, 15], as well as major
depressive disorder [16, 17], for which primary cells are difficult or impossible to obtain. One of the key advantages of using
iPSC-derived cells is that transcriptomic data from RNA-sequencing of these cells can allow us to bypass the lack of known
pathophysiological mechanisms to identify potential novel therapeutics [18]. When combined with drug databases,
transcriptomic profiles enable the screening of drugs that may reverse the gene expression differences observed between
cells derived from patients and healthy controls. Drug repurposing candidates include those that are generally already
approved or undergoing clinical trials, with established mechanisms of action and known safety profiles. This approach
significantly shortens the timeline for therapeutic development, which is typically lengthy and uncertain in conventional
drug discovery. Thus, iPSC-derived cells from ME/CFS patients and healthy donors can serve as a powerful tool for
uncovering disease-specific gene expression signatures in muscle cells and identifying targeted drug candidates capable
of reversing transcriptomic abnormalities in muscle cells of people with ME/CFS.
The present study is the first to use induced pluripotent stem cell - derived myogenic progenitors (iPSC-MPs) generated
from blood samples collected from both people with ME/CFS and healthy controls. The aim is to discover gene and
pathway signatures associated with the disease, and to identify transcriptomic targeted drug candidates for repurposing
based on the alignment of data from transcriptomic signatures, the LINCS2 drug database, and literature review. Findings
from this study will contribute to advancing drug repurposing efforts, bolster the translational potential of identified
approved drugs, and add to the development of diagnostic biomarkers and novel therapeutic strategies for ME/CFS.
Methods details
This study received ethics approval from Barwon Health Human Research Ethics Committee (20/202) and Deakin
University Human Research Ethics Committee (2021 − 165), Australia.
Study design, participant recruitment and sample collection
A case-control study was conducted at the School of Medicine, Deakin University, Australia. Thirteen participants, including
six ME/CFS cases and seven healthy controls were recruited to donate blood samples for generating iPSCs. Potential
participants with ME/CFS were identified through the Geelong ME/CFS Support Group and Emerge Australia. Control
participants were recruited from the community via advertisement flyers. After a telephone screen, eligible participants
were invited to join the study. Recruitment occurred between June 2021 and December 2023. Inclusion criteria included:
aged 18 to 65 years, diagnosed with ME/CFS by an independent physician and fulfil the criteria for ME/CFS using the
Page 3/27

Canadian Consensus Criteria [19], and have the capacity to consent to and comply with study procedures. Participants
currently under any form of therapy for ME/CFS needed to have been on that therapy for at least 4 weeks prior to
enrolment. Exclusion criteria included known or suspected active and unstable systemic medical disorder, acute suicidality,
current diagnosis of a psychotic disorder (including bipolar disorder, substance abuse/dependence, eating disorder, or
personality disorder), or a diagnosis of epilepsy. Trained researchers conducted the screening assessments.
Peripheral blood samples were collected from all participants by a trained nurse, processed to isolate peripheral blood
mononuclear cells (PBMCs), and subsequently reprogrammed into iPSCs. The pluripotency of the iPSCs was validated
using standard quality control assays. These cells were then differentiated into myogenic progenitor cells (MPs) and used
for RNA sequencing, gene signature analysis, and in silico drug screening (Fig. 1).
Hand Grip strength test
Repeat hand grip strength was tested using a hand-held dynamometer. Participants were seated comfortably with their
forearms resting on the arms of the chair and asked to squeeze the dynamometer for as long and as tightly as possible,
then rest for 30 seconds and repeat another two times. The process was then repeated for the other hand.
Karnofsky Performance Status Measurement
The functional status of participants was assessed using the Australian-modified Karnofsky Performance Status (AKPS)
scale, a validated instrument for quantifying a patient’s ability to carry out daily activities and self-care functions [20]. The
AKPS score ranges from 100 (normal, no complaints or evidence of disease) to 0 (death), in 10-point increments reflecting
progressive levels of disability. Participants were asked to describe their ability to perform routine work and personal
activities, along with any limitations related to fatigue, pain, or other disabling symptoms. Scoring adhered to the original
Karnofsky criteria, with adjustments where appropriate to reflect chronic illness populations, which were used to assess
overall functional capacity. Lower scores among ME/CFS participants indicated greater disability, consistent with disease
severity, whereas healthy controls were expected to score within the 80–100 range.
Multidimensional Fatigue Scale assessment
Fatigue severity and its impact on daily functioning were assessed using the Multidimensional Fatigue Scale (MFI), a
validated instrument commonly used in chronic illness populations, including ME/CFS [21]. The MFI is a 20-item self-report
questionnaire that evaluates the perceived impact of fatigue on five key domains: general fatigue, physical fatigue, reduced
motivation, reduced activity, and mental fatigue. Respondents use a scale ranging from 1 to 7 to indicate how much they
agree or disagree with certain statements regarding fatigue to represent their experiences. Scores were summed up to
produce a total fatigue impact score (range: 0–84), with higher scores indicating greater fatigue-related impairment. The
MFI assessment was administered either electronically or on paper, depending on participant preference and accessibility.
PBMC isolation from participant blood samples and generation of iPSCs
Peripheral blood samples were collected from participants using EDTA vacutainer tubes (Becton Dickinson Sciences).
Samples were diluted 1:2 (v/v) with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Gibco) containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco)
and 2 mM EDTA (Invitrogen), then transferred to SepMate™-15 tubes (STEMCELL Technologies) preloaded with
Lymphoprep (STEMCELL Technologies). Following centrifugation at 1,200 × g for 10 min at room temperature, the PBMC
layer was collected and transferred to a fresh tube. PBMCs were washed once with wash buffer and centrifuged at 300 × g
for 10 min. To remove residual erythrocytes, the cell pellet was briefly resuspended in ice-cold Milli-Q water, followed by an
additional wash in wash buffer and centrifugation at 300 × g for 10 min. The resulting PBMC pellet was cultured in
Page 4/27

StemSpan™ SFEM II medium (STEMCELL Technologies) supplemented with StemSpan™ erythroid expansion supplement
(STEMCELL Technologies). Reprogramming was performed using the Epi5™ Episomal iPSC Reprogramming Kit (Thermo
Fisher Scientific) delivered via the Neon™ Transfection System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s
instructions. Transduced cells were plated onto Geltrex™-coated (Gibco) culture dishes and maintained in complete
mTeSR™ Plus medium (STEMCELL Technologies) with daily medium changes. Individual iPSC colonies with characteristic
morphology were manually selected and expanded in Geltrex-coated flasks in complete mTeSR™ Plus medium. Passaging
was performed using ReLeSR™ (STEMCELL Technologies). iPSCs at passages 5 and 10 were cryopreserved in liquid
nitrogen for long-term storage. Characterisation of all iPSC lines was conducted between passages 10 and 12 to confirm
pluripotency and genomic stability. Cell stocks were stored in liquid nitrogen for subsequent experiments.
Karyotyping
Cells at passages 10–12 were dissociated using TrypLE (Gibco), centrifuged at 300 x g for 5 min, and cell pellet was stored
at -20°C until analysis. Karyotyping analysis was performed using the Illumina Infinium GSA-24 v3.0 array and aligned to the
GRCh38/hg38 human reference genome (Dec 2013) (Victorian Clinical Genetics Services, VIC, Australia).
Mycoplasma testing
Mycoplasma contamination was assessed using the MycoAlert® Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza Bioscience) following
the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were cultured in T25 flasks containing 8 mL of medium without a medium change for 48
h. Subsequently, 1 mL of conditioned medium was collected for testing. Results were interpreted against assay negative
and positive controls, with ratios < 0.9 indicating a negative result and > 1.2 indicating a positive result (Supplementary
Table 1).
Flow cytometry analysis
Cells at passages 10–12 were harvested using TrypLE and stained with BD Horizon™ Fixable Viability Stain 570 (Becton
Dickinson Sciences) for 15 min to assess viability. Then, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Thermo Fisher
Scientific) for 15 min, followed by incubation with conjugated antibodies against CD9, SSEA4, and EPCAM for 30 min.
Samples were analysed on a BD FACSCanto™ (Becton Dickinson Sciences).
Pluripotency marker immunocytochemistry
Cells were seeded into Geltrex-coated 24-well plates and grown to ~ 60% confluency. After washing with warm PBS, cells
were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (ProSciTech) for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilised with 0.5% Triton X-100
(Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS for 15 min and blocked with blocking buffer (3% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS) for 1
h. Primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution were applied for 3 h at room temperature. Following three washes in PBS,
cells were incubated with secondary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer for 1 h, then washed a further three times with
PBS. Nuclei were counterstained with NucBlue® (Hoechst 33342; Invitrogen) during the final wash for 5 min. Imaging was
performed using an EVOS M700 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a 10x objective; images are unprocessed,
with scale bars representing 275um.
Germ layer differentiation
Trilineage differentiation of iPSCs into ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm was performed using the STEMdiff™ Trilineage
Differentiation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies). iPSCs were seeded on Matrigel-coated (Corning) plates in
mesoderm/endoderm plating medium or ectoderm medium. After 24 h, lineage-specific STEMdiff™ medium was added,
with daily medium changes until day 5 for mesoderm and endoderm or day 7 for ectoderm. Cells were passaged on day 5
(mesoderm and endoderm) or day 7 (ectoderm) and cultured for a further 2 days. Cells were then fixed with 4%
Page 5/27

paraformaldehyde in PBS for immunofluorescence analysis or harvested in RLT buffer (Qiagen) for gene expression.
Imaging was performed on an EVOS M700 Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific,) using a 20× objective; images are
unprocessed, with scale bars representing 150 µm.
Proliferation
Cells were seeded into 24-well plates coated with Geltrex and cultured for 72 hours in complete mTeSR™ Plus medium, with
daily medium changes. Cells were harvested in QuickExtract DNA Extraction Solution (Biosearch Technologies), and gDNA
was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total DNA was quantified using a NanoDrop
spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 0, 24, 48, and 72 hours. Analysis shows normalised values to 0h (total
number of cells seeded = amount of DNA seeded in each well).
RNA Extraction and quantitative PCR
RNA was extracted using RNeasy® mini kits (Qiagen) and reverse-transcribed to produce cDNA using Maxima H Minus
first strand cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The Quant-iT™
OliGreen® ssDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen) was used to quantify the cDNA concentration in each sample as per the
manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was used to measure the expression of specific genes, as
shown in Supplementary Table 2. The experiments were performed on a QuantStudio 3 Real-time PCR system (Thermo
Fisher Scientific), using the following protocol: 95° for 7 min, 40 cycles of 95° for 30s and 60° for 1 min followed by data
acquisition, 60° for 30s, 55–95°, data acquisition and 20° for 10s. Gene expression data was quantified using the ΔΔCt
method normalised to the measured cDNA concentration and housekeeping gene GAPDH expression levels, then
categorised into ME/CFS and control groups.
MP differentiation
Myogenic progenitor (MP) cells were differentiated from iPSCs. Briefly, iPSCs were seeded at a density of 3.4 × 10⁶ cells
per well in Matrigel-coated 6-well plates using DMEM/F-12 GlutaMAX (Gibco) supplemented with STEMdiff™ Myogenic
Progenitor supplements A, B, C or D (STEMCELL Technologies). Media was changed daily as follows: Medium A (days 0–
1), Medium B (days 2–3), Medium C (days 4–5), and Medium D (days 6–29). On day 29, MPs at passage 0 (P0) were
passaged into P1 using TrypLE and collagenase (STEMCELL Technologies), then seeded at 1.92 x 105 cells per well in
Matrigel-coated 6-well plates and cultured in DMEM (1000 mg/L D-Glucose; STEMCELL Technologies) supplemented with
10% MyoCult™-SF Expansion 10X Supplement (STEMCELL Technologies). Y-27632 (10 µM; ROCK inhibitor, STEMCELL
Technologies) was included only in the seeding medium immediately after passaging and was omitted from all subsequent
medium changes. MPs at P1 were subsequently passaged to P2 using Accutase (Gibco), seeded at 9.6 × 104 cells per well
in Matrigel-coated 6-well plates, and cultured in DMEM (1000 mg/L D-Glucose) supplemented with 10% MyoCult™-SF
Expansion 10X Supplement. Cells were cryopreserved in CryoStor CS10 (STEMCELL Technologies) at P2 and stored in
liquid nitrogen for downstream applications. Immunocytochemistry was performed on P2 MPs to characterise the
progenitor markers MyoD and MyoG (as previously described).
RNA sequencing
Total RNA from iPSCs at passage 10–12 and MPs at passage 2 were harvested using TRIzol (Life Technologies) with
Qiagen RNeasy column purification (Qiagen), following the manufacturers’ instructions. RNA-seq libraries were prepared
using the Illumina Total RNA with Ribo-Zero Plus Library Prep Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The
sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads with a depth of
100 million reads per sample for whole transcriptome analysis.
Differential gene expression analysis
Raw sequencing data in FASTQ format were processed using the Galaxy Docker Image locally [22]. Quality control and
adapter trimming of raw reads were performed using fastp [23]. Filtered reads were aligned to the human reference
Page 6/27

genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 [24] in RF-stranded mode. Gene-level quantification was performed using featureCounts
[25]. Gene expression counts were compiled into an m × n matrix, where m represents genes and n represents samples (n 
= 13), for downstream differential expression analysis. Normalisation was performed using the median-of-ratios method
implemented in the DESeq2 package in R [26]. Genes with low expression levels-defined as < 0.5 count per million (CPM) in
more than 30% of samples-were excluded. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2, with significance
determined by an adjusted p-value (false discovery rate, FDR) < 0.05, using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple
testing. To account for unmeasured confounding variables, surrogate variable analysis was conducted using the sva
package in R [27], applying the model formula: “~ Diagnosis + Sample Type”. Biological function information of differentially
expressed genes was extracted from GeneCards® - The Human Gene Database (www.genecards.org). Raw sequencing
data in FASTQ format were processed using the Galaxy Docker Image locally [22].
Pathway enrichment analysis
To identify biologically relevant pathways enriched among the differentially expressed genes, gene set enrichment analysis
(GSEA) was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R. KEGG pathway annotations were used, and pathways with an
adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Gene Ontology (GO) annotations were also used to analyse
the top pathways and their distribution across different biological categories. Visualisation of enriched pathways was
conducted using the Cytoscape platform [28]. Nodes represented individual pathways, and a q-value threshold of 0.05 was
applied to highlight the most significantly enriched pathways.
In silico identification of drug repurposing candidates
In silico drug repurposing was conducted using a subset of the top 100 up- and down-regulated genes based on false
discovery rate (FDR). These genes were queried against the Library of Integrated Network-based Cellular Signatures
(LINCS2) database using the signatureSearch [29] package in R. Compounds were ranked based on the normalised
connectivity score (NCS), with lower NCS values indicating stronger inverse associations with the gene expression
signature, suggesting potential to reverse disease-associated gene expression patterns. Compounds with a weighted
connectivity score (WTCS) FDR < 0.05 were further investigated.
To prioritise drug candidates for repurposing, a literature review was performed on the top-ranking compounds. Exclusion
criteria included investigational drugs, agents with black box warnings, veterinary medications, topical treatments, and
cytotoxic chemotherapy agents. This filtering ensured that shortlisted drugs had the highest translational potential for safe
repurposing in the targeted disease context.
Statistical analysis
Differential gene expression, pathway enrichment and drug in silico identification analyses were described above. qPCR
results were analysed using the ΔΔCt method and normalised to cDNA concentration, then categorised into ME/CFS and
control groups. Participants’ clinical parameters, gene expression levels, and proliferation assay results were recorded and
grouped accordingly. Multiple t-test was used to assess data normality and Mann-Whitney u-test was applied to non-
normally distributed data statistical comparisons. These analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1.
Results
Clinical and demographic information and statistical analysis of participant information are in Table 1. Thirteen female
participants were included (ME/CFS: n = 6; healthy controls: n = 7). Mean age did not differ between groups (ME/CFS 48.2 ± 
16.2 years; controls 45.5 ± 8.4 years; p = 0.58); age ranges were 29–65 and 35–57 years, respectively. Patients with
ME/CFS had a mean Karnofsky Performance Score of 58.0 ± 11.0 and a MFI of 81.2 ± 5.1 (control values not collected).
Hand grip strength was lower in the ME/CFS group (23.5 ± 10.4) compared with controls (32.6 ± 7.6), with a significant
between-group difference (p = 0.001).

iPSCs and MPs characterization
PBMCs were isolated and reprogrammed into iPSCs using episomal vectors expressing Oct4, Sox2, Lin28, Klf4, and L-Myc.
All 13 lines exhibited typical iPSC morphology, forming tightly packed colonies with a high nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio and
prominent nucleoli (representative lines shown in Fig. 2A; images of all lines in Supplementary Fig. 1). Pluripotency was
confirmed by immunofluorescence staining for TRA-1-60 and OCT4 (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. 1), and by flow cytometry
for SSEA4, EPCAM, and CD9 (Fig. 2D; Supplementary File 1). Trilineage differentiation potential was demonstrated
following five days of directed differentiation, with immunofluorescence detection of ectoderm (PAX6, Nestin), endoderm
(AFP, SOX17), and mesoderm (NCAM, Brachyury T) markers (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Fig. 2), supported by qPCR analysis
(Fig. 2G).
All lines tested negative for mycoplasma by luminescence assay (Supplementary Table 1) and showed normal molecular
karyotypes with genotypes identical to their parental PBMCs, as determined by SNP array (Supplementary File 2). No
significant differences were observed between ME/CFS and control iPSCs in key pluripotency gene expression or
proliferation capacity (Fig. 2E, 2F, 2G). Finally, upon myogenic differentiation, immunocytochemistry revealed expression of
MyoD and MyoG, confirming specification toward the myogenic progenitor lineage (representative lines shown in Fig. 2C;
full dataset in Supplementary Fig. 3).
RNA-Seq Data and differentially expressed genes
RNA sequencing was performed on 13 myogenic progenitor (MP) cell lines derived from iPSCs that passed quality control
assessment. Principal component (PC) analysis showed that first two PCs captured 60% of the total variance in gene
expression across the dataset, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 44% and 16% respectively (Supplementary File 3). Following
normalisation, differential expression analysis identified 1,323 genes differentially expressed between ME/CFS and healthy
controls at nominal p < 0.05 (Supplementary Table 3). After the false discovery rate (FDR) correction (adjusted p < 0.05), 7
genes remained significantly differentially expressed (Table 2). Detailed information on the known biological functions of
these genes is presented in Table 2. Five genes (MIR205HG, FEZF1, SLC1A2, SYT13, and GALNT4) were selected for
validation by qPCR. Of these, only HLA-DMB and SYT13 were confirmed as significantly differentially expressed, with HLA-
DMB downregulated (p < 0.001) and SYT13 upregulated (p = 0.004) in ME/CFS compared with healthy controls (Table 2).

Pathway Enrichment Analysis
KEGG annotation analysis was employed to examine differential pathways in RNA-seq data from iPSC-MPs from both
ME/CFS cases and healthy controls, aiming to identify distinct biological pathway signatures. A complete list of 358
enriched pathways is provided in Supplementary Table 4. Among these, 73 differentially expressed pathways with q-values 
< 0.05 were identified and are presented in Fig. 3A, the majority of which were downregulated (96%). Only the “autophagy”
and “valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation” pathways were upregulated (Fig. 3A). The top 20 pathways, revealed by
both KEGG and GO analyses (Figs. 3B and 3C), showed substantial overlap, including two pathways associated with
skeletal muscle structure: “cytoskeleton in muscle cells” and “regulation of actin cytoskeleton”. Most of the downregulated
pathways were related to cell cycle and DNA maintenance, while a notable subset was associated with immune and
inflammatory responses, and cell adhesion and cytoskeleton (Fig. 3D).
Drug repurposing candidates for treatment of ME/CFS
A subset of the top 100 upregulated and downregulated DEGs were queried against the LINCS2 database to identify
potential drug candidates for repurposing. A complete list of identified compounds with an FDR < 0.05 was retained for
further analysis (Supplementary Table 5). The top 100 compounds were then reviewed in detail, and drugs were selected
based on a literature review, applying the pre-defined exclusion criteria. The final list of shortlisted repurposing candidates
is presented in Table 3. There are 22 drugs that met the inclusion criteria and are currently approved for use in humans.
Supporting evidence from the literature for each selected candidate is summarised in Supplementary Table 6.

Discussion
While numerous clinical trials have been conducted involving pharmacological compounds, nutraceuticals, and exercise
therapy, no effective treatment has been approved for ME/CFS, primarily due to the limited understanding of its
pathophysiology. Using iPSC-MPs, transcriptomic differences in cells derived from ME/CFS cases and healthy controls
were identified in this study. Mapping the data using KEGG annotation revealed 73 significantly altered biological pathways.
Subsequent analysis using the LINCS2 database identified 22 drugs with potential for repurposing to treat ME/CFS.
Differentially expressed genes between ME/CFS patients and healthy
controls
This is the first study to identify transcriptomic signatures of ME/CFS using iPSC-MPs, with seven genes reaching
significance based on an adjusted p-value < 0.05. Among these genes, some have previously been linked to ME/CFS in the
literature. For example, MIR205HG is a non-long coding gene that regulates the prolactin expression, primarily through its
modulatory effects on transcriptional and post-transcriptional signalling pathways [30]. Given the documented
neuroendocrine dysregulation in ME/CFS, this finding adds a novel molecular link between altered gene expression and
disrupted hormonal responses in affected individuals. Previous studies have reported heightened prolactin responses to
serotonergic and dopaminergic challenge tests in ME/CFS patients, despite baseline prolactin levels being typically normal.
Page 12/27

For instance, increased prolactin secretion was observed following d-fenfluramine and buspirone administration,
suggesting hypersensitivity of serotonergic signalling pathways [31–33]. An exaggerated prolactin response to sulpiride is
however described in depression, indexing possible dopamine supersensitivity [34]. The increase of prolactin hormonal
responses have been proposed as potential biomarkers distinguishing ME/CFS from other fatigue-related conditions and
may be associated with impaired hypothalamic-pituitary axis regulation [33]. It is possible that reduced MIR205HG
expression diminishes its regulatory control over prolactin synthesis or release, contributing to the heightened sensitivity of
the prolactin axis seen in ME/CFS. Furthermore, the sex-specific nature of prolactin regulation, particularly its stronger
association in female patients [35] and the present study including only female samples, warrants additional investigation
into whether MIR205HG downregulation exhibits sex-biased expression patterns in ME/CFS cohorts.
HLA-DMB (Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC), Class II, DM Beta) was also identified and confirmed by qPCR, a gene
that facilitates loading of antigenic peptides onto MHC class II molecules in antigen-presenting cells, particularly in
dendritic cells and B cells, which play important roles in antigen processing and presentation [36]. Impaired expression of
this gene can disrupt immune surveillance and contribute to immune dysregulation, leading to reduced efficiency in antigen
recognition and an inadequate immune response [36, 37]. Several abnormalities in B cells have been reported in ME/CFS,
including altered B cell phenotypes, reduced memory B cell numbers, and increased plasma blast differentiation [38–40].
While a recent transcriptomic study of PBMCs from ME/CFS patients reported upregulation of transcription factors
associated with CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell activation and inflammatory responses [41, 42], our data from iPSC-MPs identified a
significant downregulation of HLA-DMB. Its reduced expression may impair local antigen presentation in skeletal muscle
cells, thereby contributing to dysregulated CD4⁺ T cell activation or tolerance. Although direct evidence linking HLA-DMB to
ME/CFS in the literature is limited, previous studies have reported associations between ME/CFS and other HLA class II
alleles, including HLA-DQA101* and HLA-DQB103:03* [43]. Additionally, other HLA genes, particularly class II alleles, have
also been implicated in post-infectious ME/CFS pathogenesis and altered responses to Epstein–Barr virus [44], a known
environmental trigger of the disease. Our findings suggest that antigen presentation defects-specifically involving HLA-DMB
may extend beyond traditional immune cells, potentially contributing to aberrant T cell responses through impaired B cell-T
cell crosstalk in muscle-resident progenitor compartments. This highlights the importance of examining non-
haematopoietic cells in ME/CFS pathophysiology and supports a broader model of immune dysregulation involving both
peripheral and tissue-specific components, warranting further investigation into its role in ME/CFS.
SYT13 is a member of the synaptotagmin family, and plays a role in vesicle trafficking, membrane repair, calcium-
dependent exocytosis [45], and glucose transport to cells [46]. Its upregulation in genome-wide transcriptomics and qPCR
observed in the present study could imply changes in intracellular calcium signalling [47] and intracellular glucose levels
[46], which are critical in muscle contraction and mitochondrial energy regulation. Upregulation of SYT13 may represent a
compensatory response to cellular stress, such as impaired mitochondrial function or calcium dysregulation, both of which
have been widely reported in ME/CFS and reviewed in recent publications [6, 48]. Additionally, SYT13 has been implicated in
regulating vesicle trafficking pathways that could influence intracellular glucose handling, including trafficking of glucose
transporters (such as GLUTs) or metabolic enzymes to the cell membrane [2, 6]. Evidence from β-cell models has shown
that reduced SYT13 expression impairs insulin secretion by disrupting granule trafficking, highlighting its importance in
maintaining secretory and metabolic function [6]. Although skeletal muscle is not a classical secretory tissue, upregulation
of SYT13 may reflect an adaptive mechanism to preserve intracellular glucose uptake or utilisation under energy-restricted
conditions. Notably, mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired glycolytic flux have been observed in ME/CFS skeletal muscle
and immune cells, consistent with cellular stress responses that might drive SYT13 expression [3, 7, 8].
Significantly enriched pathways
KEGG and GO pathway analysis revealed most significant differential pathways were downregulated in ME/CFS compared
with healthy controls. These pathways were mainly linked to cell cycle and DNA repair pathways, immune-related pathways,
and pathways related to muscle function, cytoskeleton in muscle cells and regulation of the actin cytoskeleton pathways.
Cell cycle and DNA repair pathways
Page 13/27

The observed downregulation of cell cycle and DNA repair pathways including p53 signalling, mismatch repair, and base
excision repair in ME/CFS-derived MPs suggests a broad impairment in their proliferative and regenerative potential. These
pathways are essential for maintaining genomic integrity, facilitating proper mitotic progression, and executing damage
response mechanisms during cellular stress [49–52]. Suppression of the p53 signalling pathway, in particular, may reflect
an imbalance between cell cycle arrest, apoptosis and DNA repair activation as cells respond to stress which can drive
accumulated DNA damage and cellular senescence [49, 53]. Mondarano et al. (2020) reported significant telomere
shortening in ME/CFS patients, especially in CD8 + T cells and NK cells, suggesting replicative exhaustion and reduced
regenerative potential of immune cells [54]. Telomere attrition, which activates DNA damage responses and p53 signalling,
may persist due to inefficient resolution of genotoxic stress. Additionally, mitochondrial dysfunction, widely reported in
ME/CFS [6, 7, 48], may further exacerbate genomic instability. Reactive oxygen species generated by dysfunctional
mitochondria can induce oxidative DNA damage, necessitating robust repair via base excision and mismatch repair
pathways [9]. Impairment of these systems in MPs could therefore reflect both an intrinsic vulnerability and a secondary
consequence of chronic metabolic stress.
Immune-related pathways
Evidence of immune dysfunction in ME/CFS is well documented, with numerous studies reporting the impairments of
genes/pathways related to the immune response in affected individuals [38–42, 44, 55, 56]. Our KEGG-based
transcriptomic analysis of iPSC-derived muscle progenitors (MPs) revealed broad enrichment in immune-related pathways,
with 26 out of 73 significantly altered pathways associated with immune function. In contrast to transcriptomic studies
using peripheral immune cells, which report widespread upregulation of immune and viral response pathways in ME/CFS
patients [38, 40, 41], our analysis showed broad downregulation of these pathways in ME/CFS-derived MPs.
This discrepancy may reflect tissue-specific immune regulation. In peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), chronic
activation of monocytes and T cells as evidenced from sustained expression of inflammatory mediators, has been linked to
immune cell exhaustion, characterised by diminished responsiveness and altered transcriptional profiles [38, 40, 41, 55].
This exhausted state may impair these immune cells' ability to coordinate systemic immune responses, including
communication via cytokines and chemokines with peripheral tissues such as muscle. Our study observed a
downregulation of multiple key immune signalling pathways, such as cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, TNF signalling,
IL-17 signalling, JAK-STAT signalling, and T-cell receptor signalling. These pathways play essential roles in mediating
immune communication, inflammation, and cellular responses to infection or injury [57, 58]. Their suppression suggests a
diminished capacity of muscle progenitor cells to respond to systemic immune cues or inflammatory signals. In particular,
the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway integrates upstream signalling from inflammatory mediators [59], while
the JAK-STAT and T-cell receptor pathways are critical for downstream gene transcription and T cell activation [57]. The
TNF and IL-17 signalling cascades further coordinate inflammatory and immune cell recruitment processes [58, 60].
Therefore, the broad suppression of these signalling modules in MPs may indicate impaired immune responsiveness within
skeletal muscle, which may limit regenerative capacity and underlie the impaired recovery and post-exertional symptom
exacerbation characteristic of ME/CFS. In addition to canonical immune signalling suppression, our KEGG analysis also
revealed downregulation of several viral-related immune pathways, including those associated with Epstein-Barr virus
infection, cytomegalovirus infection, Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpesvirus infection, and viral protein-cytokine
interaction. These pathways typically reflect the host cell’s capacity to detect and respond to latent or active viral infections
through cytokine signalling, interferon responses, and antigen processing [61]. Their downregulation in ME/CFS-derived
MPs may reflect impaired viral recognition and clearance in muscle tissue, contributing to persistent immune
dysregulation. Taken together, while circulating immune cells in ME/CFS patients may exhibit chronic activation, MPs
display a distinct pattern of blunted immune responsiveness. This cell-type specific immune modulation supports a model
in which ME/CFS pathophysiology involves differential immune states across tissues, with systemic immune activation
coexisting alongside local immune suppression or signalling failure in affected peripheral tissues such as skeletal muscle.

Cytoskeleton pathways
There are several differentially expressed pathways related to muscle function such as cytoskeleton in muscle cells and
regulation of the actin cytoskeleton pathways. The cytoskeleton in muscle cells pathway involves a network of structural
and regulatory proteins essential for maintaining muscle cell integrity, facilitating contraction, and mediating
mechanotransduction. Key components include actin and myosin filaments, sarcomeric structures, costameres,
desmosomes, intermediate filaments such as desmin, and the LINC complex, which links the nucleus to the cytoskeleton.
These components work together to preserve muscle cellular structure and transmit mechanical force during muscle
contraction [62, 63]. Disruptions in this pathway have been linked to various skeletal muscle disorders, including Duchenne
muscular dystrophy [64], desminopathies [65], laminopathies [66], and congenital myopathies [67]. In the context of
ME/CFS, dysregulation of genes within this pathway may impair muscle structure and contractile function, contributing to
the hallmark symptoms of fatigue and muscle weakness [5, 6]. Our findings reveal that several genes involved in the
“Cytoskeleton in Muscle Cells” pathway were significantly downregulated in iPSC-derived MP cells from ME/CFS patients,
including COL9A1, ITGA11, ITGA2, PKP2, COL9A3, SDC1, DAAM2, DIAPH3, and FBN3. These genes play critical roles in
extracellular matrix organisation, cell adhesion, and actin cytoskeleton regulation, suggesting that their altered expression
may contribute to cytoskeletal instability and reduced mechanical efficiency in ME/CFS muscle cells.
The regulation of the actin cytoskeleton pathway plays a central role in maintaining cellular architecture, intracellular
trafficking, signal transduction, and contractile force generation, particularly in muscle cells. Actin filaments are dynamic
structures regulated by a range of signalling molecules such as Rho GTPases, kinases (e.g., ROCK, PAK), and actin-binding
proteins, which coordinate polymerisation and depolymerisation processes to adapt to mechanical and metabolic
demands [68]. In skeletal muscle, correct organisation and regulation of actin filaments is essential for sarcomere
structure, muscle contraction, and repair following stress or injury [62]. Disruption of this pathway can lead to altered
muscle fibre structure, impaired contractility, and abnormal cell signalling. These abnormalities may contribute to muscle
fatigue and reduced physical endurance, which are hallmark symptoms of ME/CFS. Previous studies have suggested that
ME/CFS is associated with impaired energy metabolism and muscle dysfunction, potentially linked to abnormalities in
cytoskeletal regulation [55, 69]. Altered expression of genes involved in actin cytoskeleton regulation may therefore provide
insight into the cellular mechanisms underlying muscle symptoms in ME/CFS and serve as potential targets for therapeutic
intervention. Findings from this study show that the regulation of genes involved in the actin cytoskeleton pathway is
downregulated in iPSC-derived MP cells from ME/CFS patients, highlighting the need for further investigation to evaluate
their potential as disease biomarkers or therapeutic targets.
Upregulation of amino acids degradation pathway
The upregulation of the valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation pathway in ME/CFS-derived MPs suggests a metabolic
shift toward increased reliance on branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) for energy production. BCAAs are primarily
metabolised in peripheral tissues such as skeletal muscle and serve as key alternative fuel sources, feeding intermediates
like acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, especially under conditions of glucose deprivation
or mitochondrial stress [70, 71]. This upregulation may reflect a compensatory adaptation to the energetic challenges
frequently reported in ME/CFS, including mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced glycolytic flux, and impaired fatty acid
oxidation [72, 73]. Several metabolomic and transcriptomic studies in ME/CFS cohorts have reported altered BCAA levels,
with some suggesting increased BCAA catabolism to support ATP production in the face of systemic energy deficits [74,
75]. Increased degradation of BCAAs may also attempt to counteract the accumulation of toxic intermediates or oxidative
stress. For example, while moderate BCAA breakdown replenishes TCA intermediates, overactivation may lead to excess
production of branched-chain keto acids (BCKAs) and reactive species, further exacerbating mitochondrial strain [74, 75].
Thus, while the transcriptional upregulation of BCAA degradation may reflect a metabolically adaptive response, it may also
signal a state of metabolic inflexibility in ME/CFS muscle progenitors. Furthermore, BCAA catabolism intersects with
important metabolic signalling pathways such as mTOR, AMPK, and PPARα, which regulate autophagy, protein synthesis,
Page 15/27

and mitochondrial biogenesis [76, 77]. Increased flux through BCAA degradation could therefore influence cell growth,
differentiation, and stress responses in MPs. However, the long-term effects of sustained BCAA catabolism are not
necessarily beneficial, particularly if it reflects chronic compensation for deficits in primary metabolic pathways.
Interestingly, the upregulation of this pathway in MPs aligns with broader hypotheses in ME/CFS that propose muscle and
immune cells are forced to shift to less efficient energy production pathways due to underlying mitochondrial impairments
[41, 78–80]. In this context, increased BCAA degradation may serve to buffer TCA cycle activity, sustain ATP production,
and mitigate energetic collapse. These results add to a growing body of evidence that ME/CFS involves not only immune
and mitochondrial dysfunction, but also complex metabolic reprogramming. Whether this shift represents a protective
adaptation or a contributor to metabolic exhaustion remains to be clarified, but it clearly underscores the bioenergetic
strain faced by MPs in ME/CFS.
Potential drug candidates for repurposing ME/CFS
Using transcriptomic profiles derived from ME/CFS MPs and LINCS2-based reverse gene signature screening, we identified
a shortlist of candidate drugs with potential to reverse ME/CFS-associated gene expression patterns. Following literature-
based screening, 22 compounds are proposed for further investigation, representing diverse biological mechanisms
relevant to ME/CFS pathology. A notable subset of these drugs includes antiviral or antimicrobial agents, such as acyclovir,
zidovudine, erythromycin, and disulfiram. Their identification is consistent with extensive literature implicating persistent or
latent viral infections, particularly Epstein-Barr virus and human herpesvirus 6, in ME/CFS onset and progression [2, 6, 39,
41–43, 55]. Disulfiram, an inhibitor of viral proteases, for instance, has shown activity against intracellular pathogens and
has recently gained attention for its potential to reactivate and clear latent viruses such as HIV [81], and reduce SARS-CoV-
2 infection [82]. These drugs may support viral clearance and help resolve chronic immune activation observed in ME/CFS
patients.
Several candidates also fall under the category of immunomodulators, including leflunomide, sirolimus, everolimus,
clofibric acid, and fenofibrate. These agents modulate key immune pathways implicated in ME/CFS, such as T-cell
activation, cytokine regulation, and mTOR signalling [83–86]. Both sirolimus and everolimus inhibit mTORC1 [87], a
metabolic and immune checkpoint regulator that has been associated with immune exhaustion and metabolic
reprogramming in T cells [88]. The identification of PPAR-α agonists (e.g. fenofibrate, clofibric acid) is also relevant, as they
modulate inflammation, lipid metabolism, and regulate mitochondrial function [89], all of which have been implicated in the
pathophysiology of ME/CFS [83–86].
Drugs with neuroprotective or neurotransmitter-modulating effects also featured prominently including tacrine, donepezil,
and tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4). These drugs may impact cognitive function and address neurotransmitter dysregulation [90,
91], which correspond with observed symptoms in ME/CFS such as cognitive fog and central fatigue. BH4, a cofactor for
monoamine synthesis, could help restore serotonergic and dopaminergic tone [91], both implicated in ME/CFS
pathophysiology and hypothalamic-pituitary axis dysfunction. Additionally, melatonin, a serotonin receptor agonist may
have multifaceted effects by regulating circadian rhythm, sleep patterns, neuroprotective effects, mitochondrial oxidative
stress, and immune responses [92, 93]. Notably, clinical trials reported that ME/CFS patients showed improvements in
fatigue, motivation, concentration and activity after treatment with 1-5mg of melatonin for three months [92, 94]. Thus,
these selected neuroprotective drugs have potential for ME/CFS repurposing; however, further studies are needed to
holistically evaluate their treatment doses, times and ME/CFS subtype efficacy.
Drugs targeting autonomic dysfunction and vascular dysregulation, including midodrine, naftidrofuryl, perindopril, and
nitrendipine, may alleviate orthostatic intolerance and improve cerebral perfusion, common symptoms in ME/CFS.
Midodrine for instance, which is an anti-hypotensive compound that increases blood pressure by activating the alpha 1-
receptor, has already been used to treat postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), which frequently overlaps with
ME/CFS [95]. In a case study, Naschitz et al. (2004) reported that ME/CFS patients with abnormal haemodynamic instability
Page 16/27

score treated with midodrine resulted in correction of the dysautonomia followed by improvement of fatigue [96]. They
suggested that midodrine might be used for ME/CFS patients with autonomic dysfunction; however, there are no further
placebo-controlled trials on the efficacy of midodrine on ME/CFS since.
The short list of potential drugs also includes zolmitriptan, nalbuphine, and noscapine. These drugs may offer symptomatic
relief for headaches, pain, or the neuroinflammatory aspects of the disease. While these agents may not directly modify
disease mechanisms, they may contribute to multi-modal treatment strategies.
Limitations and future directions
Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was limited, and
although consistent transcriptomic differences were observed, larger cohorts may be required to validate the
generalisability of these findings across the heterogenous ME/CFS population. For example, all participants in this study
were female, limiting the applicability of our findings to male patients, who may exhibit distinct molecular signatures. While
iPSC-MPs provide a muscle-relevant platform that captures patient-specific molecular alterations, they do not fully
recapitulate the complex multisystemic features of ME/CFS, including immune-neuroendocrine interactions and post-
exertional symptom exacerbation. Further, in vitro and in vivo functional validation of identified compounds remains
necessary to confirm therapeutic potential and disease relevance. Future studies should aim to incorporate multi-tissue
iPSC models, longitudinal sampling, and single cell analyses to better capture disease dynamics and cellular heterogeneity.
Integration with proteomic, metabolomic, and clinical phenotyping datasets may further advance biomarker discovery and
precision medicine approaches for ME/CFS.
We present the first transcriptomic characterisation of patient-derived iPSC-MPs in ME/CFS, establishing a novel disease-
relevant in vitro model to investigate pathogenic mechanisms and therapeutic opportunities. The identified drugs span key
pathophysiological domains identified in ME/CFS - immune dysregulation, neuroinflammation, autonomic instability,
mitochondrial impairment, and viral persistence, underscoring the potential of patient-specific iPSC-MPs to serve as a
scalable and mechanistically informative platform for ME/CFS modelling and rational drug repurposing. The present study
adds to the growing utility of GES of diseases that are then used for screening drug candidates [97, 98] such as
candesartan and trimetazidine to treat bipolar depression, which are currently undergoing clinical trials. By leveraging a
model that captures disease-relevant molecular features and aligns with known pharmacological mechanisms, this
approach can address key barriers in ME/CFS research, including its elusive aetiology, lack of validated biomarkers, and the
current absence of effective therapeutics.
