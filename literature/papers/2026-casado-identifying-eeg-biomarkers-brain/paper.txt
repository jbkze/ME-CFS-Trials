A B S T R A C T
Background: Fatigue and brain fog are among the most reported symptoms in Long COVID (LC), yet their un­
derlying neural mechanisms remain unclear. In this context, we aimed to identify neural correlates associated 
with LC symptoms.
Methods: Electroencephalography (EEG) was used to quantify brain oscillatory activity during sustained hand­
grips and rest in the left central region overlying the primary hand motor (C3) and somatosensory areas (C5). A 
sample of twenty LC participants reporting fatigue, brain fog, and post-exertional malaise (PEM) was analyzed, 
along with 20 age- and sex-matched controls. All participants were right-handed, and EEG activity was recorded 
on the dominant left hemisphere over motor and somatosensory areas. The main LC symptoms were assessed 
using the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS), the Cognitive Failures Questionnaire (CFQ), and the DePaul Symptom 
Questionnaire for PEM (DSQ-PEM).
Results: LC participants showed a smaller post-movement beta rebound (PMBR) during handgrips in the left 
central region (electrode C3, p = 0.02) compared to controls, which negatively correlated with cognitive 
symptoms (r = −0.35, p = 0.02). They also showed a slower alpha central frequency (CF) at rest (electrode C5, p 
= 0.003), which correlated with fatigue (r = −0.264, p = 0.019).
Conclusions: Both the lower PMBR and slower alpha CF suggest impaired inhibitory control and reduced excit­
ability in the sensorimotor cortex of LC individuals. Together, these features, which were associated with fatigue 
and brain fog, highlight altered dynamics in the sensorimotor cortex of LC individuals. These findings offer 
preliminary mechanistic insight into LC symptoms and suggest that PMBR and alpha CF warrant further 
investigation as candidate EEG markers of this condition.
1. Introduction
Long COVID (LC) affects 10–20% of individuals following SARS-CoV- 
2 infection, characterized by symptoms that persist beyond 12 weeks 
[1,3,105], including fatigue, cognitive dysfunction (“brain fog”), and 
post-exertional malaise (PEM) [24,64,67,103]. PEM, a hallmark of LC, 
involves symptom exacerbation after minor exertion [39]. Fatigue, 
another core symptom, is characterized by persistent exhaustion that 
remains unrelieved by rest [83]. Cognitive dysfunction, reported by 
~85% of LC individuals, impacts memory, attention, working memory, 
decision-making, and executive functioning [27]. Despite its high 
prevalence and disabling nature, LC lacks objective diagnostic tools and 
biomarkers [105].
Neurological involvement is increasingly recognized in COVID-19.

SARS-CoV-2 presumably affects the central nervous system (CNS) 
through direct invasion across the blood–brain barrier (BBB) or via 
infected immune cells, with associated cytokine storms contributing to 
neuroinflammation and altered synaptic function [45,53,70,71,109]. 
Using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), a cortical grey matter loss of 
~2% was found post-SARS-CoV-2 infection, comparable to a decade of 
aging [32]. Reduced grey matter volume and white matter lesions 
observed post-infection were found to be associated with cognitive 
dysfunction, notably visuospatial impairment and global deficits across 
memory, language, and perceptual motor tasks [44,104]. For those with 
lingering symptoms evolving in the LC condition, thalamic volume loss 
and weakened thalamocortical connectivity with motor areas were re­
ported, features that were associated with the level of fatigue [42,62]. In 
the brains of LC individuals, increased alpha power, slower alpha fre­
quency, and elevated beta activity were also reported in region-specific 
areas using electroencephalography (EEG) at rest [21,60,74]. More 
specifically, reduced transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)-evoked 
beta activity was observed in motor-related regions, including the sup­
plementary motor area (SMA), which was associated with fatigue 
severity [20]. Increased motor thresholds and reduced motor-evoked 
potentials were also observed in LC using TMS, indicating impaired 
corticospinal excitability, a feature associated with fatigue [79]. These 
findings confirm the presence of structural and functional brain changes 
post-SARS-CoV-2 infection and in the presence of LC, including reduced 
brain volume, impaired excitability, and altered alpha and beta oscil­
latory patterns [20,21,60,79,104].
In this study, we investigated the sensorimotor cortex (SMC), given 
its central role in movement-related beta dynamics and its modulation 
by attentional and sensorimotor demands. Specifically, alpha and beta 
oscillations in SMC were shown to vary during the orienting of attention 
to upcoming tactile stimuli, with spatially specific suppression of these 
rhythms linked to improved behavioral performance [98]. Additionally, 
beta-band activity in sensorimotor regions was shown to be modulated 
by attention and reflects higher-order processing of task-relevant sen­
sory input, rather than motor execution alone [7]. In the SMC, beta 
power decreases during movement, a phenomenon known as motor- 
related beta desynchronization (MRBD) and rebounds afterward, 
referred to as post-movement beta rebound (PMBR) [19,82]. In older 
adults, MRBDs are enhanced, with greater values associated with 
degraded motor performance [107]. On the other hand, PMBR is 
reduced in elderly individuals, a phenomenon thought to reflect altered 
motor cortical inhibition and excitability [6,107]. As the brain seems to 
undergo accelerated age-related decline following SARS-CoV-2 infection 
[32,42], LC individuals may exhibit functional changes similar to those 
observed in older individuals.
We also examined alpha activity at rest, given its central role in 
regulating cortical excitability and supporting cognitive functions 
[12,57,58], as well as its reported changes in individuals with LC [21]. 
To capture these dynamics more precisely, resting EEG signals were 
decomposed into their periodic (oscillatory) and aperiodic (1/f back­
ground) components [31,96]. This approach was motivated by evidence 
suggesting that the aperiodic component is altered across neurodegen­
erative and psychiatric conditions [11,81,85,86], with some of these 
main features being linked to disease presence, stage and severity [81].
Reduced excitability in the SMC is a known characteristic of limbic- 
cortical dysfunction [20,79]. Central fatigue is believed to stem from 
diminished voluntary motor drive, which is associated with decreased 
excitability in motor-related cortical regions and impaired corticospinal 
transmission [23,34,54]. This implies that increasing cognitive–motor 
demands could further disrupt the brain’s oscillatory activity in those 
affected by LC. To investigate this, participants learned a novel motor 
task, allowing the examination of whether post-task EEG dynamics 
would show greater deviations from those observed in healthy controls 
(HC).
Finally, whether these EEG features were associated with reported 
fatigue and cognitive impairment was examined, two primary symptoms 
among LC individuals [46]. By linking resting and movement-related 
EEG features to fatigue and brain fog, this study sought to advance 
objective biomarkers for LC and estimate how altered cortical dynamics 
relate to its disabling symptoms.
Methods
1.1. Participants
This study, conducted at McGill University (May 2023–Sept. 2024), 
included LC participants who met the following inclusion criteria: (a) 
presence of fatigue, PEM, and brain fog, assessed using the Fatigue 
Severity Scale (FSS, score ≥36) [89]), the DePaul Symptom Question­
naire for PEM (DSQ-PEM, frequency and severity score of 2 on items 
1–5) [25], and Cognitive Failures Questionnaire (CFQ) [16]; (b) symp­
toms persisting for at least three months following a confirmed COVID- 
19 infection, verified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or antigen 
testing; (c) age between 18 and 59 years to reduce the influence of age- 
related brain changes; and (d) right-handedness, confirmed by the 
Edinburgh inventory of handedness [78]. Exclusion criteria included: 
(a) a history of neurological or psychiatric conditions, head injury, or 
epilepsy; (b) use of medications that potentially influence EEG activity, 
including CNS stimulants as well as anticonvulsants; and (c) a diagnosis 
of other conditions involving fatigue symptomatology. To ensure a clear 
comparison, HC were required to have no history of COVID-19 infection 
within the previous six months and no symptoms consistent with the 
WHO definition of LC [105].
The sample size was determined using an a priori power analysis 
conducted before recruitment to estimate the number of participants 
needed to detect baseline between-group differences in MRBDs (inde­
pendent-samples t-test). Based on prior work [107], the expected effect 
size was large (d ≈1.12; SDs 8.4 and 10.4). With a one-tailed α of 0.05 
and 80% power, this corresponded to approximately 10–12 participants 
per group. A conservative minimum of 14 participants per group was 
therefore set.
Twenty-one participants were recruited from the Facebook page 
Covid Longue Qu´ebec Long-Haulers Support Group (n = 2.9k) and 
emailed through the Quebec Action for Post-COVID Project. Twenty-one 
HC were matched to the LC participants by age ( ± 3 years) and sex. 
Two participants (one from each group) were excluded due to excessive 
EEG noise, yielding a final analyzed sample of 20 LC and 20 control 
participants. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Office 
(IRB) of McGill University on March 27, 2023 (A02-M82-22B). All 
participants read and electronically signed the consent form via 
REDCap. Compensation was provided for transportation and 
participation.
1.2. Experimental paradigm
This was an exploratory cross-sectional study with matched pairs 
that included a repeated-measures component. The study consisted of 
two phases (Fig. 1). The first one involved an online assessment where 
participants completed eligibility and self-reported symptom question­
naires via REDCap platform. All materials were available in English and 
French. The second one involved an in-person laboratory session. At 
least a week after completing the online phase, EEG data were collected 
during rest and a handgrip task, both pre- and post-motor learning task 
(ML task). The ML task was used to increase the cognitive load. This task 
has been previously used in neurorehabilitation and skill learning 
studies, where brief, visually guided force tracking reliably engages 
neural systems without high exertion demands [26,75]. The second 
session lasted ~3 hours.

1.3.1. Symptom questionnaires and behavioral assessment
In addition to the ones used for eligibility, questionnaires were used

to explore quality of life [41], daytime sleepiness [52], sleep quality 
[18], symptoms of anxiety [93] and depression [61], and comorbidities 
[87] (Fig.1). The Purdue Pegboard Test was used to assess hand and 
finger coordination and dexterity [65]. Separate scores were calculated 
for Left + Right + Both (L + R + B), as well as for the assembly task. 
Handgrip strength was used to determine the maximal voluntary 
contraction [14].
1.3.2. EEG data acquisition and pre-processing
EEG signals were recorded using a 64-channel high-density EEG (HD- 
EEG) system (Brain Products, Gilching, Germany), following the 10–20 
electrode layout [56]. Signals were amplified and sampled at 2500 Hz. 
All electrodes were referenced to FCz, with AFz as ground. Electrode 
impedances were kept below 20 kΩ. Data were collected at rest for five 
minutes with eyes open, fixating on a cross in the center of the screen, 
followed by five minutes with eyes closed. Only eyes-open recordings 
were used in this study, as this state minimizes fluctuations in arousal 
and maintains stable visual engagement, providing a suitable baseline 
for analysis [9,10]. For the motor task, participants completed 50 right- 
hand contractions (4 seconds at 15% maximal voluntary contraction) 
using a dynamometer (BIOPAC, Goleta, CA, USA) with real-time visual 
feedback, following procedures routinely applied in our laboratory [72]
(details provided in the suppl. materials). All participants were right- 
handed, confirmed via the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory [78], and 
the handgrips and ML task were performed with the right hand; C3 and 
C5 (contralateral, left-hemisphere electrodes) were therefore selected to 
capture task-relevant sensorimotor activity. Reaction time and rise time 
were extracted from the force signals. The ML task consisted of 100 trials 
of a force-matching task with real-time visual feedback and were per­
formed as described in [26] (see Supplementary Materials).
EEG signals were filtered (0.1–120 Hz bandpass, 60 Hz notch), 
resampled at 250 Hz, and re-referenced to electrode FCz. Noisy epochs 
and channels (e.g., muscle, head, and jaw movement artifacts) were 
removed by visual inspection. Independent component analysis cor­
rected eye movement artifacts (Brainstorm MATLAB toolbox) [95]. 
Frequency bands were set as: alpha (8–12 Hz) and beta (13–29 Hz).
Time-frequency representations were computed using Welch’s 
method [102] with 10-second no-overlap windows. Power spectral 
density (PSD) estimates were obtained for each epoch and averaged 
within the predefined frequency bands (alpha, beta). Band power was 
extracted as the mean PSD within each frequency range for each time 
window of interest.
Although EEG was recorded using a 64-channel montage to ensure 
full scalp coverage and data quality, analyses were restricted a priori to 
electrodes C3 and C5 to test region-specific hypotheses related to SMC 
function. The same electrodes were used for resting alpha analyses to 
maintain anatomical consistency across resting-state and movement- 
related measures within the contralateral sensorimotor region. This 
choice was also supported by evidence that sensorimotor alpha and beta 
rhythms are modulated by somatosensory attention and behavioral 
performance [36,98].
Relative power (RP%), which represents the power changes relative 
to the baseline, was computed from electrodes C3 and C5, located over 
the left SMC, opposite to the right hand performing the task [29,84,90]. 
Brain imaging studies of EEG electrode localization show that C3/C4 
consistently project onto the central sulcus region, spanning both pre­
central (M1) and postcentral (S1) gyri, despite individual variability in 
the precise gyrus [59,77,90]. We therefore use the SMC consistent with 
prior studies targeting this same region under the same terminology 
[72,108]. Time windows were defined as: Baseline (pre-movement): 1.0 
- 0.1 seconds preceding movement onset (see Figs. 2 & 3). MRBD (active 
contraction): 0.5 - 3.5 seconds post-movement onset. PMBR (rebound 
phase) 5 - 8 seconds post-movement onset. These windows were selected 
based on established temporal profiles reported in prior work [82,107], 
with visual inspection used as a confirmatory step to verify alignment 
with the observed signal patterns. RP% was calculated using the 
following formula, where P(t) is the absolute power at time t, and B is 
the baseline power:

1.4. Data analysis
1.4.1. Symptoms, cognitive and behavioral data analysis
Group comparisons were conducted using independent-sample t- 
tests or the Mann-Whitney U Test for non-normal data. ML task per­
formance across blocks was analyzed using a linear mixed model (LMM) 
to assess group differences and changes over time.
1.4.2. EEG data analysis
Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Group com­
parisons of MRBD, PMBR, periodic and aperiodic components were 
conducted using independent-sample t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests 
for non-normally distributed data. LMM assessed changes from pre- to 
post-task.
Correlations between EEG features and clinical symptoms were 
calculated using Pearson or Kendall’s tau-b correlation. Bonferroni 
correction was applied to hypothesis-driven tests, and FDR correction 
was applied to exploratory analyses (reported in the Supplementary 
Material). Group and LC-only correlations were both reported to cap­
ture full and within-group associations.
2. Results
2.1. Self-reported clinical symptoms and behavioral results
Out of the 42 participants recruited, two (one from each group) were 
excluded due to excessive noise in their EEG recordings, leaving 20 
participants per group. For the LC group, the average time between the 
confirmed COVID-19 infection and the recruitment date was 24.0 ±
10.7 months. Pre-task EEG recordings were successfully obtained from 
all 20 participants in both groups. In the LC group, three participants 
had incomplete post-task recordings due to discomfort with the EEG cap 
and system malfunctions.

2.3. EEG power spectral during resting state
Differences between groups were found in the alpha CF at electrode 
C3 (p = 0.03), where the control group had a higher value (10.5 ± 0.83) 
than the LC group (9.8 ± 1.16), t(35) = −2.27. A similar trend was 
observed at electrode C5 (p = 0.005), with the HC group having a higher 
power (10.6 ± 0.6) than the LC group (9.6 ± 1.0), t(34) = −3.06 
(Fig. 3). However, after adjusting for multiple comparisons, only the 
results from electrode C5 remained significant.
No differences were found in the alpha PW on electrode C3 (t(35) =
0.21, p = 0.83) or C5 (t(34) = −0.43, p = 0.66) nor in the alpha BW on 
electrode C3 (U = 196.00, p = 0.45) or C5 (U = 145.00, p = 0.64).
No differences were found in the aperiodic components such as the 
exponent in electrode C5 (t(38) = −0.74, p = 0.46) or C3 (t(38) = 0.01, 
p = 0.98), nor in the offset in electrode C5 (U = 140.50, p = 0.11) or C3 (t 
(38) = -0.60, p = 0.54).
No differences were found in the effect of group x time (pre-task vs. 
post-task) for any of the periodic and aperiodic components at electrode 
C3 or C5. This is shown in the Supplementary Material.
2.4. Association between EEG components, questionnaires, and motor 
scores
A negative correlation was observed between CFQ scores and PMBR 
at electrode C3 (r = −0.35, p = 0.02) (Fig. 4A). No correlation was found 
between the PMBR at electrode C3 and the FSS questionnaire scores (r =
−0.13, p = 0.22) (Fig. 4B).
The alpha CF at electrode C5 was negatively correlated with both the 
CFQ (r = −.23, p = 0.04) (Fig. 4C) and the FSS scores (r = −.26, p =
0.01) (Fig. 4D), although only the correlation with FSS remained sig­
nificant after correction for multiple comparisons. Neither PMBR at C3 
nor alpha CF at C5 correlated significantly with time since infection (r =
−0.12, p = 0.62; r = −0.33, p = 0.17, respectively), suggesting that the 
observed group differences were not simply a function of variability in 
illness duration within the sample.
No other significant correlations were found in the additional 
exploratory analyses between motor performance and EEG components, 
whether analyzing the full sample or only the LC group (see Suppl. 
Materials).

3. Conclusions
To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess how EEG oscillatory 
patterns are modulated in the SMC of LC individuals during movement, 
as well as their periodic and aperiodic characteristics. LC participants 
showed reduced beta activity post-movement, which was associated 
with the severity of self-reported cognitive symptoms. During rest, they 
showed a lower alpha CF, which was linked to fatigue severity. These 
findings show that cortical oscillations are disrupted in LC symptoms, 
and they might contribute to the symptomatology observed in this 
population
3.1. Self-reported clinical symptoms and behavioral results
LC participants exhibited severe fatigue, brain fog, and PEM, 
consistent with previous studies [16,27,28,49,89,97,110]. They also 
reported increased symptoms of anxiety, symptoms of depression, poor 
sleep quality, daytime sleepiness, comorbidities, and reduced quality of 
life,

Motor performance was affected in the LC group, where poorer 
bimanual coordination and fine motor control were present [65]. No 
differences were found in the assembly task or grip strength, suggesting 
coordination may be more affected than strength or precision. Slower 
reaction times were observed during the handgrip task in LC partici­
pants, while rise time did not differ between groups. Reaction time is 
broadly recognized as a sensitive marker of neurobiological integrity 
and processing efficiency [40]. This pattern of delayed initiation with 
preserved motor output is consistent with prior reports of slower reac­
tion times in LC, observed across visual detection tasks and neuropsy­
chological assessments [22,69].
3.2. EEG-based MRBD & PMBR
PMBRs in the SMC were reduced in LC individuals. This feature is 
believed to reflect motor cortical inhibition following movement [55]
and to be mediated by GABAergic neurotransmission [33,37]. Reduced 
PMBR has been reported in Parkinson’s disease and multiple sclerosis, 
linked to impaired inhibitory control, diminished motor accuracy, and 
fatigue in these conditions [6,63,66]. Reduced beta activity post- 
movement in LC this suggest disrupted GABAergic function. This 
aligns with observations of reduced TMS-evoked beta responses in in­
dividuals with LC who experience high levels of fatigue, where higher 
fatigue correlated with reduced beta activity in the SMC [20].
Beta activity in the SMC is thought to arise from corticothalamic and 
cortico-basal ganglia loops [8,15]). In aging, reduced PMBR has been 
linked to impaired thalamocortical connectivity [47]. Comparable 
findings in LC, including structural disruptions in the thalamus and basal 
ganglia [19], and weakened thalamocortical coupling with M1 and SMA 
in fatigued individuals [20], suggest that altered thalamocortical cir­
cuits may underlie impaired regulation of motor cortical excitability.
3.3. EEG power spectral analysis
LC participants showed lower alpha band CF at rest over the SMC. 
Reduced alpha peak frequency has been reported in aging and several 
neurological conditions associated with cognitive impairment, although 
these findings have generally been derived from global, posterior, or 
non-sensorimotor electrode sets rather than specifically from C3/C5 
[21,35,111]. We prioritized anatomical consistency between our 
resting-state and movement-related measures, both drawn from C3/C5, 
since our primary aim was to link rest and movement-related activity 
within the same sensorimotor region. This choice limits direct compa­
rability with prior alpha CF findings. Alpha CF is thought to reflect the 
temporal organization of cortical processing, with slower frequencies 
associated with less efficient information integration and reduced

cognitive speed. Accordingly, a lower alpha CF may indicate a shift in 
the balance of excitation and inhibition, potentially disrupting neural 
communication and cognitive processing [50,88]. Cognitive efficiency 
can be affected by chronic systemic inflammation, which has been 
shown to alter synaptic function and large scale network dynamics, 
leading to disruptions in alpha oscillatory activity and reduced cortical 
network stability, a process also implicated in LC [50,101].
Alpha oscillations are thought to depend on thalamocortical circuits 
that regulate cortical activity [48]. In LC, both slower alpha CF and 
reduced PMBR findings point to disruptions in the thalamocortical and 
basal ganglia circuits. Evidence of structural abnormalities and weak­
ened thalamocortical coupling in fatigued LC individuals further sup­
ports this interpretation [42,62].
There were no differences in the periodic and aperiodic measures 
extracted from the alpha band (PW, BW, exponent, offset). Previous EEG 
studies in LC reported increased alpha power but did not separate pe­
riodic and aperiodic components, leaving it unclear whether the 
observed effects reflected genuine oscillatory changes or broadband 
power shifts [21,60,74]. By applying this method, our approach pro­
vides a more specific characterization, indicating that the alterations in 
LC are more likely related to synchronized oscillatory dynamics rather 
than global cortical excitability [31].
No differences in spectral components were observed post-task, 
suggesting that these features may be relatively resistant to short-term 
modulation by cognitive load. Alternatively, the small sample size and 
inter-individual variability may have reduced sensitivity to detect subtle 
changes.
3.4. Associations between EEG variables, self-reported symptoms and 
behavioral scores
A negative correlation was found between brain fog, measured 
through cognitive failures, and beta activity in the SMC after movement. 
This indicates that greater cognitive difficulties were associated with 
reduced PMBR. PMBR is thought to index inhibitory control and the 
resetting of motor networks [17,55], processes that depend on 
GABAergic tone [33,73]. In LC, a reduced PMBR may therefore reflect 
GABAergic dysfunction contributing to both brain fog and fatigue 
[20,79]. Given the SMC’s involvement in motor and cognitive functions, 
reduced PMBR could therefore reflect abnormalities in a shared neural 
substrate underlying these symptoms [51,100].
A lower alpha CF was associated with greater fatigue in our sample. 
This finding is consistent with prior post-COVID EEG work showing 
alpha frequency slowing, although the electrode locations and symptom 
domains differed from those examined here [21]. Therefore, these re­
sults are not directly comparable to the present C3/C5 EEG-based 
measure. Alpha band slowing is observed in mild cognitive impair­
ment, multiple sclerosis, burnout, and Alzheimer's disease, where it is 
associated with deficits in executive function, working memory, atten­
tion, and mental fatigue [5,50,99]. Reduced PMBR has also been re­
ported in Parkinson's disease [43], multiple sclerosis [2], amyotrophic 
lateral sclerosis [13], and major depressive disorder [106]. This suggests 
a transdiagnostic marker of impaired sensorimotor inhibition rather 
than an LC-specific one. A recent systematic review similarly found 
overlapping alpha- and beta-band abnormalities across fibromyalgia, 
chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), and LC [92], further indicating 
that these features likely reflect a shared response to chronic fatigue- 
related states. These findings suggest reduced alpha CF could serve as 
a neurophysiological marker for LC-related cognitive and fatigue 
symptoms. This interpretation is further in line with prior work linking 
disruptions in alpha rhythms to brain fog in LC [4,60].
Within the LC group alone, neither PMBR nor alpha CF correlated 
significantly with fatigue or cognitive symptoms (see Suppl. Table 1), 
suggesting that associations observed in the combined sample partly 
reflect between-group differences in symptom severity rather than a 
continuous symptom-EEG relationship within LC.
A trend-level association was observed between cognitive failures 
and alpha CF, suggesting that lower alpha frequencies may be associated 
with cognitive symptoms. Although not statistically significant, this 
aligns with previous reports [5,99] and warrants further investigation.
Exploratory correlations (see suppl. materials) revealed several 
emerging trends. In the full sample, MRBDs were associated with higher 
depression symptoms and cognitive failures, while smaller PMBRs were 
linked to worse sleep quality, increased cognitive failures, symptoms of 
depression, and increased comorbidities. In the LC group, MRBDs 
correlated positively with daytime sleepiness and negatively with the 
number of comorbidities, whereas PMBR was positively linked with 
sleep quality and comorbidities. Depression symptoms were negatively 
associated with alpha CF and positively associated with alpha PW, while 
alpha BW was positively correlated with anxiety symptoms. These 
findings provide direction for future hypothesis-driven studies with 
larger LC samples.
As an exploratory study, a primary limitation is the small sample 
size. This reduces sensitivity to detect subtle group differences or asso­
ciations between EEG markers and clinical symptoms. Given the sample 
size, the study was underpowered to detect small-to-moderate effects in 
secondary and exploratory analyses, and the absence of significant 
findings in these analyses should not be interpreted as evidence of no 
effect. For instance, LC participants performed below controls across 
learning blocks during the ML task. Although this difference was not 
statistically significant, the pattern may reflect subtle impairments in 
adaptive motor processes potentially influenced by fatigue and cognitive 
symptoms. Task-related effects should also be considered when inter­
preting PMBR, as even low-effort tasks may induce mental or physical 
fatigue in LC participants, making it difficult to disentangle task-induced 
strain from underlying condition-related fatigue. These consistent 
trends, together with the sample size limitation, suggest that subtle 
group effects may have been masked by variability and could become 
more evident in larger or longitudinal cohorts. Correlation analyses 
between EEG features and clinical symptoms should be interpreted with 
caution. Associations were examined across the full sample and may be 
driven by group differences in symptom severity between LC and con­
trols. Within-group analyses did not consistently reach statistical sig­
nificance, limiting conclusions about symptom–EEG relationships 
within LC. As this was a cross-sectional study, it cannot determine 
whether the observed EEG alterations change over the course of illness. 
Because analyses were a priori restricted to electrodes over the left SMC, 
our findings cannot be generalized to broader cortical dysfunction or 
other brain regions. Additionally, as all participants were right-handed, 
our findings are specific to the dominant left SMC and may not gener­
alize to left-handed or ambidextrous individuals. We also did not control 
for potential confounders that may impact both symptom presentation 
and brain activity, such as hormonal variation, age, and depressive 
symptoms. Furthermore, LC participants differed from controls in sleep 
quality, daytime sleepiness, anxiety, depression, quality of life, and co­
morbidity burden, none of which were statistically controlled for in the 
present analyses. In addition, participants varied considerably in time 
since infection and longitudinal studies are needed to determine 
whether cortical oscillatory changes evolve over the course of LC. As 
several of these variables are known to influence alpha and beta oscil­
latory activity, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting 
the LC phenotype broadly. PEM was monitored given its relevance as a 
hallmark symptom of LC; however, it was assessed using a binary clas­
sification (DSQ-PEM), limiting the ability to examine associations with 
EEG features. In addition, the study was not designed to provoke PEM, 
making it difficult to capture its relationship with neural activity or to 
distinguish it from general fatigue. Future work should extend this 
paradigm to test whether these EEG markers generalize across different 
patterns of hemispheric dominance.
In conclusion, this study provides evidence of altered neural oscil­
latory dynamics in LC, with a slower alpha frequency at rest and reduced 
beta rebound following movement. These findings highlight disrupted
