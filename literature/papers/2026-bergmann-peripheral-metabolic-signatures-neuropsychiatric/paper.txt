Abstract 
64 
Neuropsychiatric symptoms are considered the most common feature of long COVID disease. 
65 
Recent studies have demonstrated structural brain changes and highlighted the importance of 
66 
neuroinflammation in the development of cognitive deficits as seen in long COVID patients. In 
67 
addition, peripheral studies have demonstrated heterogeneous molecular subtypes of long 
68 
COVID pathology. However, it is unknown which peripheral metabolomic alterations occur in 
69 
patients with neuropsychiatric long COVID syndrome and how these relate to symptom 
70 
severity. In the present study, we investigated differences in the peripheral serum metabolome 
71 
profiles of healthy controls and patients with long COVID syndrome with neuropsychiatric 
72 
symptoms. We found that patients with long COVID showed peripheral alterations in lipid 
73 
species such as triacylglycerides and acylcarnitines. Furthermore, metabolites altered in 
74 
patients with long COVID syndrome were also associated with depressive and fatigue symptom 
75 
burden as well as with differences in cortical thickness in multiple brain regions. Our results 
76 
demonstrate a metabolic phenotype of long COVID patients that may reflect a dysregulation of 
77 
lipid metabolism and deficits in mitochondrial energy production as potential contributors to 
78 
symptom burden and brain structural alterations. These data may serve as a resource and basis 
79 
for further studies aimed at investigating peripheral molecular alterations in patients with 
80 
neuropsychiatric long COVID syndrome.

I. 
Introduction 
87 
Post-acute sequelae of infections with the SARS-CoV-2 virus (PASC) that persist for more than 
88 
4 weeks are subsumed under the term “long COVID syndrome” (1). Symptoms can manifest in 
89 
nearly all organs (2), however, neuropsychiatric symptoms such as fatigue, cognitive 
90 
impairment (e.g. concentrations and memory deficits) and depressed mood belong to the most 
91 
common symptoms that evoke severe mental suffering for affected individuals (3). According 
92 
to Iwasaki et al., long COVID syndrome is clinically almost indistinguishable from other so 
93 
called post-acute infection syndromes (PAIS) that occur after other viral infections such as after 
94 
influenza A virus and varicella zoster virus infections, which share fatigue, cognitive deficits 
95 
and depressed mood as leading symptoms (1, 4, 5). If clinical symptoms persist and the 
96 
Canadian consensus criteria are fulfilled (6), patients with long COVID syndrome can also be 
97 
diagnosed with myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/ CFS) as both 
98 
diseases may share a similar pathophysiological basis characterised by peripheral 
99 
endocrinological, metabolic and immunological changes, as reviewed elsewhere (7).  Patients 
100 
with ME/CFS systemically display an impairment in ATP production accompanied by a 
101 
“hypometabolic state” and an imbalance of pro-and antioxidant metabolites towards more pro-
102 
oxidant processes (8, 9). This hypometabolic state is characterized by reduced oxidation of fatty 
103 
acids via the TCA cycle (8, 9), which has already been observed already in long COVID patients 
104 
during exercise (10). Acylcarnitines have been identified as significant regulators of fatty acid 
105 
oxidation. These metabolites facilitate the import of acyl groups derived from fatty acids into 
106 
mitochondria for subsequent beta-oxidation and are important modulators of different CNS 
107 
processes (11). Short-chain acylcarnitines have been hypothesised to exert a neuroprotective 
108 
effect by supporting neuronal function, while medium- and long-chain acylcarnitines are 
109 
believed to be rather harmful (11). Interestingly, in ME/CFS patients a decrease in short-chain

133

II. 
Materials and Methods 
136 
Participants and cohort phenotyping 
137 
Patients were recruited from the post-COVID outpatient clinic of the Department of Internal 
138 
Medicine IV (Infectiology) and the Department of Neurology at Jena University Hospital. 
139 
There, they underwent verification of their post-COVID condition via real-time reverse 
140 
transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) at the time of acute infection, as well as a 
141 
medical history assessment conducted by a board-certified physician, covering the timepoint 
142 
and severity of their COVID-19 symptoms according to the WHO criteria. The long COVID 
143 
condition was defined according to the S1 guideline as the presence of persistent symptoms 4 
144 
to 12 weeks after the initial SARS-CoV-2 infection, while post-COVID syndrome was 
145 
classified as the persistence of symptoms beyond 12 weeks (18). In the cohort described in this 
146 
study, the average time since the initial SARS-CoV-2 infection was 10.5 months (range: 3.5 – 
147 
24.5 months). Inclusion criteria did not impose restrictions regarding specific symptoms or 
148 
recovery time to ensure coverage of the full spectrum of long-COVID symptomatology. None 
149 
of the participants met the criteria for any DSM-5 disorder at the time of assessment, as 
150 
determined through careful screening using the MINI Interview, conducted by a trained rater 
151 
according to DSM-5 criteria (19). Additionally, participants had no history of major 
152 
neurological or untreated medical conditions. Exclusion criteria for all participants included: 
153 
general MRI contraindications, neurological conditions, and untreated internal medical 
154 
conditions, particularly chronic inflammatory diseases, as well as a history of/or current 
155 
substance use disorder. All participants completed the Multiple-Choice Vocabulary Test B 
156 
(MWT-B), which is available in German (20), to estimate crystallized IQ and confirm the 
157 
inclusion criterion of an IQ above 80. All participants provided written informed consent before 
158 
participating in the study. The study protocol was approved by the local Ethics Committee of 
159 
Jena University Medical School. Between April 2021 and June 2022, we included 54 long-

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and calculation of cortical thickness  
190 
MRI data obtained in this study were published before (26). Subjects underwent high–
191 
resolution T1-weighted MRI on a 3 Tesla Siemens Prisma fit scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, 
192 
Germany) using a standard quadrature head coil and an axial 3-dimensional magnetization. 
193 
prepared rapid gradient echo (MP-RAGE) sequence (TR 2400 ms, TE 2.22 ms, α 8◦, 208 
194 
contiguous sagittal slices, FoV 256 mm, voxel resolution 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm; acquisition time 
195 
6:38 min). All subjects gave their consent for the native brain MRI scan after consultation of a 
196 
licensed radiologist. The scan was part of an MRI protocol of 60 min total duration. All scans 
197 
were checked to exclude imaging artefacts. Results reported in this manuscript derive from 
198 
preprocessing performed using fMRIPrep 23.2.1(27). FreeSurfer’s recon-all pipeline 
199 
(FreeSurfer 7.3.2)(28, 29), integrated within fMRIPrep, was used for cortical reconstruction 
200 
and segmentation. This pipeline generates cortical parcellations based on the Desikan-Killiany 
201 
atlas (commonly referred to as aparc or DK40)(30), typically partitioning the cortex into 68 
202 
regions across both hemispheres. Average cortical thickness values for each region were then 
203 
extracted using FreeSurfer’s aparcstats2table command, yielding robust morphometric metrics 
204 
for subsequent analysis. The “ggseg” R package (31) was used to visualise the regression 
205 
analysis estimates of cortical thickness across the DK40 region in relation to specific 
206 
metabolites.

Blood collection and preanalytics 
209 
Standardized blood collection was performed on all participants within a designated time frame 
210 
between 7:30 and 9:00 a.m. on the day of MRI scanning. Serum gel monovettes (7.5 mL, 
211 
Sarstedt) were used, and on average 30 minutes after collection (range 20-40 minutes), samples 
212 
were centrifuged at 800 g for 10 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was then divided into aliquots 
213 
of 0.25 mL each and stored at −80°C. Frozen samples were subsequently transferred to the 
214

Differential abundance, pathway enrichment analysis and data visualization 
264 
In order to identify differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs), we used the lme4 R package 
265 
(35) and therein linear mixed models that included age, sex and BMI and all interactions of 
266

275 
obtained, unadjusted, p-values of the top 15 enriched pathways were visualized using a bar plot.

Integration with neuropsychological/psychometric scores and cortical thickness values 
283 
and validation in the INCOV cohort 
284 
All neuropsychological/psychometric scores obtained as well as all cortical thickness values 
285 
determined as described above, were used to fit linear mixed models as described above, but 
286 
for each score two models were fitted, one with the interaction between the score and cohort 
287 
(e.g. "BDI_2*cohort") in addition to all covariates, and one without the scores. Likelihood ratio 
288 
tests were then performed to compare the two models for each score and to identify models 
289 
where the inclusion of the score improved the model fit. All obtained p-values (Pr(>Chisq)) 
290 
were further false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted and we used padj(fdr) ≤ 0.1 as a pragmatic 
291 
threshold for prioritizing candidate metabolite associations with the respective score/cortical 
292 
thickness of the respective region. Model summaries and estimates for each model variable 
293 
were obtained using the jtools R package (40). To validate our findings, we used the previously 
294 
published metabolomic data of PASC patients from the INCOV cohort (15), based on the 
295 
datasets provided in their publication (Table S2, sheet "S2.2 Metabolomics" and Table S1, sheet 
296 
"S1.3 PASC data"), as described in the Results section.

Statistical procedures 
298 
All analyses were performed using R 4.5.2 (41) or Graphpad Prism 8.4 (Figure S1). Shapiro-
299

Having established overarching metabolic differences between cohorts, we next performed a 
419

420

421

422

424

Validation of the longCOVID peripheral metabolite signature in an independent cohort 
650 
We wanted to determine whether our observations could be replicated in an independent cohort. 
651 
Therefore, we took the - to our knowledge - largest metabolomics dataset from Su et al. (15) 
652 
and examined alterations in the differentially altered metabolites identified in our study in long 
653 
COVID patients in their INCOV cohort of patients with PASC (≥ 1 PASC symptom according 
654 
to the clinical information from Table S1, sheet “S1.3 PASC data” (15)). Of all 56 metabolites 
655 
that were differentially regulated in our study, 13 metabolites were identified, that were also 
656 
measured in the Su et al. study. Interestingly, most of the metabolites (10 out of 13) that we 
657 
identified as being different in long COVID patients showed a similar pattern of regulation in 
658 
their study and, moreover, for 9 out of 13 in the same direction (i.e. up- or downregulation) as 
659 
in our cohort (Figure 5, threonine was not altered in the Su et al. dataset nor in the comparisons 
660 
of PC with HC_N or HC_S in our dataset). Different results were identified for hypoxanthine 
661 
(upregulation in Su et al. dataset, downregulation in the comparison PC vs. HC_N in our study), 
662 
lauric acid (downregulation in Su et al. dataset, upregulation in the comparison PC vs. HC_N 
663 
in our study) and serine (upregulation in Su et al. dataset, downregulation in the comparison PC 
664 
vs. HC_N in our study).

IV. 
Discussion 
677 
In this study, we used targeted metabolomics to identify biological factors that are altered in 
678 
long COVID patients and that may shed light on potential underlying pathophysiological 
679 
processes of PASC. The cohort we studied was well phenotyped and showed a mild depressive 
680 
phenotype, mild cognitive deficits and increased fatigue. We revealed subtle, but biologically 
681 
coherent alterations, with increases in tri-and diacylglycerides as compared to healthy survivors 
682 
and a decrease in several acylcarnitines, including acetylcarnitine. Circulating tri- and 
683 
diacylglycerides serve as an energy source for all tissues, as they are taken into cells after 
684 
hydrolysis by lipoprotein lipase (LPL) (42). An increase in the blood stream may thus reflect a 
685 
lower uptake into cells due to decreased LPL activity, a well-known mechanism in patients with 
686 
insulin resistance (43). In fact, insulin resistance and decreased LPL activity can be driven by 
687 
chronic inflammation through elevated circulating cytokines, such as in patients with obesity 
688 
by interferon-γ (44), which is one of the most important antiviral cytokines induced during viral 
689 
infections (45). IFN-γ has also been identified to be of high importance for the host response to 
690 
an acute Sars-CoV-2 infection (46) and has been identified to be not only persistently increased 
691 
also in long COVID patients, but also driving inflammatory serum signatures in a subset of long 
692 
COVID patients with especially fatigue as PASC symptom (47) and was upregulated in long 
693 
COVID patients with cognitive deficits (26). Interestingly, an upregulation of triacylglycerides 
694 
has also been observed in a large number of patients with ME/CFS in the UK Biobank, where 
695 
ME/CFS patients showed the highest TAG levels in total, followed by depressive patients and 
696 
patients with hypertension (48). In addition to the upregulation of TAGs in depressive patients, 
697 
obesity itself, i.e. a higher BMI, has previously been shown to be associated with a more chronic 
698 
course of the disease in MDD patients (49) and also with a reduction in temporo-frontal cortical 
699 
thickness (50). We also found in this study that levels of TAGs that were – after controlling for 
700 
BMI – upregulated in long COVID patients, were not only associated to primarily associated

718

719

720

723

724

760

761

762

763 
This study also has several limitations. First of all, an important limitation is the small sample 
764 
size, which may impair – given the potentially considerable heterogeneity of biological 
765 
subtypes of long COVID syndrome (47, 63, 64) – the ability to detect both, between-cohort 
766 
differences as well as associations to other biological variables. This is important to bear in 
767 
mind when interpreting our results, because we also found differences between our control 
768 
groups, i.e. between the healthy survivors and the healthy uninfected individuals, which need 
769 
to be investigated in further studies with larger cohorts. In addition, compared to the study by 
770 
Su et al. for example, our study cohort not only consisted of patients with PASC 2 to 3 months 
771 
after initial infection, but the patients also showed a greater heterogeneity in terms of duration 
772 
of PASC symptoms, which may not only influence the concentration of metabolites, which vary 
773 
up to 2 years after initial infection (65) but may also explain some of the different results of our 
774 
analyses compared to the Su et al. metabolome (15), though the majority of metabolites detected 
775 
in both studies showed similar profiles in the PASC/long COVID cohort (Figure 5). In this 
776 
study, we used a targeted metabolomics approach and analysed a pre-defined panel of 
777 
metabolites, which obviously introduces a strong selection bias, as a major part of this panel is 
778
