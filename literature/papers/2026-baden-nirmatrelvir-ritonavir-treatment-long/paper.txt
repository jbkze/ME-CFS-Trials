Summary
Background Post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection, more commonly known as long COVID, has emerged as a 
major health problem. The pathogenesis of long COVID is unknown, but among the leading hypotheses is viral 
persistence. We aimed to investigate whether the use of the SARS-CoV-2 antiviral nirmatrelvir–ritonavir improved 
long COVID symptoms.
Methods We conducted a double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomised trial involving adults who had developed 
persistent symptoms (≥12 weeks) associated with three major symptom phenotypes (cognitive, autonomic, or 
exercise) after acute SARS-CoV-2 infection at 69 US sites. Participants were eligible if they were 18 years or older and 
had a previous suspected, probable, or confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, as defined by the Pan American Health 
Organization. Eligible participants were also required to have either at least two moderate symptoms from the same 
phenotype or one severe phenotype-associated symptom, as identified with the Cluster Targeted COVID-19 Symptom 
Questions. Participants were randomly allocated in a double-blind manner in a 1:1:1 ratio using permuted blocks of 
size 30 to receive either 15 days of active intervention followed by 10 days of placebo (300 mg nirmatrelvir–100 mg 
ritonavir twice daily, then 100 mg ritonavir–placebo); 25 days of active intervention (300 mg nirmatrelvir–100 mg 
ritonavir twice daily); or 25 days of placebo–ritonavir (100 mg ritonavir–placebo). A clinically significant change in 
patient-reported outcomes at day 90 comprised the primary endpoint: Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement 
Information System Cognitive Function Short Form 8a, Orthostatic Hypotension Questionnaire question 1, and a 
modified version of the DePaul Symptom Questionnaire Post-Exertional Malaise short form. Secondary outcomes 
were phenotype-specific performance measures. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05595369) and is 
complete.
Findings Between July 27, 2023, and Sept 6, 2024, 1207 individuals were screened. Of these, 964 were randomly 
allocated and 959 participants, excluding four participants who were later found ineligible and one who did not 
initiate treatment, were enrolled in the three phenotypes: 332 to cognitive, 334 to autonomic, and 332 to exercise. In 
the 959 participants in the mITT population, 643 (67%) self-reported as female, 314 (33%) were male, and 
two participants had a sex of unknown or undifferentiated; 750 (78%) were White; and 108 (11%) were Hispanic, 
Latino, or Spanish. The median age was 49 years (IQR 38–59). No statistically significant benefits were observed for 
any phenotype for primary endpoints. For the cognitive phenotype, adjusted differences compared to placebo were 
3·2% (95% CI –10·4 to 16·8, p=0·65) for the 25-day regimen and –2·2% (–15·5 to 11·1, p=0·74) for the 15-day 
regimen. For the autonomic phenotype, adjusted differences were –6·4% (–18·5 to 5·7, p=0·30) for the 25-day 
regimen compared to placebo and –0·1% (–12·5 to 12·3, p=0∙99) for the 15-day regimen compared to placebo. For 
exercise, adjusted differences were –7·8% (–19·5 to 3·8, p=0·19) for the 25-day regimen compared to placebo and 
0·9% (–11·4 to 13·2, p=0∙88) for the 15-day regimen compared to placebo. There were no differences in secondary 
endpoints, and no safety signals were observed; there were no deaths, and 52 serious adverse events occurred in 
42 (4%) of 963 participants over the course of the study.

Introduction
Post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection, more 
commonly known as long COVID, is a chronic condition 
that affects nearly every organ system, with more than 
200 symptoms described, ranging from new-onset 
cognitive difficulties to dizziness and arrhythmias.1–5 
Over 5% of US American adults currently experience 
long COVID, with over 1% experiencing significant 
activity limitations due to long COVID; spontaneous 
remission is rare.6 The pathogenesis of long COVID is 
unknown, but several possibilities being investigated 
include 
viral 
persistence, 
immune 
dysregulation, 
endothelial damage, and thrombotic diathesis.7–11
The US National Institutes of Health (NIH) RECOVER 
(Researching COVID to Enhance Recovery) initiative has 
facilitated an improved but still evolving understanding 
of long COVID. Research results, including data from 
the RECOVER initiative, have highlighted substantial 
heterogeneity in long COVID symptomology and 
presentation, with scant concordance between clinical 
findings and reported symptoms. Three predominant 
phenotypes are frequently reported and are of substantial 
importance to patients: cognitive dysfunction, autonomic 
dysfunction manifesting as orthostatic intolerance, and 
exercise 
intolerance 
with 
post-exertional 
malaise 
(PEM).12–14 Given the importance of these symptom 
complexes for a broad array of illnesses beyond long 
COVID, tools to characterise these symptoms have been 
developed and validated.

compartmentalised replication is a leading aetiological 
hypothesis for long COVID based on detection of 
SARS-CoV-2 years after primary infection, virus detection 
in some patients with long COVID symptoms,15–17 reports

Research in context
Evidence before this study
Persistent symptoms post-SARS-CoV-2 infection (long COVID) 
have emerged as a major cause of morbidity globally. Multiple 
possible aetiologies of long COVID are being investigated, with 
viral persistence emerging as a leading possibility based on 
detection of SARS-CoV-2 years after primary infection, virus 
detection in some patients with long COVID symptoms, reports 
of long COVID prevention with antiviral therapy for acute 
COVID-19, and several anecdotal reports of antiviral therapy 
improving long COVID symptoms. We searched MEDLINE for 
studies reported in English from Jan 1, 2020, to June 1, 2026, 
using the terms “Long COVID”, “PASC”, “antiviral therapy”, 
“nirmatrelvir–ritonavir”, “randomized controlled trial”, AND 
“prolonged COVID”. We found 36 trials fitting our criteria. 
Two randomised trials (STOP-PASC and PAX-LC) using 15 days 
of nirmatrelvir–ritonavir did not show a benefit of treatment, 
but are not considered definitive, due to the small number of 
patients treated (151 randomised to nirmatrelvir–ritonavir), 
entry criteria that were broad, and the primary outcome 
assessed—a Likert scale on improvement across six organ 
symptoms (developed for STOP-PASC) or overall Patient-
Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System 29 
(PROMIS-29) Physical Health Summary Score (for PAX-LC).
Added value of this study
Between July 2023 and September 2024, 1207 individuals 
were screened for inclusion. Of these, 964 were randomised 
and 959 were enrolled with three major symptom 
phenotypes: 332 cognitive, 334 autonomic, and 332 exercise. 
Tools were used which focused on specific clinical phenotype 
dysfunction including both patient-reported outcome 
measures (PROMs) and objective performance measures. No 
significant improvement was observed by cluster, PROMs or 
performance measures, or by treatment duration. No safety 
concerns were identified. This trial is substantially larger than 
previous trials, and the added value of this trial over previous 
reports includes its treatment duration, double-blind design, 
reducing heterogeneity by looking at carefully defined 
phenotypes, and having phenotype-specific outcomes. These 
data further demonstrate the importance of a double-blind 
randomised controlled trial design given the significant 
undulation and improvement in long COVID symptoms in the 
research setting.
Implications of all the available evidence
Taken together, treatment of long COVID with nirmatrelvir–
ritonavir for up to 25 days did not provide any measurable 
clinical benefit. More research is needed to better define the 
pathogenesis of long COVID so treatments can be developed. 
It is important to assess repurposed medications in trials 
while pursuing an improved mechanistic understanding. 
These data add insights into how phenotype-specific 
measures behave over time in those with long COVID 
symptoms.

Given these observations and the logic that persistence of 
an acute infection might contribute to chronic symptoms, 
it is crucial to rigourously assess viral persistence as an 
aetiology for some of the symptomatology in long COVID 
and to determine whether antiviral therapy can improve 
long COVID symptoms.
We conducted a randomised, double-blind, placebo-
controlled study to investigate whether the use of the 
SARS-CoV-2 antiviral nirmatrelvir–ritonavir for 15 days 
or 25 days improved long COVID symptoms in 
three predominant clinical phenotypes. This phase 2 trial 
was designed—in conjunction with academic invest­
igators, the NIH, patient representatives, and the US 
Food and Drug Administration—to also develop 
endpoints to assess long COVID and define elements 
crucial to characterising potential response to therapy, 
including 
standardised 
patient-reported 
outcome 
measures (PROMs), performance measures, kinetics 
and durability of improvement, and variability of long 
COVID symptomatology over time.
Methods
Study design and participants
The study recruited from July 27, 2023, to Sept 6, 2024, at 
69 US sites across 27 states. Research sites were a mix of 
large academic centers, health systems, and small 
independent sites in rural, urban, and suburban 
outpatient settings, and all had or were associated with 
clinics that had significant experience in managing long 
COVID.
Participants were eligible if they were 18 years or older 
and had a previous suspected, probable, or confirmed 
SARS-CoV-2 infection, as defined by the Pan American 
Health Organization.23 Ascertainment of the index-
COVID illness was determined in discussion with the 
participant. We documented what type of test or 
confirmation was done, and then the records to back that 
up were obtained (eg, a laboratory result). If no records 
were available, then the site principal investigator 
provided a narrative explaining the patient-reported 
situation and symptoms that led to a suspected diagnosis. 
Eligible participants were also required to have either at 
least two moderate symptoms from the same phenotype 
or one severe phenotype-associated symptom, as 
identified with the Cluster Targeted COVID-19 Symptom 
Questions (CTCSQ). All symptoms had to be new since 
the index COVID-19 illness and persistent for at least 
12 weeks. After assignment to a phenotype based on the 
CTCSQ, participants were required to meet the 
phenotype-specific threshold on a PROM to qualify for 
enrolment (appendix p 10). Sex and ethnicity data were 
collected based on participant self-report.
Initially, participants could be enrolled in more than 
one phenotype; however, this approach proved overly 
burdensome for participants. In November 2023, the 
protocol was modified to assign each participant to the 
phenotype in which the most severe criteria were met. 
As phenotypes completed enrolment in 2024 (cognitive 
on June 26, exercise on Aug 9, and autonomic on Sept 6), 
participants were enrolled in remaining phenotypes as 
appropriate.
This study in the RECOVER Clinical Trial programme, 
VITAL (Viral Persistence and Reactivation, and Immune 
Dysregulation; NCT05595369), was investigator-initiated 
under US investigational new drug application and funded 
by the NIH. The protocol was approved by a central 
institutional review board (Advarra; approval Pro00067170; 
appendix pp 12–14), and all participants provided written 
informed consent. Safety oversight was under the direction 
of the NIH RECOVER Data Safety Monitoring Board. 
Details of study conduct can be found in the protocol and 
statistical analysis plan (appendix p 44).
Randomisation and masking
Participants were randomised in a 1:1:1 ratio using 
permuted blocks of size 30 through the central Almac 
randomisation system to receive either 15 days of 
active intervention followed by 10 days of placebo 
(300 mg nirmatrelvir–100 mg ritonavir twice daily, then 
100 mg ritonavir–placebo); 25 days of active intervention 
(300 mg nirmatrelvir–100 mg ritonavir twice daily); or 
25 days of placebo–ritonavir (100 mg ritonavir–placebo 
twice daily). Low-dose ritonavir was administered to the 
placebo group to maintain masking, as it has a known 
and unique taste. Dose reduction for renal dysfunction 
was done as appropriate.
All three groups received the same number of tablets 
per dose, with doses packaged in two kits (15 days 
followed by 10 days). Participants, investigators, site 
personnel, data management personnel, and the blinded 
statistical analysis team were masked to treatment 
assignment. A separate unmasked biostatistical team 
prepared closed interim reports for the data and safety 
monitoring board. Only the central pharmacy for the 
study and the unmasked biostatistical team had access to 
unmasked treatment assignment.
Procedures
Participants were followed up for 6 months, with the 
primary timepoint assessment conducted at an in-person 
clinical visit on day 90 (appendix p 11). Following 
completion of intervention, weekly remote follow-up was 
performed, and optional in-person visits for blood 
collection occurred on days 45, 60, and 120. The final 
study visit was a remote assessment on day 180.
Adverse events, serious adverse events, and events of 
special interest, including specific abnormalities in liver 
and thyroid function, were followed up through to day 
180. Blood specimens to assess events of special interest 
were obtained at baseline, dosing midpoint (day 15), end 
of dosing (day 25), and day 90. Grading of adverse events 
was by the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse 
Events version 5.0, Nov 27, 2017.

Outcomes
The primary endpoint for each phenotype was the 
proportion of participants achieving a clinically meaningful 
improvement as assessed by phenotype-specific PROM 
at day 90 compared to baseline. Clinically significant 
improvement for each phenotype was supported by 
literature in non-long COVID populations24–27 and defined 
as follows: for cognitive, an increase by at least 5 T-score 
points on the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement 
Information System (PROMIS) Cognitive Function Short 
Form 8a; for autonomic, a decrease by at least one rating 
scale category on a modified version of the Orthostatic 
Hypotension Questionnaire (OHQ) question 1; and for 
exercise, absence of any moderate, severe, or very severe 
symptoms occurring 50% or more of the time as assessed 
by a modified version of the DePaul Symptom 
Questionnaire Post-Exertional Malaise (DSQ-PEM) short 
form. To account for within-person variability in cognitive 
and autonomic symptoms over time, an average of 
three weekly assessments closest to day 90 was used for 
these measures.
Secondary endpoints were based on the proportion of 
participants obtaining clinically meaningful improve­
ment on phenotype-specific performance measures, 
which was assessed at day 90 compared to baseline and 
defined as follows: for cognitive, an increase by 0·5 SD in 
either the WHO–University of California Los Angeles 
Auditory Verbal Learning Test delayed recall measure or 
Symbol Digit Modalities Test number of correct 
substitution measure, and no decrease exceeding 
0·15 SD in either of these measures; for autonomic, 
either a decrease in heart rate by at least 10 beats 
per minute or an increase in systolic blood pressure by at 
least 10 mm Hg, or an increase in diastolic blood pressure 
by at least 5 mm Hg, and no increase in heart rate or 
decrease in systolic or diastolic blood pressure compared 
to baseline on the active stand test at 10 min; and for 
exercise, an increase by at least 3 min in walking time on 
the Endurance Shuttle Walk Test.
Statistical analysis
Sample size was chosen to provide 80% power to detect a 
difference of 25 percentage points in the proportion of 
participants who met the binary primary outcome for each 
pairwise comparison between a treatment group and 
control within each phenotype, assuming a 2-sided test 
with α=0·0166. A sample size of 82 per group yields 80% 
power assuming the true outcome probabilities are 37·5% 
in the control group and 62·5% in the active treatment 
group. This sample size was increased by 1/0·82=1·22 to 
account for a potential 18% dropout and loss to follow-up 
rate.
The primary analysis was based on a separate modified 
intention-to-treat (mITT) population defined for each 
phenotype. Each phenotype-specific mITT population 
consisted of all randomly allocated participants who 
received at least part of one dose of the study intervention 
and were eligible, enrolled, and randomised into that 
phenotype. Participants were analysed according to their 
randomly assigned intervention group. Participants who 
were enrolled into more than one phenotype, as allowed 
under Protocol Version 3.0, were included in the mITT 
population of each phenotype for which inclusion criteria 
were met. The overall mITT population consisted of 
participants included in at least one phenotype-specific 
mITT population.
The primary analysis was repeated in the population 
with high study drug adherence, consisting of all mITT 
participants who missed no more than three planned 
doses during their entire drug dosing period. Safety 
analyses were based on a population of all randomly 
allocated participants who received at least part of 
one dose of the study intervention or control, irrespective 
of eligibility for efficacy analysis. Treatment adherence 
was assessed by participant self-report and pill counts.
Treatment effects were estimated using logistic 
regression with direct standardisation (g-computation) 
and reported as the unadjusted and adjusted difference in 
the percentages improving between each intervention 
group and the control group. To enhance precision and 
account for chance imbalances across treatment groups, 
the primary analysis was adjusted for prespecified baseline 
covariates, including region (based on US census 
divisions), race, years of education, and the baseline value 
of the phenotype-specific PROM. The missing-data 
approach assumed that outcomes were missing at random, 
conditional on the model covariates. Participants with 
missing primary endpoint data did not contribute to model 
fitting but were retained when predicted outcomes were 
averaged over the phenotype-specific mITT population. To 
account for partial knowledge to inform endpoint 
definitions and timing, outcome measures were also 
assessed in a continuous manner and at several different 
time points: dosing midpoint, end of dosing, and day 180.
A Bonferroni–Holm procedure was used to control the 
family-wise type I error at a two-sided α of 0·05 across the 
three phenotype-specific primary outcomes. Within each 
phenotype, comparisons of the two active dose regimens 
with placebo–ritonavir were performed sequentially, with 
the 25-day regimen tested first, followed by the 15-day 
regimen if the preceding comparison was significant, 
according to the prespecified multiplicity strategy. No 
adjustments were made for other analyses, including 
secondary endpoints, continuous versions of the primary 
endpoints, subgroup analyses, or exploratory outcomes. 
Results are reported as point estimates and 95% CIs. 
CI widths were not adjusted for multiple comparisons 
and should not be used in place of hypothesis testing. 
Additional details are provided in the statistical analysis 
plan (appendix p 44).

Results
Between July 27, 2023, and Sept 6, 2024, 1207 individuals 
were screened. Of these, 964 were randomly allocated 
and 959 participants, excluding four participants who 
were later found ineligible and one who did not initiate 
treatment, were enrolled in the three phenotypes: 332 to 
cognitive, 334 to autonomic, and 332 to exercise 
(figure 1). In the 959 participants in the mITT 
population, 643 (67%) self-reported as female, 314 (33%) 
were male, and two participants had a sex of unknown or 
undifferentiated; 750 (78%) were White; and 108 (11%) 
were Hispanic, Latino, or Spanish. The median age was 
49 years (IQR 38–59).  Similar demographic distributions 
were seen in the three phenotypes and across randomly 
allocated treatment groups (table 1). COVID infections 
were confirmed in 768 (80%) incidences, 96 (10%) were 
probable, and 45 (5%) were suspected cases of COVID.

Discussion
The results of this randomised, double-blind, placebo-
controlled trial showed no evidence that up to 25 days of 
the antiviral intervention nirmatrelvir–ritonavir provided 
clinical benefit by PROMs or performance measures for 
participants living with long COVID with cognitive 
dysfunction, 
autonomic 
dysfunction, 
or 
exercise 
intolerance as dominant symptoms. Benefit was not seen 
at any assessment timepoints or in either the 15-day or 
25-day course of therapy. These findings are consistent 
with data from smaller studies assessing the role of 15 days 
of nirmatrelvir–ritonavir for treatment of long COVID in 
all patients reporting long COVID without careful clinical 
phenotype assessment and consideration.28,29
Previous trials of nirmatrelvir–ritonavir in long 
COVID and retrospective assessments of these trials 
have attributed negative findings to potential limitations 
in design elements (eg, small sample size, disease 
heterogeneity, and non-specific outcome measures or 
insensitive endpoints) as well as incongruity between 
the therapeutic agent and long COVID biology 
(eg, inaccessible viral reservoirs, non-replicating virus, 
inadequate 
duration, 
and 
alternative 
aetiology). 
RECOVER-VITAL sought to overcome these limitations 
with focus on sufficient sample sizes for each of 
three major phenotypes, use of previously validated 
assessment tools, phenotype-specific assessment with 
both PROMs and performance measures, assessment 
at multiple timepoints, binary and continuous 
endpoints to minimise the risk of missing an effect, 
and two dosing durations to investigate a potential

Nevertheless, 
the

pathophysiology of the symptom complex and the 
mechanism of the treatment and healing, remains 
unknown and could have contributed to negative 
findings. Phenotypes such as PEM are relapsing and 
remitting, which could further complicate endpoint 
assessment, and repeated assess­ment might have 
resulted in regression to the mean. Moreover, due to 
feasibility, RECOVER-VITAL did not restrict enrolment 
to those with evidence of persistent virus who are 
potentially most likely to benefit. The study also 
enrolled those with previous SARS-CoV-2 vaccination 
and prolonged long COVID duration, who might have a 
lower probability of improvement.
An important set of observations from this study is the 
behaviour of phenotype-specific outcome measures in the 
placebo–ritonavir group. Across all three phenotypes, 
substantial percentages of participants showed improve­
ment over time by PROMs and less so by performance 
measures. Although the Hawthorne effect is commonly 
seen in clinical trials, perhaps due to attention and care 
during the course of a trial, the proportion improving in 
RECOVER-VITAL exceeded assumptions used for initial 
sample size calculations, perhaps limiting the possibility 
of detecting effect with the final sample size. Importantly, 
this background rate of improvement is not believed to be 
due to low-dose ritonavir administered to each group to 
maintain masking; previous studies suggested no 
meaningful biological activity of ritonavir against SARS-
CoV-2, especially when used at a low dose.30 Nonetheless, 
the high proportion improving in the placebo–ritonavir 
group cautions against overinterpretation of uncontrolled 
observations and highlights the need for placebo control in 
studies of long COVID to better define therapeutic 
improvement rather than variation of symptomatology 
over time.
Nirmatrelvir–ritonavir has been used extensively to 
treat acute COVID, typically for 5 days. Extending the 
course to 25 days did not reveal any new safety concerns. 
A slight increase in transient mild nausea and diarrhoea 
was observed compared to ritonavir alone. Overall, 
treatment was well tolerated with few significant adverse 
events or discontinuations.

has limitations, including difficulties establishing the

other two groups where this was not a consideration), 
lack of clarity around whether long COVID due to viral 
persistence would require a longer course of therapy, the 
fact that the proportion of individuals with viral 
persistence is unknown, and the fact that the duration of 
symptoms before enrolment is long, so those with severe 
symptoms might be beyond the point where antivirals 
can have an effect. In addition, as long COVID probably 
has more than one pathogenesis driving symptomatology, 
the question of whether nirmatrelvir–ritonavir leads to 
symptom improvement and associated viral clearance 
among those with evidence of viral persistence at 
baseline remains unanswered. Although RECOVER-
VITAL obtained necessary blood samples to pursue these 
evaluations, both in terms of evidence for persistent 
virus at baseline and if viral antigen cleared with 
treatment, analyses are pending and will be published. 
Participants with persistent virus and with changes in 
viral antigen levels with treatment could provide crucial 
insights into long COVID pathogenesis and viral 
kinetics, and whether there are subgroups of patients for 
whom an antiviral could provide benefits.
In conclusion, antiviral treatment with nirmatrelvir–
ritonavir in patients with long COVID for up to 25 days 
did not improve cognitive, autonomic, or exercise 
symptomatology as measured by symptom-specific 
PROMs and performance measures. Future directions to 
test this mechanistic hypothesis that long COVID 
symptoms relate to viral persistence include biomarker 
screening for measures of viral persistence at baseline, 
and possibly longer durations or combinations of 
antiviral or other therapies.
